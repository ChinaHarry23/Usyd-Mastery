Foundations: the vocabulary and the dials behind intelligent interfaces

Week 8 is a design week rather than a mathematical one, so this page does the equivalent job: it pins down the definitions the lecture relies on, gives you a compact notation for inference, confidence and autonomy, turns the levels of automation and the latency budget into relations you can write down, reduces Grice's maxims to a failure taxonomy, and ends with the checklist that turns “the AI was wrong” into a markable answer.

Definitions that must be exact

Marks in this unit are lost on definitions more often than on ideas. Write each of these in one sentence, with the consequence for interaction included.

AI system — EU AI regulation, current wording

A machine-based system designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy, which may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and which, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments.

AI system — EU AI regulation proposal (the earlier, technique-based wording)

Software developed with one or more of the techniques and approaches listed in Annex I that can, for a given set of human-defined objectives, generate outputs such as content, predictions, recommendations or decisions influencing the environments they interact with. Annex I covers machine-learning approaches (supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement, including deep learning); logic- and knowledge-based approaches (knowledge representation, inductive logic programming, knowledge bases, inference and deductive engines, symbolic reasoning, expert systems); statistical approaches and Bayesian estimation; and search and optimisation methods.

Narrow and general AI

Narrow AI performs one or a few tasks really well and exists today. General AI would perform any kind of task extremely well and is not yet realised. Everything discussed in this unit is narrow, however capable it appears.

Automation versus augmentation

Automation moves a decision from the person to the system. Augmentation keeps the person as the decision-maker and uses the system to extend what they can do. The deck's claim is that the potential of AI lies not in automation but in augmentation.

Over-trust and automation bias

Over-trust is accepting a system's output as correct without checking it. Automation bias is the specific failure of favouring an automated suggestion over other information — including the user's own judgement. Both are made worse by confident-sounding output from an uncertain model.

Seven relationships worth writing from memory

Each of these turns a design instinct into something you can state, and therefore something an examiner can mark.

1. An AI system is an inference, not a specification

The system does not execute a rule you wrote; it estimates an output from an input:

\[ \hat{y} \;=\; f(x), \qquad \hat{y} \;\approx\; y^{*} \text{ only in expectation} \]

Consequence for interaction: because \(f\) is probabilistic, the interface must communicate capability and error rate before the output is trusted. The first two human-AI guidelines are exactly this consequence.

2. Confidence decides the response tier

With \(c\) the system's confidence in its own interpretation, and two thresholds \(\tau_c < \tau_a\):

\[ \text{response}(c) \;=\; \begin{cases} \text{ask again}, & c < \tau_c \\[2pt] \text{confirm, then act}, & \tau_c \le c < \tau_a \\[2pt] \text{act silently}, & c \ge \tau_a \end{cases} \]

This is the deck's three-tiered confidence, written as a policy. The engineering problem is estimating \(c\) honestly; the design problem is setting the thresholds per action rather than globally, because the cost of a wrong irreversible action is not the same as the cost of a wrong song choice.

3. The level of automation is a function of error cost, not of model quality

Let \(\ell \in \{1,\dots,10\}\) be the Sheridan and Verplank level, \(u\) the urgency of the decision, \(r\) its reversibility, \(E_a\) the attention the user can spare, and \(P(\text{err})\) the probability the inference is wrong:

\[ \ell^{*} \;=\; g\bigl(u,\; r,\; E_a,\; P(\text{err})\bigr) \]

Read the signs: higher urgency and lower attention budget push \(\ell\) up; lower reversibility and higher error probability push \(\ell\) down. Note what is absent — the model's benchmark score. A better model does not by itself justify more autonomy; it only shifts \(P(\text{err})\), which is one input among four.

4. Augmentation rather than automation

Let \(H\) be what the human can achieve alone and \(S\) what the system can achieve alone. Automation replaces \(H\) with \(S\); augmentation is bounded by the human:

\[ K \;=\; H \cdot (1 + S) \qquad\text{(augmentation)} \qquad\text{versus}\qquad K' \;=\; S \qquad\text{(automation)} \]

The product form carries the argument: if the human's contribution goes to zero, augmentation collapses to \(S\) and you have built automation with extra steps. This is why the deck's claim is a design stance rather than a slogan — it only pays off where \(H\) is genuinely non-zero.

5. The voice latency budget is a sum, and the sum is the experience

A voice interaction is a chain of stages, so the delays add rather than hide:

\[ L \;=\; L_{\text{wake}} + L_{\text{asr}} + L_{\text{nlu}} + L_{\text{dialogue}} + L_{\text{skill}} + L_{\text{tts}} \]

The practical reading: you cannot buy back a slow stage by making another one faster than zero. Turning a 1 200 ms round trip into a 1 500 ms one is the difference between a conversation and a transaction, and no amount of model accuracy repairs the feel of it.

6. Language has four levels, and each level has its own error rate

The deck's progression — words, syntax, semantics, pragmatics — composes into a success probability:

\[ P(\text{understood}) \;=\; P(\text{words}) \cdot P(\text{syntax} \mid \text{words}) \cdot P(\text{semantics} \mid \text{syntax}) \cdot P(\text{pragmatics} \mid \text{semantics}) \]

Because the terms multiply, a stage at 0.9 caps the whole chain: four stages at 0.9 give 0.66. This is the formal reason the deck's repair strategies exist, and the reason a correct transcript is not the same as a correct understanding.

7. A detector has two error directions, and the threshold trades one for the other

For a wake word or any other detector, let \(\mathrm{FA}\) be a false accept (it reacts when nobody spoke to it) and \(\mathrm{FR}\) a false reject (it ignores a real request):

\[ \text{raise the threshold} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{FA} \downarrow,\; \mathrm{FR} \uparrow \]

Both directions have a cost and they are not symmetric. A false accept is a privacy event; a false reject is a repetition, and after two or three of them the user stops talking to the device. Choose the threshold by naming which cost you would rather pay.

The sentence that connects the whole page. An intelligent interface makes the design problem harder rather than easier: behaviour that used to be specified is now inferred, so the designer's job becomes choosing \(\ell\), setting \(\tau_c\) and \(\tau_a\), budgeting \(L\), and designing the repair path for the case where \(f(x)\) is wrong — knowing all the while that the user will trust \(\hat{y}\) more than the evidence warrants.

Self-check and worked answers

Three interactive checks and three worked answers. Answer out loud before you flip, and write the worked answers out in full at least once.

A wake word detector is tuned so that it almost never reacts to speech that was not addressed to it. What have you paid for that?
More privacy events, because the device wakes up more often
More false rejects: genuine requests are ignored, so the user repeats themselves and eventually stops trying
Nothing: false accepts and false rejects are independent of the threshold

Worked answer — a latency budget. A voice assistant has a round-trip budget of 1 000 ms. The current pipeline measures: wake word 250 ms, speech recognition 320 ms, natural-language understanding 180 ms, dialogue management 90 ms, skill or API call 400 ms, text-to-speech 160 ms.

1

Sum the stages. \(L = 250 + 320 + 180 + 90 + 400 + 160 = 1\,400\) ms — 400 ms over budget.

2

Note that the sum, not the worst stage, is the experience. Five stages at 180 ms plus one at 500 ms is still 1 400 ms. Shaving a fast stage buys almost nothing; the budget is decided by the sum.

3

Find the stage that can be overlapped or removed. The skill call at 400 ms is the only stage that leaves the device, and it is also the only one that can start speculatively: issue the call from the partial transcript, and cancel it if the final transcript disagrees. That removes most of the 400 ms without touching the model.

4

Fix the remainder at the interface. If the budget still cannot be met, acknowledge immediately — a short filler — so that perceived latency stops tracking measured latency. This is a design intervention in a latency problem, which is the point: \(L\) is a property of the interaction, not only of the stack.

5

State the caveat. The numbers here are illustrative; the method is the markable part. Sum the chain, find the removable or overlappable stage, and say what the user perceives while you wait.

An essay-grading model reports that it agrees with human markers 85 % of the time. Which conclusion is best supported?
It is an automating-judgement task: some error is inevitable, so the design must handle the 15 % of cases where the grade is wrong
The model is 85 % accurate, so it can be deployed with confidence
It is a social-outcome prediction, so it is fundamentally dubious and must not be deployed

Worked answer — placing a proactive feature on the ladder. Feature: a calendar assistant that notices a double booking, proposes a fix, and — if you do not respond in ten minutes — moves the less important meeting and tells you afterwards.

1

Separate the two decisions. Detecting the clash and proposing a fix is one behaviour; executing the fix is another. A single feature can sit at two levels at once, and saying so is worth marks.

2

Place the proposal. Offering one suggested alternative is level 4. If it offers several options it is level 2 or 3; if it offers one and waits for approval before acting, the pair is best described as level 4 followed by level 5.

3

Place the execution. Acting after a timeout without a response is level 6: the human had a period in which to veto, and the system proceeded automatically when the period expired. It is not level 7, because you are informed before the fact by the ten-minute window; it would become a notification-only design, level 7, if it moved the meeting immediately and told you afterwards.

4

Justify with the four inputs. Reversibility \(r\) is high for a meeting that can be moved back, urgency \(u\) is moderate, the attention budget \(E_a\) is the whole reason for the timeout, and \(P(\text{err})\) is the risk that the model misjudges which meeting matters more. High \(r\) supports the automatic execution; the error probability is what makes the notification and the undo action mandatory rather than optional.

5

Name the guideline obligations. Make clear what the system can do and how often it is wrong (initially); make correction efficient and explain the action (during interaction); convey the consequences of your non-response and provide a global control over what it may move (over time). A proactive feature that skips these is a level-6 system without the level-6 safeguards.

Worked answer — diagnosing a miscommunication with the maxims and the language levels. Transcript: “Ok, Google, what was the last song?” answered with a plot summary of the 2010 film The Last Song.

1

Locate the level that failed. The words were recognised and the syntax parsed, so the failure is at semantics: “the last song” was resolved as a title rather than as a reference to recent history.

2

Name the maxim. Relation. The answer is true and well-formed but not relevant to what the speaker meant — the classic failure of answering the words rather than the intent.

3

Name the guideline that would have prevented it. Remember recent interactions: with short-term memory of what was played, “the last song” is resolvable without any improvement to the language model.

4

Check the confidence policy. Two parses existed, so \(c\) should have been below \(\tau_a\). The correct response was the middle tier: confirm which one was meant, rather than acting on the more literal interpretation.

5

Predict the user's repair. Faced with a film summary, the user will reformulate: “what was the last song I played” or “what was playing before this”. That is the third repair pattern — a new utterance — and it is a semantic repair, which is exactly where the defect is.
