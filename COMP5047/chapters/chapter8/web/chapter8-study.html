Are We Cyborgs? Inference in Every Object

The week opens with a definition and an uncomfortable question. The definition is easy; the question is the one worth answering in an exam.

Cyborg: a person whose physiological functioning is aided by or dependent upon a mechanical or electronic device. The lecture's follow-up is not “is that you?” but “who is controlling this interaction?”

The lecture's questions

  • Do we control our phones, or do our phones control us?
  • If you are in control of someone's phone, would you be able to control them?
  • What can be inferred about you by looking at your purchase history?
  • We can turn our augmentation off — but will we?

Why pervasive computing makes this sharp

In an IoT world, AI is part of every object. Once inference is embedded in the objects you already live with, the interface stops being the only thing that shapes your behaviour — the model's decisions do too.

Two lines from the deck to remember verbatim: “We shape our buildings; thereafter they shape us” becomes “We shape our digital infrastructure; thereafter it shapes us.” The history framing that follows is Stone Age → Bronze Age → Iron Age → AI Age?

Defining AI: Regulation, Not Hype

“But what is AI?” The deck answers with legal definitions rather than marketing ones, because a definition you can quote is a definition you can design against.

EU AI regulation proposal

Software developed with one or more of the techniques in Annex I of the regulation that can, for a given set of human-defined objectives, generate outputs such as content, predictions, recommendations or decisions influencing the environments they interact with. Annex I lists machine-learning approaches (supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning, including deep learning); logic- and knowledge-based approaches (knowledge representation, inductive logic programming, knowledge bases, inference and deductive engines, symbolic reasoning, expert systems); statistical approaches and Bayesian estimation; and search and optimisation methods.

EU AI regulation (current wording)

A machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments.

Phrase in the current definitionWhy it matters for interaction design
Varying levels of autonomyAutonomy is a dial, not a switch — which is exactly what the levels of automation make explicit
Adaptiveness after deploymentThe system changes after you ship it, so behaviour is not fully specified by the designer
Explicit or implicit objectivesOptimising a proxy can satisfy the letter of the objective and miss the intent
Infers, from the input it receivesOutputs are probabilistic, so the interface must communicate uncertainty
Can influence physical or virtual environmentsAn embedded agent can act on the world, so errors have consequences
Exam framing: the two definitions are not interchangeable. The proposal's definition is technique-based (if it uses these methods, it is AI); the current one is behaviour-based (if it infers, adapts and acts with some autonomy, it is AI). Quote the one that matches the question you are answering.
Which feature of the current EU AI regulation definition is doing the most work for interaction designers?
It lists machine-learning techniques, deep learning in particular
It treats autonomy as varying and allows adaptiveness after deployment
It requires symbolic reasoning rather than statistical methods

Kinds of AI and the Imitation Game

Two axes are used in the deck: how many tasks a system can do, and whether it is judged by acting like a human or by thinking rationally.

Narrow

  • Performs one or a few tasks really well
  • Exists today
  • Everything in this unit is narrow AI, however impressive it feels

General

  • Performs any kind of task extremely well
  • Not yet realised
  • The Turing Test is one attempt to operationalise the question
The imitation game — what a computer would need

To pass, a machine needs four capabilities the deck names explicitly: natural language processing (to communicate successfully with humans), knowledge representation (to store what it knows and learns), automated reasoning (to answer questions and draw conclusions) and machine learning (to adapt to new circumstances and extrapolate patterns).

Levels of Automation: Sheridan and Verplank

The deck's central design instrument for this week. The potential of AI lies not in automation but in augmentation — and this scale is how you say how much of each you have built.

Reading the scale: the two ends pull in opposite directions. Moving up buys speed and consistency; moving down buys oversight and recoverability. The deck's “augmentation, not automation” claim is an argument for staying low on the scale wherever the human can actually contribute judgement.
BandLevelsWho decidesInteraction costFailure mode to design for
Low1–3The human, informed by the systemHigh: the user must read and chooseThe system's framing quietly steers the choice
Middle4–6Shared; the human retains a vetoMedium: one confirmation or one countdownConfirmation fatigue turns the veto into a click-through
High7–10The system, with notification somewhere on the dialLow to noneAutomation bias, unrecoverable errors, no explanation

Worked example — placing a system on the ladder and defending the placement. Scenario: a continuous glucose monitor paired with an insulin pump for a person with type 1 diabetes.

1

Name the decision being automated. Not “diabetes care” but a specific act: how many units of insulin to deliver in the next five minutes. Levels of automation only make sense per decision.

2

Find the current level. Read the numbers off the scale. If the pump suggests a dose and waits, that is level 4. If it announces a dose it has already given, that is level 7. If it adjusts basal rates continuously, notifies and allows the user to suspend it, that is level 6–7 rather than 10.

3

Ask what the human can contribute. The person knows about exercise, illness and meals that no sensor sees. That knowledge is the argument for a veto — so the defensible level is one where a veto exists, not the highest level the hardware allows.

4

Name the cost of the two error directions. Too much insulin is an acute emergency; too little is slow harm. Asymmetric costs are the standard reason to keep a human in the loop and to make the system's reasoning visible.

5

Write the justified sentence. “The system sits at level 6–7: it acts automatically but must inform the user and permit a veto within a stated window, because the harm from over-delivery is acute, the model cannot observe meals or exercise, and the user holds information the system does not.”

A scheduling agent reads your calendar, books a meeting room, moves the meeting, and sends you a notification afterwards. Where does it sit, and what is the main design risk?
Level 5 — it asks the human to approve first
Level 7 — it executes automatically then informs, so the risk is an action that is hard to undo
Level 10 — it acts autonomously and ignores the human
The deck claims that “the potential of AI lies not in automation but in augmentation”. Which design is the clearest instance of augmentation?
A system that highlights the three most likely diagnoses for a radiologist to confirm or reject
A system that issues the diagnosis and files the report without review
A system that replaces the radiologist with a model trained on more scans than any human has read

What Actually Works: Perception, Judgement, Social Outcomes

The deck's test for snake oil is a three-way split. The category a claim falls into tells you what kind of accuracy to expect — and therefore what kind of ethical problem you are about to create.

CategoryVerdict in the deckExamplesWhy the ethics bite
Automating perceptionGenuine and rapid progressContent identification (Google Lens, Shazam, reverse image search); face recognition and analysis (identity, action-unit detection); medical diagnoses from scans such as X-ray analysis; speech-to-text; deepfakesHigh accuracy. Because it works, it gets deployed at scale — which is precisely when surveillance, consent and deepfake harms scale too
Automating judgementFar from perfect, but improvingSpam detection; detection of copyrighted material and plagiarism; automated essay grading; hate speech detection; content recommendationSome error is inevitable. A judgement call with a known error rate is being made automatically about people, at volume
Predicting social outcomesFundamentally dubiousPredicting criminal recidivism; predicting job performance; predictive policing; predicting terrorist risk; predicting at-risk kidsLow accuracy, high stakes. The ethical concerns are not mitigated by better engineering but amplified by the pretence of measurement
The pattern worth memorising: accuracy and ethical risk do not travel together. High-accuracy perception creates risk through scale; low-accuracy social prediction creates risk through consequence. Only the middle category has the familiar shape of “the model is sometimes wrong”.

Voice Interaction: Why, Why Not, and How It Runs

Voice is the week's flagship intelligent interface, and the deck gives it a balanced treatment: four arguments for it, four against it, and a pipeline that explains why failures are usually structural rather than accidental.

Why voice user interfaces

  • Speed — faster than typing
  • Hands-free — useful when hands are busy or otherwise unavailable
  • Intuitiveness — talking is a natural way of interacting with others
  • Empathy — allows for nonverbal content, such as prosody

Why not voice user interfaces

  • Public spaces — risk of interference and annoying others
  • Discomfort — speaking to others is natural, but not to a computer
  • Preference — some users simply prefer to type
  • Privacy — users do not want to broadcast sensitive content

Worked example — tracing one utterance through the pipeline. Utterance, from the deck: “Alexa, play the Beatles on Spotify”, followed by a failure and a repair.

1

Capture. The wake word fires and the device records. If the wake word misfires in a living room, the failure is already at stage one — nothing downstream can fix it.

2

Speech to text. The recogniser produces a transcript. Every later stage sees only this text, so an acoustic error here is indistinguishable from the user saying something odd.

3

Natural language understanding. Words → syntax → semantics → pragmatics. The deck's example: “Play Yesterday” resolves into book, film, song or artist — and the system also has to decide whether “studio or live?” matters. Ambiguity is a property of language, not a bug in the parser.

4

Skill or API call. The request leaves the device. At this point the system either has the capability or it does not: the deck's Google example answers “I can't open apps”, which is an honest capability boundary rather than a comprehension failure.

5

Dialogue management. Confidence decides the response tier: act (“OK, ordering more dog food”), confirm (“I understand you'd like to order more dog food. Is that correct?”) or ask again (“I'm sorry, I didn't get that. What would you like to buy?”).

6

Speech synthesis, then repair. The user hears the answer, and if it is wrong they change their own behaviour: the deck's four documented strategies are hyperarticulation, simplification, a new utterance, or more information.

Conversation Design: Grice, Maxims and Miscommunication

Conversation design sits where psychology, linguistics, audio design, speech synthesis and NLP meet. The deck's most quotable content this week is what happens when a voice assistant breaks the rules of ordinary talk.

Grice's cooperative principle (Paul Grice, 1913–1988)

Participants expect that each will make a conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange. In other words: both parties assume the other is trying to be understood.

Three-tiered confidence

The deck's concrete policy for uncertainty:

  • Confident — act: “OK, ordering more dog food”
  • Uncertain — confirm: “I understand you'd like to order more dog food. Is that correct?”
  • Lost — ask again: “I'm sorry, I didn't get that. What would you like to buy?”

Silent action on low confidence is the failure this policy exists to prevent.

Language levels, and where they break

  • Words — the acoustic model has to hear them
  • Syntax — the grammar can be ambiguous: the deck's “I shot an elephant in my pyjamas” has two parses
  • Semantics — “Play Yesterday” could be a song, an album, a film or a book
  • Pragmatics — what the speaker actually wanted, which is never in the transcript
Two voice milestones the deck highlights: real-time translation and conversational interfaces. Both raise the same design question as the maxims — who is responsible when the machine's rendering of your meaning is wrong? The deck's answer for repair is the next section.
You ask a voice assistant for the next flight to Melbourne and it reads out every Monday flight, one after another. Which maxim has it broken?
Quality — the contribution was not true
Quantity — the contribution was not as informative as required, and no more
Relation — the contribution was not relevant

When It Fails: Whose Fault Is It?

The deck puts two reactions side by side: “That's my fault… silly fat thumbs…” and “I said what I said and you should have understood.” Which one a person adopts determines whether they repair the interaction or abandon the system.

Self-blame

“That's my fault — silly fat thumbs.” The user treats the failure as their own error. This is cheap for the system in the short term and expensive in the long term: it hides a design defect and trains people to distrust their own accurate input.

System-blame

“I said what I said and you should have understood.” The user holds the assistant accountable for a cooperative principle it failed to honour. This is the reaction that produces improvement — if the transcript ever reaches the designer.

Why this matters for the week's theme: an intelligent interface that fails silently transfers the cost of its errors onto the person, who then has to work out what the machine wanted. Designing the failure — making the system's interpretation visible and its limits stated — is what the human-AI guidelines formalise.
The failure you observeWhere it most likely originatesWhat the designer can actually change
The device woke up when nobody spoke to itWake word detectionThreshold, and an honest indication of when it is listening
The transcript is wrongAcoustic model and microphone placementArray geometry, partial-transcript display, confirmation for irreversible actions
The words are right but the intent is wrongNLU and ambiguity in the utterance itselfAsk a disambiguating question instead of guessing
The intent is right but nothing happensCapability boundary — there is no skill or API for itState the boundary, then offer the nearest thing you can do
It works, but the user stops using itCost of repair: too many confirmations, or repeated failure in publicReduce the confirmation burden and make the third attempt different from the first two
A user says “silly fat thumbs” after a misrecognition. Why is that reaction a design problem rather than a user problem?
Because users should be more careful when speaking to a system
Because self-blame hides the defect: the error is absorbed by the user instead of being surfaced and fed back to the design
Because users should always be polite to artificial agents

Repair Strategies: What People Do When It Fails

The deck documents four things users do after a failure — and each one is a signal the system could detect and act on. They come from Chelsea Myers and colleagues, Patterns for How Users Overcome Obstacles in Voice User Interfaces (CHI '18).

#StrategyDeck's definitionDeck's exampleWhat the system should notice
1HyperarticulationSpeaking louder and/or slower, trying to clearly articulate the utterance“Alexa, play the Beatles on Spotify” → “A-LE-XA, PLAY THE BEA-TLES ON SPO-TI-FY”Volume and rate change, same words: the acoustic model is failing, so ask rather than retry the same parse
2SimplificationReducing the complexity of the utterance, leaving only keywords“Alexa, can you play ‘Beat the intro'?” → “Alexa, ‘Beat the Intro'”Shorter, telegraphic input: the user no longer believes you can parse a sentence
3New utteranceRe-formulating the utterance to achieve the same goal“Modify my group meeting on Wednesday” → “Edit my group meeting on Wednesday”Same goal, different verb: the skill needs a synonym list, not a louder prompt
4Give more informationAdding more information to help the system disambiguate“Hey Google, play Hello” → “Hey Google, play Hello by Adele”The first attempt was under-specified: the system should have asked which Hello
Read the four as a diagnostic ladder. Hyperarticulation and simplification are acoustic repairs — the user thinks the machine cannot hear. New utterance and added information are semantic repairs — the user thinks the machine cannot understand. Which repair a person reaches for tells you which subsystem to fix.

Worked example — diagnosing a failure from the repair the user chose. Transcript: the user asks for a playlist three times, getting louder each time.

1

Classify the repair. Same words, higher volume, slower rate: hyperarticulation. That is an acoustic repair, so the user believes the system cannot hear them.

2

Test the hypothesis where the stages are separable. Show the transcript. If the words are correct, the acoustic model is fine and the real failure is downstream — the user is repairing the wrong stage, which is itself a design failure, because nothing told them where the problem was.

3

Check the confidence policy. A system that silently retries a low-confidence parse produces exactly this loop. If confidence is low the correct behaviour is to ask, not to try again harder.

4

Fix the third attempt. Repeating the same failure three times teaches the user that the system cannot be helped. The third response must be different from the first two: offer the nearest available option, or hand off to another modality such as a list on screen.

5

Log the repair pattern. The distribution of the four strategies across sessions is one of the few honest usability metrics a deployed voice interface can give you.

After a failure, the user retries with “Hey Google, play Hello by Adele” instead of “play Hello”. Which strategy is that, and what does it tell you?
Hyperarticulation — the acoustic model is failing
Simplification — the user has stopped trusting full sentences
Giving more information — the first request was ambiguous and the system should have asked which one

Designing Human-AI Interaction: Guidelines and Ethics

The deck closes with the guidelines that turn “be careful with AI” into checkable design commitments, grouped into four phases: initially, during interaction, when wrong, and over time.

Risks the guidelines are aimed at

  • Over-trust: confident output accepted without verification
  • Automation bias: the machine's suggestion outweighs better evidence
  • Inference beyond intent: purchase history, location and voice reveal what you never said
  • Bias at scale: a model's unfairness is deployed to everyone at once
  • Proactivity without consent: the system acts before it is asked

Controls that make proactive systems defensible

  • State capability and error rate up front
  • Fail loudly and early rather than plausibly
  • Keep a human veto at the level the risk justifies
  • Make explanations reachable in one step
  • Provide global controls over what is monitored
Why the deck's closing line matters: human-AI interaction is an exciting new sub-discipline requiring expertise from a wide range of professionals, and we have only scratched the surface on ethics, implementation challenges and multimodal input. For this unit, the practical consequence is that every pervasive system you build this semester now contains an inference step — so say what it infers, how often it is wrong, and who carries the cost when it is.
A predictive-policing tool is deployed in a suburb with a low accuracy rate on its risk scores. Using the deck's three-way split, what is the correct characterisation?
Automating perception, so the ethics concern is the scale of deployment
Automating judgement, so the ethics concern is that some error is inevitable
Predicting social outcomes: fundamentally dubious, with ethical concerns amplified by low accuracy

Week 8 Recap

Autonomy is a dial, not a switch

Sheridan and Verplank's ten levels run from no assistance to ignoring the human. Name the level per decision, not per product.

Core instrument

Augmentation beats automation

The deck's claim: the potential of AI lies not in automation but in augmentation. Keep the person on the decision wherever their judgement adds something.

Design stance

Accuracy and ethical risk travel separately

Perception works and creates risk through scale; judgement is imperfect and creates risk through inevitable error; social prediction is dubious and creates risk through consequence.

Capability map

Conversation has rules

Grice's cooperative principle and the maxims of quality, quantity, relation and manner describe the failures users experience — and the three-tiered confidence policy is the repair.

Voice

Users repair the system's errors

Hyperarticulation, simplification, new utterance, more information. Which one they choose tells you which stage of the pipeline is failing.

Failure analysis

Guidelines make “be careful” checkable

State capability and error rate, time services to context, explain actions, remember interactions, learn over time, and give global controls.
