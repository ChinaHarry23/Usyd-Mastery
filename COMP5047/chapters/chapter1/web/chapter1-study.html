What Ubiquitous Computing Is

Week 1 asks the question the whole unit is named after: what changes when computation stops living in a box on a desk and starts living in the world?

The founding question from the lecture: what if computers were embedded into everyday objects and environments to the point they “disappear”?

The literal reading

Computers are everywhere: in phones, watches, speakers, thermostats, lights, cars, farm sensors and clothing. On this reading ubiquitous computing is simply the observation that computation became cheap and small enough to spread.

The reading that matters here

The interesting claim is about attention, not hardware count. If the technology is working well you should not be thinking about it at all — you should be thinking about your task, the room, or the person in front of you.

Terminology

Ubiquitous computing (ubicomp) and pervasive computing name the same programme: computation distributed into environments and everyday objects rather than concentrated in one general-purpose device. Mark Weiser (1952–1999) articulated it at Xerox PARC and published it as Ubiquitous Computing in 1991.

What the unit builds on top of that vision

Prototyping tools

  • Electronics and microcontrollers (Arduino)
  • Digital fabrication: 3D printing and laser cutting

Post-desktop interaction

  • Wearables and eTextiles; tangibles
  • Movement and gesture; gaze; voice and sound; mixed reality

Weiser's Vision: Technology That Disappears

Mark Weiser (1952–1999): “The most profound technologies are those that disappear. They weave themselves into the fabric of everyday life until they are indistinguishable from it.”

Two further lines from the deck explain what Weiser was actually asking for. Machines should fit the human environment instead of forcing humans to enter theirs — using a computer should be “as refreshing as a walk in the woods”. And the design goal was to put computing back in its place: reposition it into the environmental background so we can concentrate on human-to-human interfaces rather than human-to-computer ones.

EraRatioWhere the machine livesWhat the user does
MainframeMany people, one computerIn a machine room, behind glassBooks time, submits a job, waits
PCOne person, one computerOn your desk, switched on and offSits down and operates it explicitly
UbicompOne person, many computersIn the walls, objects and environmentLives and works; the technology fades into the background
Calm technology

Technology that informs without demanding attention. Information moves between the centre of attention (what you are deliberately doing) and the periphery (what you can notice without attending to it), and it can move back to the centre when it becomes relevant. Weiser and Brown named the idea in 1996 as the design counterpart of the ubicomp vision.

Why the periphery is a design material

A kettle that clicks, a clock you can read without focusing on it, a light that changes hue as a room fills up: all encode state in the environment so you are informed without being interrupted.

What breaks calm

Notifications, alarms and modal dialogs pull attention to the centre whether or not the moment is right. A pervasive system that interrupts constantly has re-created the desktop problem in a new form factor.

A wearable nudges you with a vibration pattern you can feel but do not have to interpret consciously. Which concept does this best illustrate?
Mixed reality
Explicit interaction at the centre of attention
Calm technology: information encoded in the periphery

Context: The Core Resource

A desktop program can rely on you to type everything it needs. A pervasive system has to work out the situation for itself — that inference problem is what “context” names.

Definition

Context is any information that can be used to characterise the situation of an entity — a person, a place, or a physical object — that is relevant to the interaction between the user and the system (Dey, 2001). A system is context-aware when it uses that information to adapt its behaviour.

CategoryQuestion it answersTypical signals
IdentityWho is involved?Login, badge, paired phone, voice profile
LocationWhere is it happening?GPS, BLE beacons, room-level presence
Activity / statusWhat is going on?Motion, heart rate, whether a seat is occupied, temperature
TimeWhen, and for how long?Clock, calendar, duration of a state, time since last event
Social / relationalWho is with whom?Co-presence, proximity, conversation, shared activity

Context is what turns raw sensing into a decision. A light sensor reading of 80 lx only becomes interesting once the system also knows that a person is present, that it is evening, and that the person is reading rather than sleeping. The Week 1 project brief asks for exactly this move: context-recognition and adaptive functionality rather than a device that only responds to a button.

A meeting room dims its lights and shows a “busy” panel when it detects that people are talking. Which part of that is the context?
The change in light level
The inferred situation: the room is occupied and a conversation is happening
The busy panel

Pervasive vs Mobile Computing

Mobile computing is the era we already live in; pervasive computing is the paradigm it grew into. Both involve computers away from a desk, so the exam question is always about which property does the distinguishing.

PropertyMobile computingPervasive computing
Unit of designOne personal, general-purpose deviceA system of many special-purpose devices and sensors
Relationship to placeWorks anywhere; place is mostly irrelevantDeeply situated: the room, the site, the ecology is the point
Role of contextOpportunistic (GPS, orientation, notifications)Constitutive: without context the system has nothing to adapt to
Interaction styleMostly explicit: you unlock and operate itIncreasingly implicit: the system senses and acts around you
Attention demandedHigh — the screen competes for focusShould be low — calm technology in the periphery
Typical failureDistraction and interruptionWrong inference, privacy breach, or an action nobody asked for
Careful with the exam wording: mobile computing is not “wrong” or superseded. It is the delivery model that made the ubicomp vision affordable. The distinguishing claim is that pervasive systems are embedded, situated and context-driven, and that they aim to reduce the attention they demand rather than to capture it.
A museum installs beacons so that a phone app shows information about the exhibit you are standing in front of. What keeps this closer to mobile computing than to pervasive computing?
The interaction still happens on one personal device, and the visitor must attend to its screen
The beacons are too cheap and simple to count as pervasive technology
Location is being used, and using location is a mobile-computing technique

How Interaction Design Got Here

The lecture traces a shift in design attention: from the object, to the average user, to specific users, and finally to the interaction between people, hardware and software.

Bill Moggridge on interaction design

“This would be the equivalent of industrial design but in software rather than three-dimensional objects. Like industrial design, the discipline would start from the needs and desires of the people who use a product or service, and strive to create designs that would give aesthetic pleasure as well as lasting satisfaction and enjoyment.”

The four shifts to memorise

  • Object — make the artefact beautiful and manufacturable
  • Average user — anthropometrics and human factors
  • Specific users — target demographics, usability testing
  • Interaction — the loop between user, hardware and software
Why a pervasive-computing unit starts here: once the artefact is a behaviour rather than an object, the design question stops being “what does it look like?” and becomes “what does it do, when, and on what evidence?”

Invention, Inquiry and Grounded Innovation

Three terms the deck defines

Invention — creating something new. Inquiry — understanding what something is and how it works. Innovation — the adoption of a new practice. Invention is vision-driven; inquiry is needs-driven.

Opportunity → invention → solution

Start from a possibility and build something that demonstrates it. Gadgets, human-centred design work and grounded innovation all sit on this side of the deck's diagram.

Problem → inquiry → solution

Start from an observed situation, study it (sociology, ethnography, field work) and let the understanding produce the design. This is the side that keeps prototypes honest.

Lars Erik Holmquist, as quoted in the deck: grounded innovation needs “both a high level of originality and invention (to come up with something new) and a strong grounding base of inquiry in the real world (to know what the possibilities and opportunities really are)”.

The risk of inquiry-only

Solving immediate, observed needs produces small iterative improvements. The deck's challenge to the class: how can we take big steps?

The risk of invention-only

Prototypes that emerge from pure exploratory invention often do not address a real need. Greenberg and Buxton's warning applies: evaluation must suit the problem, not the other way round.

Herbert Simon (1916–2001): “To design is to devise courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones.” Vision tells you which preferred situation to aim at; inquiry tells you whether you understand the current one.
A team reads that koalas are being hit on a particular road, and builds a sensor-triggered warning sign for drivers. Which process does this describe?
Invention first, because the sign is a new artefact
Inquiry-led: the design follows from studying a real situation
Neither — it only counts as design once a usability study is run

Worked example — placing an idea on the invention–inquiry map.

A student proposes “a solar-powered sign that warns drivers when wildlife is near the road”. Place it step by step:

1

Name the claim. The idea asserts that driver behaviour changes when a warning appears at the right moment. That is an invention: something new is being created.

2

Ask what is already known. Where do animals actually cross? At what times? Do drivers slow down for static signs? Each question is inquiry, and each answer changes the design.

3

Decide what would make it grounded. Detection that works at night and in rain, a warning that is understandable in two seconds, and a placement based on observed crossing data — not on where the sign is easiest to install.

4

State the risk. The invention-only failure mode is a clever detector that warns at the wrong place. The inquiry-only failure mode is another static sign. Grounded innovation is the deliberate pairing of the two.

Designing a Pervasive System

Two processes are worth separating: how you design the system, and how the finished system runs. The lecture and tutorial exercise both.

StageQuestion to askWeek 1 example
SenseWhat signal actually carries the information I need?Motion, light level, soil moisture, audio level
Infer contextWhat situation does this reading imply, and how confident am I?“A person is reading in this room after sunset”
DecideWhat should happen, and what should deliberately not happen?Dim the lamp, but do not flash a notification
ActWhich output channel is least demanding?Change the light level rather than play a sound

Worked example — running one scenario through the loop. Scenario: a balcony garden that keeps herbs alive through a Sydney summer.

1

Sense. Capacitive soil-moisture probes in two pots, an ambient light sensor, and a temperature reading. Moisture is the variable that actually matters; the rest help interpret it.

2

Condition. Moisture probes are noisy and drift, so each reading is averaged over a window and calibrated against a dry and a saturated sample before it is trusted.

3

Infer context. Moisture below threshold and temperature above 28 °C and no rain in the forecast means “this pot is drying out today”. One number alone would over-water after rain.

4

Decide. Water for a fixed number of seconds only if the pot is dry, at most once per day — a threshold plus a state machine, not a model.

5

Act, then observe. Open a valve and log the moisture afterwards. The log is the evidence that the inference was right, and it is what you show when asked whether the system works.

Tutorial exercise — the non-human client. The Week 1 tutorial asks you to advise on a device installed in a specific place, where the client is not a human: it is the animal, plant or ecosystem the device is meant to serve. Worked answer for the magpie that swoops the same corner every September:

1

What the client needs. The magpie needs its nesting territory to stay viable while pedestrians pass through it: distance, predictability and no new perches for predators.

2

What would make the client worse off. Anything that habituates the bird to people, removes the shrubs it nests in, or makes the corner more attractive to cats. A deterrent tuned only to human comfort can be a net loss for the bird.

3

What the client would never notice. Signage, an app, a dashboard, a notification. Those are for the humans; the bird only experiences the physical change in the place.

4

Where the idea anthropomorphised. “The magpie is angry at pedestrians” imports a human motive. The defensible framing is seasonal territorial defence during nesting — a claim you can check against observed dates.

5

What you still need to learn. Swooping dates and locations, the vegetation the birds depend on, and how pedestrians currently route around the corner. Until you have those, every design decision is a guess.

Future directions in the deck: Gregory Abowd's provocation “Ubicomp is dead! Week 1 Recap

Ubicomp is a claim about attention

Computers embedded in everyday objects until they disappear from awareness — so attention can go back to people and tasks.

Core definition

Three eras, one trajectory

Mainframe (many people, one machine) → PC (one person, one machine) → ubicomp (one person, many machines in the environment).

Historical spine

Context is the resource

Identity, location, activity, time and social relations let a system infer the situation instead of asking the user to describe it.

Inference

Invention × inquiry

Vision-driven invention supplies the big step; needs-driven inquiry keeps the prototype honest. Grounded innovation pairs both.
