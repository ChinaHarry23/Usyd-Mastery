Foundations: the vocabulary behind pervasive computing

Week 1 is conceptual rather than mathematical, so this page does the equivalent job: it pins down the definitions the lecture relies on, builds a taxonomy of computing paradigms, gives you a compact notation for context and the sense–reason–act loop, and ends with the checklist that turns a vague exam answer into a markable one.

Definitions that must be exact

Marks in this unit are lost on definitions more often than on ideas. Write each of these in one sentence, with the consequence included.

Ubiquitous / pervasive computing

Computation embedded into everyday objects and environments to the point that it “disappears” from attention, so that people deal with other people and with their task rather than with a computer.

Context

Any information that can be used to characterise the situation of an entity — person, place or object — that is relevant to the interaction between the user and the system. A system is context-aware when it uses that information to adapt its behaviour.

Calm technology

Technology that informs without demanding attention: information is encoded in the periphery and can move to the centre of attention when it becomes relevant, and back again when it does not.

Invention, inquiry, innovation

Invention is creating something new (vision-driven). Inquiry is understanding what something is and how it works (needs-driven). Innovation is the adoption of a new practice. Grounded innovation pairs a high level of originality with a strong grounding base of inquiry in the real world.

Axis 2 — the paradigm (mobile vs pervasive)

Mobile computing

  • One general-purpose personal device
  • Place largely irrelevant
  • Explicit interaction through a screen
  • Context used opportunistically

Pervasive computing

  • Many cooperating devices and sensors
  • Deeply situated in a place
  • Implicit interaction is the default
  • Context is constitutive: without it there is nothing to adapt to

Context and the sense–reason–act loop

Three relationships are worth being able to write from memory: how context is composed, how a pervasive system is structured, and what grounded innovation multiplies.

1. Context is a composite, not a scalar

Context is not one number. It is the union of the categories that are relevant to the interaction:

\[ C \;=\; C_{\text{identity}} \cup C_{\text{location}} \cup C_{\text{activity}} \cup C_{\text{time}} \cup C_{\text{social}} \]

Practical consequence: a reading only becomes context once it is joined with the other categories. “80 lx” is a measurement; “80 lx, one person present, 21:40, reading” is context.

2. A pervasive system is a loop

Sensors produce readings \(R\), an inference step maps them to context \(C\), a decision step maps context to an action \(A\), and the action changes the world that the sensors read next:

\[ S \;=\; \langle\, R \;\xrightarrow{\;f\;}\; C \;\xrightarrow{\;g\;}\; A \;\longrightarrow\; R' \,\rangle \]

Two design questions follow immediately from the diagram. What does \(f\) get wrong, and what happens to the user when it does? A loop with no answer to the second question is the standard source of “smart” devices that people switch off.

3. Grounded innovation multiplies rather than adds

With \(V\) for vision-driven originality and \(Q\) for the grounding base of inquiry:

\[ G \;=\; V \cdot Q \]

The product form is the point: if either factor is zero the result is zero. A gadget with \(Q = 0\) never addresses a need; fieldwork with \(V = 0\) produces only small iterative improvements. The lecture's challenge — how do we take big steps? — is the request for both factors to be large at once.

4. Visions outlive the technology that realises them

The deck's three horizons:

\[ h_{\text{vision}} \approx 100\ \text{years}, \qquad h_{\text{applications}} \approx 10\ \text{years}, \qquad h_{\text{tech}} \approx 1\ \text{year} \]

Judge a vision by whether it still provokes work (MEMEX, 1945; man–computer symbiosis, 1960; ubiquitous computing, 1991), an application by whether people adopt it, and a technology by its specification.

Attention as the design objective. The ubicomp vision and calm technology can be stated together as: minimise \(E_a\), the attention the system demands, subject to still delivering the outcome the person wanted. Every other property — device count, context richness, connectivity — is instrumental to that.

Classification checklist

Most Week 1 exam questions reduce to “is this mobile or pervasive, and why?”. Run the system through these steps in order and the justification writes itself.

StepLook forMarks are lost when…
1. Devices and locusOne personal device, or a system embedded in a place?You list hardware without saying where the reasoning happens
2. Role of placeWould the system work unchanged anywhere?You assume “uses GPS” means “pervasive”
3. Context categoriesIdentity, location, activity, time, socialYou call a raw sensor reading “context”
4. Interaction styleExplicit, implicit or autonomousYou treat any sensing as implicit interaction
5. AttentionDoes it interrupt, or sit in the periphery?You describe features instead of the attentional cost
6. Failure modeWrong inference, privacy, unwanted actionYou claim the design has no trade-offs
A farm installs soil probes, a weather feed and an automatic irrigation controller that nobody has to check. Which label is most defensible?
Mobile computing, because the system works without a desk
Context-free automation, because no user is present
Pervasive computing: situated, context-driven and acting implicitly

Worked classification. Place the three systems below with the checklist, in two sentences each.

1

A museum phone app with beacons. One personal general-purpose device; the place supplies only a location cue; interaction is explicit (you look at the screen); attention demanded is high. Verdict: mobile computing that uses one piece of context.

2

A room that dims its lights when people are reading. Many special-purpose devices embedded in one place; identity, activity and time are all used; interaction is implicit; attention demanded is low by design. Verdict: pervasive, and a calm-technology case. Failure mode: wrong inference makes the light annoying rather than helpful.

3

The irrigation controller. Sensors and actuators are embedded on site; context composition decides watering; interaction is autonomous; the person is informed rather than asked. Verdict: pervasive. Failure mode: over-watering when the inference is wrong, and the cost falls on the garden and the water bill, not on the device.

4

The sentence that earns the mark. “It is pervasive because the computation is embedded in a specific place, the behaviour is driven by composed context rather than by an explicit command, and the design goal is to lower the attention it demands — with the trade-off that an incorrect inference acts on the world without being asked.”

Tutorial ideation review

The Week 1 tutorial is an ideation studio: one specific non-human encounter, a mind map of the concepts involved, then eight or more response ideas judged afterwards. Use the checklist to audit what you produced.

Be specific

Where, when, what actually happened, what you noticed, and what you did not understand. “The possum that got into the roof and would not leave” is the level of detail required.

Mind map the concepts

Actors, place, season, materials, conflicts, the humans involved, and what is unknown.

Ideate through the lens list

Shelter, deter, attract, feed, warn, reveal, mediate, remember, withdraw — let each word suggest a different device.

Run the novelty check

List twenty existing projects that do something similar and ask how you would iterate to make yours unique. Ideas that arrive quickly arrived quickly for everyone.

Worked answer — auditing one ideation pass. Encounter: the magpie that swooped the same corner every September.

1

Check the specificity. “Same corner every September” names a place and a season. Add the street, the time of day, and what you did in response.

2

Check the concept map. Nesting season, the tree or shrub being defended, pedestrian traffic, sightlines, other birds, council signage, and the fact that you do not know which individual bird it is.

3

Check the lens coverage. Deter/redirect (move people rather than frighten the bird), reveal/translate (let a pedestrian feel that nesting is under way), mediate (a temporary route that both species can live with), withdraw (remove the perch that makes the corner attractive). Four lenses, four genuinely different devices.

4

Check for anthropomorphism. “The magpie is angry” imports a human motive. “The corner is within the territory of a nesting pair in September” is a claim you can test against observed swooping dates.

5

Check novelty. Existing work includes scaring devices, council warning signs, citizen-science swooping maps and wildlife-aware routing. The iteration that makes this unique is usually a change of client: design for the bird's continued use of the corner, and let the human be the one who routes around it.
