From SketchPad to Multi-Touch

The lecture opens with a lineage rather than a definition: gestural interaction on a surface is not a recent invention, and every step in the line adds one capability that the previous step lacked.

The framing quote from the deck: “And we have invented a new technology called multi-touch, which is phenomenal.” — Steve Jobs, 2007. The interesting question for this unit is what had to be invented before that sentence could be said, and what the sentence quietly leaves out.

Working definition

Gestural interaction is input in which the system infers intent from the movement, configuration or contact pattern of the hand and body, rather than from the actuation of a dedicated control. Two families dominate this week: surface gestures on a touch-sensitive plane, and mid-air gestures performed in the space around the device.

What direct touch buys you

  • The finger is the pointer — no intermediate device to acquire
  • The on-screen object is touched where it appears
  • Bimanual and multi-finger input become possible
  • The vocabulary is learnable in seconds (tap, drag, pinch)

What it costs you

  • The hand covers what it manipulates
  • Contact geometry is ambiguous and changes with posture
  • There is no hover state, so nothing can be previewed
  • Precision and fatigue limits arrive with the finger itself
Exam framing: the lineage is not a story about screens getting better. It is a story about the input problem changing shape — from “how does the machine know where the pen is?” (1963) to “how does the machine know which of these contact points is a fingertip and which is a palm?” (2007).
What does the Sensor Frame (McAvinney, 1986) add to the lineage that SketchPad does not already demonstrate?
The ability to draw directly on a display
A set of simultaneous contact points instead of a single pointer position
The overlaying of paper and projected digital content

The Challenges of Direct Input

Touch is only “direct” from the user's point of view. From the system's point of view the finger is a large, soft, opaque blob whose contact geometry is ambiguous — and the list below is the deck's own account of what that costs.

ChallengeWhy it happensWhat the designer can do about it
Fat fingerA fingertip covers roughly 1–2 cm of a display, so a contact point is a region, not a coordinateEnlarge targets, snap to the nearest selectable item, offset the target away from the point of contact
OcclusionThe hand that touches also hides the content underneath itDisplace the visual feedback above the finger, or move the control surface away from the display
Palm rejectionHeel, thumb base and forearm rest on the surface while writing or drawingClassify contacts by size and shape, ignore the largest blob, require a stylus to switch modes
Support for bimanual skillsHuman hands are asymmetric: a non-dominant hand holds and orients while the dominant hand actsGive the non-dominant hand a continuous role (anchor, frame, tool palette) instead of treating it as a second pointer
Soft vs hard touchA press carries force and duration information that a binary contact does notAdd pressure or contact-area sensing where the hardware supports it
Points of contactSensors report blobs, and blobs merge or splitUse temporal tracking: identities persist across frames, so merging can be resolved by continuity
ParallaxThe sensing layer sits behind the display layer, so the reported position is offset from what the eye seesCalibrate the layers, or keep the sensing plane and the display plane optically coincident
The two point-of-contact failure modes, straight from the deck. Two fingers touching close together are reported as one point; a single large contact — a thumb, a palm edge, a resting forearm — is reported as two. Both errors are geometric rather than electrical, which is why they are fixed with tracking and classification rather than with a better sensor.
A drawing app on a tablet registers a long stroke across the canvas every time the user's resting hand shifts. Which challenge of direct input is this?
Occlusion
Palm rejection
Parallax

Worked example — designing one control for a gloved finger on a wet screen. A field device for orchard workers must be used outdoors, one-handed, with work gloves, in the rain. Work through the deck's list in order.

1

Fat finger, amplified. A gloved fingertip is far wider than a bare one, so the effective target size grows again. Every control becomes a large tile with generous spacing; the smallest interactive element is a swipe, not a tap.

2

Soft vs hard touch. A glove removes the pressure signal that a bare finger still leaks through contact area. The interface must therefore not depend on press force at all: selection is by dwell or by an explicit confirm.

3

Points of contact. Water on the surface and a loosely fitting glove both create spurious and merging contacts. Temporal tracking with stable identities is required before any gesture is recognised, or a raindrop becomes a tap.

4

Occlusion and bimanual skill. One-handed use in the field means the other hand is holding the branch, not the device. Feedback must be displaced (above the finger, or as audio/haptic output) because the finger covers the control, and no design may assume a second hand is available.

5

The sentence that earns the mark. “Direct touch moves the input device onto the display, which removes the acquisition step but imports the properties of the hand: a large ambiguous contact region, self-occlusion, resting contacts that must be rejected, and no hover. Each is a sensing problem with a design consequence, not just an engineering defect.”

Tracking the Body: Extrinsic and Intrinsic Sensing

Mid-air interaction begins with a question the deck asks directly: how can we track people's bodies? The first answer is not a sensor but a decision about where the sensors live.

Extrinsic — sensors in the environment

The body is tracked by sensors placed in the environment. Nothing is worn. Covered in the deck by structured light (RGB camera plus IR illumination, IR camera — Kinect v1), time-of-flight (Kinect v2), passive and active IR markers, and plain RGB cameras running pose estimation.

Intrinsic — sensors on the body

The body is tracked through sensors directly attached to it. Nothing external is needed, and the person can leave the room. Covered in the deck by inertial measurement units: accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer — sensitive enough to pick up microgestures.

Open-source pose estimation, named in the deck: MMPose, MediaPipe's pose landmarker and OpenPose make markerless extrinsic tracking a software problem rather than a hardware one. Whenever a technique becomes a library, the research question moves up a level — from “can we track the body?” to “what should the interface do with a tracked body?”
A researcher needs to detect finger movements of a few millimetres while the participant walks around a city block. Which approach fits, and why?
Extrinsic structured light, because it is the most accurate for small movements
Intrinsic inertial sensing, because the sensors travel with the body and resolve motion at the source
RGB pose estimation with OpenPose, because it needs no markers

A Taxonomy of Gestures

Before asking how to recognise a gesture, the deck asks what kind of thing a gesture is. Six types are named, and each one carries a different recognition problem and a different design obligation.

TypeWhat the machine must recoverWhere it is usedCharacteristic failure
DeicticA target: which object, and where it isPointing at a screen, a lamp, a shop; disambiguating “that one”Pointing is a line, not a coordinate — the intended target is under-determined
ManipulativeA continuous parameter that tracks the handDragging, pinching, rotating, physically moving a proxyMapping breaks when the hand's degrees of freedom exceed the parameter's
SemaphoricA label from a fixed dictionaryShortcuts, media control, stroke alphabets (Graffiti, unistroke, multistroke, $N-protractor)Users cannot guess the dictionary; the same gesture means different things to different people
Motion correlationWhich moving target the user is matchingSelecting one of several independent moving objectsSlower than pointing; requires the target to be moving and distinguishable
GesticulationMeaning carried alongside speechAugmenting a spoken interface; expressive systemsIdiosyncratic and continuous — there is no dictionary to classify against
Sign languageLinguistic structure: signs plus grammarConversational interfaces for deaf usersReal linguistic systems, not gesture sets; recognition must handle grammar, not just vocabulary
Deictic techniques named in the deck

Turning a point into a screen coordinate is a design choice, not a measurement. Image-plane pointing intersects the pointing ray with the camera image plane. Fixed-point pointing assumes a stable origin and works from the ray's direction. Shadow reaching uses the hand's shadow as the pointer, keeping the arm low. MultiRay (Matulic & Vogel, 2018) reads several rays at once — through the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) and distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints — so one pointing posture yields more than one target estimate.

How a Gesture Becomes a Command

Every gesture system, on a touchscreen or in mid-air, runs the same four stages. The taxonomy tells you what the last stage must produce; the sensing choice tells you what the first stage has to work with.

StageQuestion it answersDeck vocabulary
SegmentationWhen does the gesture start and stop?“Determining when a gesture starts and stops” — the deck's first listed challenge
FeaturesWhat is the smallest description that still separates the gesture set?Follows from the sensing choice: joint positions extrinisically, rates and orientations intrinsically
RecognitionWhich gesture is it?“Distinguishing between gestures”
FeedbackHow does the user know it worked?“How can we make sure the gesture is correct?”
The uncomfortable property of this pipeline. Errors compound left to right. A classifier cannot repair a bad segmentation, and no amount of feedback repairs a classifier that was never given a reject option. When a gesture system “feels unreliable”, the fault is usually one stage earlier than where the user notices it.
A recogniser returns “swipe left, 0.42 confidence” and the system performs the swipe anyway. Which stage of the pipeline is at fault?
Segmentation, because the gesture boundaries must be wrong
The decision after classification: a confidence of 0.42 should have produced a reject or a confirmation, not an action
Feedback, because the user was not told the gesture was recognised

Worked example — debugging a wave-to-dismiss feature that fires at random. Users report that the TV mutes itself while they are gesturing at someone in the room. Walk the pipeline from the left.

1

Segmentation first. The most likely cause is that the segmenter has no explicit start trigger, so any motion of sufficient magnitude opens a candidate window. Fix: require an explicit onset (a raised hand entering the tracking volume) before a gesture window can open.

2

Then features. A wave and a conversational gesture differ in where they happen and in how periodic they are. Adding a feature for periodicity and for distance from the torso separates them far better than a longer template.

3

Then the classifier's reject option. If the classifier is forced to choose among the gesture set, every conversational movement becomes the nearest label. A confidence threshold that outputs “no gesture” is not a nicety; it is the difference between a working system and a haunted one.

4

Then feedback. Even with a good classifier, dismissing on recognition alone removes the user's chance to notice a mistake. Show the gesture was seen (an on-screen echo of the recognised label) and let the action commit after it, so a misrecognition is visible rather than mysterious.

5

The takeaway sentence. “Gesture recognition is a pipeline whose stages can only lose information: segmentation defines the candidate, features define the comparison, classification adds a reject option, and feedback gives the user a chance to catch the errors that remain.”

Guessability: Finding Intuitive Gestures

The deck states the problem bluntly: different people read different meanings out of the same gesture. It then asks how you would discover intuitive mappings for a gesture set — and answers with a method, not an intuition.

The claim to memorise, verbatim in spirit: “There is no inherently natural set of gestures for performing anything beyond a couple of the most commonplace manipulations.” Any claim that a gesture is “natural” is therefore an empirical claim that has to be tested on the people who will use it.

Before

Participants are shown the effect — the command and what it does — and are asked to invent the gesture that would trigger it. No gesture vocabulary is shown.

After

Every proposal is recorded and pooled across participants. What matters is not any single answer but the distribution of answers.

Agreement

The gesture with the highest agreement score becomes the candidate for that command. Low agreement means the command has no intuitive gesture and needs a visible affordance instead.

The output

A gesture set whose members are ranked by how likely users are to guess them — the empirical substitute for the designer's intuition.

What elicitation fixes

  • Discoverability — the set contains gestures users actually produce
  • Learnability — gestures that were guessed are remembered better than gestures that were taught
  • Ambiguity — agreement scores expose commands that share a gesture

What it does not fix

  • Fatigue: an intuitive gesture can still be an exhausting one
  • Tracking: agreement says nothing about whether the sensor can see it
  • Social acceptability: what people invent alone is not what they will do in public
A team runs an elicitation study and finds that “next track” produced eleven different gestures from twenty participants. What is the correct reading of that result?
The study failed and should be re-run with better instructions
The command has no intuitive gesture, so it should be given a visible control or an explicitly taught shortcut
The eleven gestures should be averaged into one composite gesture

Problems of the machine

  • Segmentation — determining when a gesture starts and stops
  • Recognition — distinguishing between gestures
  • Unobtrusive tracking — minimising the burden on the user

Problems of the person

  • Discoverability — how do users find out which gestures exist?
  • Learnability — how do users learn and remember them?

Cost named in the deckWhy it appearsDesign response
Gorilla armsMid-air gestures require holding the arm against gravity; the cost scales with repetition, not with a single useKeep gestures short and low, allow resting postures, or prefer wrist and finger movement over whole-arm movement
EncumbranceIntrinsic tracking means wearing something, and worn sensors must be charged, donned and toleratedUse what the person already carries; make wearing optional; keep extrinsic tracking as the fallback
Social acceptabilityThe gesture is visible to everyone nearby, and its meaning is notMake the gesture small and quiet, or move the interaction to a private channel — and treat this as a design requirement, not a matter of taste
Two that the deck leaves implicit, and the exam will not: gesture sets collide with accessibility when they assume a standard range of motion, and every always-on camera brings a privacy question that a button never raised. Neither is an argument against gesture — both are reasons the sensing choice (extrinsic camera, worn IMU, or touch) is a design decision with consequences beyond accuracy.
A gesture system tracks perfectly, recognises every gesture correctly, and has a well-designed gesture set — but users stop using it after a week. Which challenge from the deck's list is the most likely explanation?
Segmentation, because gestures that start and stop ambiguously eventually fail
Learnability or physical cost: users forget a set they do not use daily, or the gesture is tiring or awkward in public
Feedback, because users never learn that the gesture was recognised

Week 6 Recap

Gesture is old; the sensing is new

SketchPad (1963), the sensor frame (1986), the Digital Desk (1991) and multi-touch (2007) are four answers to the same question: how does the machine know where the hand is?

Lineage

Direct input imports the hand

Fat finger, occlusion, palm rejection, bimanual asymmetry, soft vs hard touch, merging contact points, parallax — every one of them is a property of the hand, not a defect of the sensor.

Surface

Extrinsic or intrinsic

Sensors in the environment (structured light, time-of-flight, markers, RGB pose estimation) or sensors on the body (accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer). Tracking volume and occlusion versus drift.

Mid-air

Taxonomy before recognition

Deictic, manipulative, semaphoric, motion correlation, gesticulation, sign language. The type tells you whether the recogniser must output a target, a parameter or a label.

Classification

Guessability beats intuition

There is no inherently natural gesture set beyond a few commonplace manipulations, so it has to be elicited: show the effect, collect the proposals, rank them by agreement.

Method

The user-side challenges end deployments

Discoverability, learnability, ambiguity, feedback, gorilla arms, encumbrance and social acceptability. Track and classify perfectly and the system can still be abandoned in a week.

Reality check
The deck's own conclusion, restated for an exam: gestural interaction, on a surface or in mid-air, is a powerful input modality because it leverages our intuitions about direct manipulation; those intuitions can be learned to a degree through gesture elicitation studies; and it nevertheless presents challenges ranging from technical tracking problems to supporting users in discovering and learning new gestures.

Check yourself

Can you name the four milestones, classify a gesture into one of the six types, and state what each stage of the recognition pipeline must produce?

Run a miniature elicitation study: pick five commands, ask three people to invent a gesture for each, and see where the agreement is low. That column is where your interface needs a visible control.
