Foundations: dimensions, tolerances and process choice

Week 5 is a making week rather than a formula week, so this page does the equivalent job: it pins down the definitions the lecture relies on, builds the taxonomy of fabrication families, gives you compact notation for the arithmetic that actually decides whether a part fits — tolerance, kerf, layer count and tooling cost — and ends with the procedure for choosing a process.

Definitions that must be exact

Marks in this unit are lost on definitions more often than on ideas. Write each of these in one sentence, with the consequence included.

Digital fabrication

A manufacturing process in which the machine is controlled by a computer. The design exists as a digital model, and the machine executes instructions derived from that model rather than being guided by hand. It is not a synonym for 3D printing: laser cutters, vinyl cutters, water jets and CNC mills are all digital fabrication.

Additive manufacturing

Create an object by depositing material. The deck's examples are fused deposition modelling, binder jetting, stereolithography and digital light processing. Consequence: the part is a stack of layers, so layer height sets vertical resolution and overhangs need support structures (in FDM and SLA).

Subtractive manufacturing

Create an object by removing material from a solid block of material. The deck's examples are CNC router, mill and lathe, plasma torch, vinyl cutter, laser cutter and water jet. Consequence: material waste, limited by tool reach, but fast and accurate on sheet stock.

Forming

Create an object by pouring, injecting or pressing material into a mould: injection moulding and resin casting in the deck. Consequence: one master or tool produces many identical parts, so the economics are dominated by tooling cost and production volume.

“Looks like” and “works like”

A looks-like prototype explores appearance: it evokes the right visual semantics, explores proportions and shape, and enables study of placement in context. A works-like prototype explores function and physical behaviour: the mechanism, the feel of the interaction, the weight of the device. They are different instruments for different questions and are rarely the same object.

CAD, CAM and G-code

CAD — Computer-Aided Design: producing the digital model, exported as a mesh (the deck lists .stl, .obj, .dae, .x3d, .wrl and .ply). CAM — Computer-Aided Manufacturing: checking and repairing that mesh, slicing it into layers and emitting machine instructions. G-code is that instruction stream: the machine-specific output of the CAM stage.

Kerf

The width of material a cutting process removes along its path. It is why a laser-cut slot is wider than the line you drew, and why every joint has to be designed from the measured thickness of the stock plus the kerf rather than from the nominal dimension.

Tolerance, kerf and cost arithmetic

Four relationships decide whether a prototype actually goes together and what it costs. Every number below is either from the Week 5 deck or from the Autodesk Fusion tutorial.

1. The enclosure is a tolerance stack

The tutorial measures the Arduino Uno and then adds a deliberate error tolerance of “1.00 mm each side” before sketching. In notation, with \(\delta = 1.00\ \text{mm}\):

\[ W_{in} = W_{board} + 2\delta \qquad H_{in} = H_{board} + 2\delta \]

The wall is then created with an offset rather than a second rectangle, and the tutorial uses \(t = 2\ \text{mm}\):

\[ W_{out} = W_{in} + 2t = W_{board} + 2\delta + 2t \]

Practical consequence: the enclosure is not “the board plus a bit”. It is a sum of named contributions, and if the board arrives 0.4 mm wider than measured, the 1.00 mm per side is what absorbs it.

2. Base and walls are two extrusions with a direction

The base is extruded by \(h_{base} = 2\ \text{mm}\); the walls are extruded from the offset profile by more than that, with \(h_{wall} \ge h_{base} + c\) where the tutorial simply adds about 3 mm of clearance. Two rules come with the operation:

\[ \text{distance} > 0 \Rightarrow \text{one direction}, \qquad \text{distance} < 0 \Rightarrow \text{the opposite direction} \]

The sign is direction, not size: the wall extrude must be +2 and not −2, while the text engraving is deliberately extruded with a negative distance so the cut goes into the model. The operation matters too — Join merges the new extrusion with the existing body so the enclosure prints as one piece, whereas New Body would leave two separate overlapping solids; Cut removes material, which is what turns text into an engraving.

3. Kerf turns a nominal dimension into a cut dimension

A laser removes the kerf \(k\) along every path. To end up with a slot that the sheet actually fits into, you draw the slot undersize by the kerf and add the clearance you want:

\[ w_{slot} = t_{meas} - k + c \]

where \(t_{meas}\) is the measured sheet thickness, not the nominal one. Two further numbers from the deck belong in the same breath: every shape must be a 0.01 pt single-path outline, and

\[ 0.01\ \text{pt} = 0.01 \times \tfrac{1}{72}\ \text{in} \approx 0.00353\ \text{mm} \approx 3.5\ \mu\text{m} \]

So the line weight the cutter follows is three orders of magnitude thinner than the tolerance a 3D printer works to. The imprecision in a laser-cut joint comes from the stock and the kerf, never from the path.

4. Layer count, material volume and time

A printed part of height \(H\) at layer height \(h_{layer}\) is a stack of \(N\) layers:

\[ N = \left\lceil \frac{H}{h_{layer}} \right\rceil \]

With the deck's ranges, a 60 mm-tall shell is 200 layers at FDM's finest 0.3–0.1 mm setting, and 2400+ layers at SLA's 0.025 mm. Material use is set by the shells plus the infill fraction \(\phi\) of the interior volume:

\[ V \approx V_{shells} + \phi\, V_{interior} \]

Print time tracks \(V\) plus the number of layers, which is why the two cheap levers are a thicker layer and less infill — and why both levers cost you surface quality and stiffness respectively.

5. Tooling cost is amortised over the run

The deck gives two cost structures: injection moulding has “high upfront costs, low cost per part” and is used for large volumes; resin casting is “cheap and suitable for small production runs”. For a run of \(n\) parts with tooling cost \(C_{tool}\):

\[ C(n) = \frac{C_{tool}}{n} + c_{part} \]

The break-even between a cheap-to-tool, expensive-per-part route (resin casting) and an expensive-to-tool, cheap-per-part route (injection moulding) sits where the two curves cross:

\[ n^{*} = \frac{C_{tool}^{inj} - C_{tool}^{cast}}{c_{cast} - c_{inj}} \]

Below \(n^{*}\), the silicone mould wins; above it, the steel tool does. This is why the deck places resin casting between the 3D printed prototype and the production tool in the lifecycle diagram.

The single design rule behind all five: decide what a dimension is for before you write it down. A dimension that has to locate two parts is a fit dimension and needs the measured stock plus clearance; a dimension that only has to look right can be nominal. Most failed assemblies in this unit are dimensions that were never classified.

Choosing a process

Worked answer — dimensioning an enclosure and checking that it is manufacturable. Take a board measured at 68.6 × 53.4 mm, \(\delta = 1.00\) mm per side, wall \(t = 2\) mm, base 2 mm, wall height 5 mm.

1

Inside dimensions. \(W_{in} = 68.6 + 2(1.00) = 70.6\) mm and \(H_{in} = 53.4 + 2(1.00) = 55.4\) mm. These are the sketch dimensions of the inner rectangle.

2

Outside dimensions. Offset the rectangle by \(t = 2\) mm: \(W_{out} = 70.6 + 4 = 74.6\) mm, \(H_{out} = 55.4 + 4 = 59.4\) mm. If the offset appears outside the blue line, flip its direction before accepting it.

3

Height. Base extrusion \(h_{base} = 2\) mm, wall extrusion \(h_{wall} = 5\) mm with the Join operation, so the total is one body 5 mm tall with a 2 mm floor — 3 mm of internal depth above the base, which is exactly the clearance the tutorial adds over the 2 mm base.

4

Layers. At \(h_{layer} = 0.2\) mm, \(N = \lceil 5 / 0.2 \rceil = 25\) layers. At SLA's 0.025 mm the same part is 200 layers, which is the resolution you are paying the $$ for.

5

Manufacturability check. 74.6 × 59.4 × 5 mm fits the 600 × 900 mm laser bed many times over and is far below the 3 cm thickness limit — so if the enclosure is to be laser cut from sheet, the constraint is not the bed but the joinery: the walls and base must be finger-jointed, and the joint must be cut from the measured sheet thickness plus kerf. If it is to be printed, the constraint is overhangs and supports.

Worked answer — a kerf and fit calculation, and what to do when it fails. Nominal 3 mm acrylic, measured at 2.85 mm; the laser kerf measures 0.2 mm; you want a hand-tight finger joint with 0.1 mm of clearance.

1

Start from the measured thickness, not the nominal one. \(t_{meas} = 2.85\) mm. Designing to 3.00 mm produces a slot 0.15 mm too wide before the kerf is even considered.

2

Subtract the kerf and add the clearance. \(w_{slot} = t_{meas} - k + c = 2.85 - 0.2 + 0.1 = 2.75\) mm. A 2.75 mm drawn slot comes out at 2.95 mm after the beam has removed its 0.2 mm, which is 0.1 mm looser than the sheet — a hand-tight fit.

3

Test the assumption before trusting it. Cut a comb of slots at 2.65, 2.75 and 2.85 mm in cardboard or plywood, as the deck instructs, and measure which one fits. Kerf varies with material, thickness, power and focus, so the arithmetic gives you the starting point, not the answer.

4

Check the file, not just the numbers. Every shape must be a 0.01 pt single-path outline; press Ctrl+Y and if a shape does not appear it will not cut. A correct dimension on a path the cutter ignores is still a failed part.

5

Dry-fit before adhesives. Interior cuts, then exterior cuts, then assemble with no glue. Acrylic cement is flagged “caution!” in the deck: it is a solvent with no second chance, so the dry fit is where you discover that the slot is 0.1 mm wrong.

Tutorial numbers & self-test

The Autodesk Fusion tutorial is the week's worked procedure. These are the numbers and settings it commits to, and the checks that catch the common mistakes.

Sketch

Create sketch → choose the bottom plane → two-point rectangle from the axis intersection (R) → type dimensions and Tab between them → edit later with Sketch Dimension (D).

Offset

Offset (O) with a value of 2 builds the wall boundary; if the red line lands outside the blue rectangle, press Flip. Then Finish Sketch and Orbit to check the profile in 3D.

Extrude

Base: inner profile, distance +2 mm. Walls: outer offset profile, distance greater than the base (about +3 mm) with the Join operation, never New Body.

Text and export

New sketch on the base → Text tool → extrude with a negative distance and the Cut operation. Then right-click Body1 → Save as Mesh → STL (Binary), which is preferred over ASCII because the file is smaller.

Worked answer — selecting a process for three briefs. Answer each in two sentences with the checklist from this page.

1

“I need to feel ten different grip shapes tomorrow.” The question is about form in the hand, so it is a looks-like study. Foam core or polyurethane foam, cut by hand — the Duo case study's route — because ten variants cost an afternoon and no machine booking. Failure mode: foam cannot tell you how the electronics will fit.

2

“I need a working sensor node I can leave in a garden for a month.” This is a works-like prototype that also has to survive weather, so the question is functionality plus enclosure. FDM in a suitable filament for the housing, because 3D printing is the most versatile route for a one-off enclosure with cable exits, and the electronics are the point. Post-process and seal the part; layer lines are pathways for water.

3

“I need 200 identical translucent covers for a user study.” Quantity plus a finish printing cannot reach. Resin casting from a printed, sanded master: cheap to tool with a silicone mould and suitable for small production runs — and if the study grows into a product, the same master feeds an injection mould, where the tooling cost is finally amortised.

4

The sentence that earns the mark. “The process follows from the question and the quantity: a looks-like grip study is hand-cut foam because it is cheap and fast; a one-off functional enclosure is printed because printing is the most versatile route; 200 identical covers are cast because tooling is affordable at that volume and the finish is unreachable by printing.”
