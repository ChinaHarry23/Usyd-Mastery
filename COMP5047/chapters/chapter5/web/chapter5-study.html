What Digital Fabrication Is

Week 5 opens with a one-line definition and then spends the rest of the lecture showing what that definition unlocks for an interaction designer.

Definition — digital fabrication

A manufacturing process in which the machine is controlled by a computer. The design lives as a digital model, and the machine that makes the part executes instructions derived from that model rather than being guided by hand.

What the definition excludes

Hand-cutting foam core with an X-Acto knife is fabrication, but it is not digital fabrication: no computer controls the cut. The moment the same outline is exported to a laser cutter, the file — not the hand — defines the geometry.

Why an HCI unit teaches it

Because the artefacts of pervasive computing are physical. If you cannot build the enclosure, mount the sensor and place the device in a room, the interaction design never gets tested. The deck names three moves: building new devices, augmenting existing devices, and transforming them.

The unit's own toolchain: the Week 5 deck advertises two capabilities the course supports directly — 3D printing support and laser cutting support. Those are the two processes you are expected to be able to prepare files for.

Why prototype at all? The deck's four reasons

Exploration

  • A fast and cheap way of experiencing devices in a physical and playful way
  • You learn by holding the thing, not by imagining it

User testing

  • Formative feedback from your stakeholders during the design process
  • Feedback arrives while it can still change the design

Communication

  • A way of presenting your ideas to stakeholders, engineers and designers
  • A shared object ends an argument that a sketch cannot

Validation

  • Design verification, technical performance testing, safety standards testing
  • Evidence that a claim about the device is true

Exam framing: digital fabrication is not a synonym for 3D printing. It is any computer-controlled making process, which is why the same week covers laser cutters, vinyl cutters, water jets, moulding and hand-cut foam core. Choosing among them is a design decision, not a shopping decision.
A team cuts an enclosure outline out of foam core with a knife. Why does the Week 5 deck treat this as part of the digital-fabrication story rather than outside it?
Because foam core is a 3D-printing material
Because fabrication techniques are complementary and are chosen by what the prototype needs to learn
Because a computer was used to draw the outline first, so any cut counts as digital fabrication

Worked example — turning a design question into a fabrication route. The brief is a pair of handheld gaming controllers for iOS devices that must be comfortable in the hand, look good, establish a brand identity, complement the polish of Apple products, and work cognitively and effectively as gaming devices (the Duo case study). Work through it:

1

Separate the questions. “Comfortable in the hand” and “works as a gaming device” are different questions with different evidence. No single prototype answers both, so the route has to be staged.

2

Start with cheap form. Sketches, then a paper template, then polyurethane foam models — the deck's own sequence for Duo. Foam is fast, full size and sandable, so ten grip shapes cost an afternoon rather than a week of printing.

3

Measure behaviour, not opinion. Duo used blind testing and 3D scans to tune ergonomics, plus colour studies and iPhone mount studies for the visual and mechanical questions. Each study isolates one variable.

4

Escalate fidelity only where the question needs it. A works-like prototype needs electronics in a shell you can hold for an hour; a looks-like prototype needs the correct surface, colour and proportions. They are not the same object.

5

Finish with a production tool. Once the shape is settled, a resin-cast silicone mould or an injection mould produces many identical parts with the finish a prototype cannot reach. The deck's chain from sketch to production tool is exactly this escalation.

The markable sentence: the prototype is chosen by the question it has to answer, and the cheapest process that answers it wins.

“Looks Like” vs “Works Like”

The deck's central design idea for this week: before choosing a machine, decide what you want the prototype to teach you. Two prototype types, two different sets of questions.

Two prototype types

“Looks like” — a prototype that explores appearance: it evokes the right visual semantics, explores proportions and shape, and enables study of how the object sits in its context. “Works like” — a prototype that explores function and physical behaviour: what the interaction feels like, how heavy the device is, whether the mechanism works.

The lecture makes the distinction concrete with the Candela LUAU lamp: a lamp that can be used both indoors and outdoors. The same product generates two different prototypes with two different jobs.

Third questionDoes the mechanism survive repeated use?How does it sit on the charger?

Case study: Kurio

Brief: how can technology enhance interaction among family members in a museum setting? The team built exploratory prototypes in foam core and Styrofoam (polystyrene foam, XPS) from sketches and 2D designs, tested non-functional prototypes in a fake museum, and only then produced 3D concept models based on metaphors — a “wand” to point, a “magnifying glass” to capture text, a “radio” to collect sounds, a “dowsing rod” for wayfinding. Working prototypes with a 3D printed enclosure came last, followed by field testing.

Case study: Duo gaming controllers

The brief demanded comfort, looks, brand identity and cognitive effectiveness at once. The team answered it in layers: sketches, a paper template, polyurethane foam models, blind testing and ergonomic tuning, 3D scans, colour studies, iPhone mount studies — and only then a 3D printed enclosure with the electronics, followed by a production tool.

The failure mode to name in an exam: building the high-fidelity object first. A works-like prototype that also has to look right costs weeks, and when the mechanism turns out to be wrong the beautiful shell is scrap. Fidelity is a budget, and it should be spent on the question you cannot answer any other way.
You need to know whether people can tell that the lamp is charging when it is placed on its base. Which prototype answers that question most cheaply?
A full works-like prototype with working electronics and a dimming mechanism
A full-scale looks-like model in foam core or XPS that sits on the charger the same way
A CAD model rendered in the modelling software

Additive, Subtractive, Forming

The deck's taxonomy of fabrication types. Every machine in the rest of the lecture belongs to one of these three families, and each family has an older, analogue ancestor.

FamilyHow material is handledDigital examples from the deckAnalogue ancestor
AdditiveMaterial is deposited to build the object up3D printing: FDM, binder jetting, stereolithography, digital light processingClay sculpting
SubtractiveMaterial is removed from a solid blockCNC (router, mill, lathe), vinyl cutting, water jetting, laser cuttingCarving
FormingMaterial is poured, injected or pressed into a mouldInjection moulding, resin castingCasting
Read the family first, then the machine. Almost every exam question about a fabrication process can be answered from the family: additive parts are built up (supports, layer lines, slower for volume), subtractive parts are carved out (material waste, tool reach, fast on sheet), formed parts are copies of a cavity (tooling cost, surface finish, production volume).

Subtractive Manufacturing

Create an object by removing material from a solid block of material. The deck lists five machine types, and the exam is usually about which one suits which job.

MachineWhat the computer controlsGood forWatch out for
CNC mill / routerA rotating cutter that drills or cuts through material along a toolpathFlat panels, pockets, 2.5D and 3D forms in virtually any materialTool reach and fixturing; material waste
CNC latheThe rotation of the stock and the position of the cutting toolRound, axially symmetric partsOnly round geometry; setups are job-specific
Vinyl cutterA sharp blade dragged through sheet materialStencils, car wraps, t-shirts, stickers, thin cardboardSheet material only; the blade cannot cut thick stock
Water jetA high-pressure stream of water along a cutting pathThick, heat-sensitive material cut without a heat-affected zoneBest on water-resistant materials to avoid damage; avoid warping from high temperatures
Laser cutterA focused beam that burns through or engraves the materialPlywood, acrylic and cardboard sheet — the workhorse of this unitMaterial safety, thickness limits and kerf, covered in the next section
CNC

Computer Numerical Control — the machine executes a numbered sequence of coordinates rather than being steered by hand. That is what lets a design be cut straight from a CAD file and reproduced consistently.

Pros (from the deck)

  • Can be used on virtually any material
  • Designs can be cut straight from CAD files
  • Highly adaptable for cutting intricate and complex shapes consistently

Cons (from the deck)

  • Not suited to high-volume production
  • Can be slow
  • Machines are expensive
  • Material waste — you start from a block and throw away the difference
Why subtractive processes dominate prototyping: sheet stock is cheap, the cut is fast, and a laser-cut part is flat and accurate in the plane that matters for enclosures and panels. The cost is that the geometry is fundamentally 2D sheet with joints — which is exactly why joinery gets its own section.
A team needs 40 identical 8 mm-thick acrylic side panels for a study rig, with holes and engraved labels. Which process does the deck's material point at?
CNC mill, because subtractive processes cannot cut thin sheet
Laser cutter, which the deck lists as suited to acrylic, and which can cut and engrave in the same job
Water jet, because water never damages plastic

Laser Cutting in Practice

The laser cutter cuts or engraves material by burning through it with a laser. Most of the skill is not in the machine — it is in the file you bring to it and the tolerances you designed for.

Preparing files in Illustrator

StepSettingWhy it matters
Document setupSet units to mmEvery dimension in the machine's world is metric; mixed units are the classic source of parts that do not fit

Colour codingUse different colours for different goalsThe operator's software maps each colour to an operation (cut, vector engrave, raster engrave)

Things to keep in mind

Engraving vs cutting

  • Engraving marks the surface; cutting separates the part
  • They are different passes over the same lines, so they must be different colours

Material shifting on the laser bed

  • If the stock moves mid-job, the second pass misses the first
  • Nesting several parts makes a shift cost the whole sheet

Thickness and tolerance

  • Consider the material thickness and tolerance when designing for assembly
  • Nominal 3 mm acrylic is not 3.000 mm, so joints need clearance

Test on cheaper material

  • Test on cardboard or plywood before acrylic
  • The test costs minutes; the ruined acrylic costs the sheet

You drew a panel outline in Illustrator and the job came back with no cut at all — only the engraving. What is the most likely cause?
The cutting outline is not a 0.01 pt single closed path, so pressing Ctrl+Y would not show it
The document was set to RGB instead of CMYK
The part was larger than the 600 × 900 mm bed

Worked example — preparing the base and two walls of an Arduino enclosure for the laser cutter. The enclosure is 70 × 55 mm inside (the board plus the tutorial's 1.00 mm tolerance each side), from nominal 3 mm acrylic.

1

Fix the units and the colour mode first. mm and RGB. If the document is in points or pixels, every dimension you typed is a guess, and the wall will not meet the base.

2

Separate the goals into layers and colours. Annotations (not cut), exterior cuts (the outline that frees the part), interior cuts (vent slots, cable hole, mounting holes), vector engraving (the label), bitmap engraving (a photo or logo, single colour with diffusion dither).

3

Design the joints with the real thickness. The finger joint that holds wall to base is cut from the measured sheet thickness, not the nominal one, and the laser removes a kerf of material on every path. Cut a test comb in cardboard, measure the fit, and adjust the slot width before touching acrylic.

4

Check every shape with Ctrl+Y. If a path does not appear in the outline preview it will not be cut, no matter how it looks on screen. This one check catches most failed jobs.

5

Nest for the bed and the budget. Fit the parts inside the machine's bed (600 × 900 mm on the Epilog Fusion M2 named in the deck), leave a margin so a shifting sheet does not ruin a neighbour, and cut interiors before exteriors so the part does not move once it is free.

6

Dry-fit, then glue. Assemble with no adhesive first. Acrylic cement is unforgiving — the deck marks it “caution!” — whereas wood glue has many types and is more tolerant. Only glue once the geometry is proven.

Adhesives, Joinery and Assembly

A laser cutter can only make flat parts. Everything that turns flat parts into a device is joinery, and the deck gives you a short catalogue of joints plus two adhesives.

Acrylic cement

Dissolves and welds acrylic surfaces together, giving a strong, nearly invisible joint. The deck flags it with “caution!”: it is a solvent, it wicks into gaps, it damages surfaces it touches, and it gives no second chance to reposition a part.

Wood glue

Comes in many types (PVA, polyurethane, cyanoacrylate for quick tacking). More forgiving, slower to set, easier to clean up — and the right choice when you expect to dry-fit, adjust and re-fit before committing.

The deck's joint catalogue

Finger joint — interlocking rectangular fingers that give a large glue area and self-aligning assembly at a right angle. Mortise (hole) and tenon — a tab that fits a matching hole, locating two panels precisely. Captive nut — a nut trapped in a laser-cut pocket so a bolt can be tightened without holding it. Plus engraving, hinges and layer stacking, where many cut sheets are stacked to build up a 3D volume.

The tolerance lesson: laser cutting looks exact because the machine is exact, but the stock is not. Nominal 3 mm acrylic varies sheet to sheet, and the laser removes a kerf on every path. Every joint in this catalogue is therefore a fit problem before it is a geometry problem — which is why the deck tells you to test on cheap material first.

Foam Core: When You Do Not Need a Laser Cutter

The deck's blunt reminder: maybe you don't need a laser cutter. Foam core, a ruler and a knife produce a full-size looks-like model in an afternoon, and both case studies in the lecture start there.

Where foam core appears in the lecture: the Kurio team built exploratory prototypes in foam core and Styrofoam (polystyrene foam, XPS) from sketches and 2D designs; the Duo team moved from sketches to a paper template to polyurethane foam models before any digital machine was involved.
ToolWhat it is for
T-squareGuarantees that a line is actually perpendicular to the sheet edge — the cheapest accuracy you can buy
Metal rulerA straight edge that a knife cannot shave into, so the cut stays true after hundreds of passes
Box cutterHeavy cuts and thick stock; the blade can be extended for deep foam
X-Acto knifeFine detail, tight corners and clean openings in thin foam core
Cutting matProtects the bench, keeps the sheet from sliding, and preserves the blade edge
Hot glue gunFast, gap-filling joins. Sets in seconds, which is why it suits mock-ups rather than finished models
White glueSlower, cleaner joins that can be sanded; the choice when the seam will be visible
Push pinsHold a joint while the adhesive cures, and can be removed without marking the surface

What foam core is good at

  • Full-size form and proportion studies in hours, not days
  • Layout and placement questions: does this fit the room, the hand, the shelf?
  • Communicating an idea to stakeholders who cannot read a CAD model
  • Testing a metaphor: the Kurio “wand”, “magnifying glass”, “radio” and “dowsing rod” were all concept models before they were products

What it cannot do

  • Carry load or hold a thread — it is not a structural material
  • Survive repeated handling, so it is not a works-like body
  • Reproduce the surface, colour or weight of the production material
  • Answer any question about electronics, mechanism or safety
Choosing by cost of being wrong: a foam model that turns out to be the wrong shape costs an afternoon; a 3D printed enclosure that turns out to be the wrong shape costs a print queue, filament and a week. That asymmetry, not the fidelity of the machine, is what should decide where you start.

Additive Manufacturing

Create an object by depositing material. The deck lists four additive processes, then compares the three you are most likely to use on materials, layer height, supports, post-processing and cost.

The three processes to know

FDM — Fused Deposition Modelling: thermoplastic filament is melted and extruded through a heated nozzle. SLA — Stereolithography: a laser cures liquid resin into solid plastic. From CAD to G-code

The digital half of digital fabrication: a model is designed, repaired, sliced and finally executed. Every machine in this week consumes the output of this chain.

CAD and CAM

CAD — Computer-Aided Design: producing the digital model. CAM — Computer-Aided Manufacturing: converting that model into machine instructions. The handover format is a mesh file — the deck lists .stl, .obj, .dae, .x3d, .wrl and .ply — and the output is G-code.

StageWho does itFailure if skipped
DesignCAD softwareNo model, no part — and hand-modelling the part twice wastes the advantage of the digital file
ExportCAD softwareA proprietary format the slicer cannot open, or a mesh with the wrong scale (mm vs inches)
Check & repairMesh tools or the slicer's repair modeLayers with holes, missing walls, or a slicer that guesses the inside of the part wrongly
SliceCAM / slicer (Cura in the tutorial)Wrong supports, impossible overhangs, or a print time that does not fit the booking
Set upYou, at the machineFailed first layer, warping, or a print that detaches mid-job
PrintThe machineFailures are normal: the tutorial tells you to allow enough time for more than one attempt
FinishYou, by handSupport scars and layer lines on a part the study was supposed to evaluate for look and feel

Three mesh defects to recognise

Boundary edges

An edge with a face on only one side — a hole in the surface. The slicer cannot tell what is solid there.

Intersecting faces

Two surfaces passing through each other, usually from an unintended overlap or a boolean that failed quietly.

Non-manifold edges

An edge shared by more than two faces, which has no consistent inside or outside and breaks the assumption every slicer makes.

Course logistics named in the tutorial: the library's ThinkSpace and CreateSpace 3D printers are free for staff and students but must be booked in advance — bookings at least 24 hours ahead, limited to 4 hours (overnight only as the final print of the day), a limit of 2 bookings per equipment per week, and all overnight prints started before 4 pm on weekdays. Arrive on time: a booking can be cancelled if you are more than 15 minutes late. Prints fail, so schedule more than one attempt.
In the Autodesk Fusion tutorial, why must the wall extrusion use the Join operation rather than New Body?
Because STL files can only contain a single body
Because Join merges the new extrusion with the existing base so the enclosure prints as one piece
Because a negative extrude distance is only allowed with Join

Worked example — from an Arduino Uno measurement to a printable enclosure (the Week 5 tutorial).

1

Measure the real object. Take the Arduino Uno's dimensions with a scale or callipers. The tutorial designs around the board rather than around a datasheet, because the enclosure must fit the object in your hand.

2

Add the tolerance before you draw. Set an error tolerance of 1.00 mm each side — add “+1” to each dimension — so the board drops in instead of jamming. This is the design decision that the rest of the build inherits.

3

Sketch on a plane. Create a sketch, choose the bottom plane, and draw a two-point rectangle from the axis intersection with the measured-plus-tolerance dimensions (press R for the rectangle tool, D to edit a dimension). Then use Offset (O) with a value of 2 to create the outer boundary — if the red offset line falls outside the blue rectangle, press Flip.

4

Extrude the base, then the walls. Finish the sketch, then extrude the inner profile by 2 mm for the base (positive, not −2: the sign is direction). Now extrude the outer offset profile with Join for the walls — the wall height must exceed the 2 mm base, and the tutorial uses about 3 mm more.

5

Cut the text with a negative extrude. Create a second sketch on the base, add the Text tool, type the label, then extrude with a negative distance and the Cut operation so the arrow points into the model. Engraving the name is a cut, not a separate body.

6

Export and slice. Right-click Body1 in the browser and choose Save as Mesh, saving as STL (Binary) — binary is preferred over ASCII for 3D printing because the file is smaller. Import into Cura, rotate or resize, choose settings that suit the purpose and shape of the object, then slice into layers. Preview mode shows the layers and the estimated time, which may run from an hour to days.

7

Book, print, iterate. Book the printer well in advance, bring the STL on a USB drive, and start the job. The tutorial's own warning is the right note to end on: iterations are key to 3D modelling — your first model may be a total waste and still a good learning. Turn on the design history and automatic backup so you can edit a sketch or feature instead of rebuilding it.

Moulding and Casting

The forming family. The deck contrasts one industrial process with one workshop process, and the difference is entirely about tooling cost and production volume.

Injection mouldingResin casting
MechanismTakes granular pellets of plastic, melts them and injects them at high pressure into a mouldPlastic resin plus hardener are poured into a mould and hardened
Cost structureHigh upfront costs, low cost per partCheap, and suitable for small production runs
VolumeTypically used for large volumesPrototype and short-run quantities
Mould materialMachined metal toolingSilicone mould cast from a master
Where it sits in the projectThe production tool at the end of the lifecycleThe step between a 3D printed prototype and production
The single most important sentence in this section: all problems in the master are transferred to the moulded parts. The master is usually 3D printed, so layer lines, support scars and sanding marks are copied into every single casting. Sand and polish the master until it is better than the part you want.

Why the mould needs a vent and a fill port

Resin has to get in and air has to get out. Pour through the fill port and let displaced air escape through the vent; without one, the cavity traps bubbles and every casting inherits them at the same place.

Worked example in the deck: Spinal Log

The lecture's moulding example is a resin-cast object — the same six steps, from a printed and polished master through a silicone mould to a cured, post-processed part. It is the bridge from a one-off prototype to something you can hand out.
