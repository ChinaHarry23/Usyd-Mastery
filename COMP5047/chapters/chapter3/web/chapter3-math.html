Foundations: the arithmetic and the timing behind digital I/O

Week 3 is the week the unit becomes quantitative. This page collects the definitions that have to be word-perfect, the three rearrangements of Ohm's law that size every resistor in the kit, the datasheet limits that decide whether a circuit is safe, and a timing model that turns “debounce the button” into a number you can defend.

Definitions that must be exact

Marks in this unit are lost on vocabulary more often than on arithmetic. Each of these has to be written in one sentence, with the consequence included.

Digital signal and logic level

A voltage interpreted as one of exactly two discrete states. A logic level is the state itself — HIGH or LOW — and the receiver decides which one it is by comparing the measured voltage against threshold bands rather than against an exact value.

GPIO

General-purpose input/output: a pin whose direction and behaviour are chosen by software rather than fixed by the circuit. Its direction is assigned with pinMode(), and nothing about it is predictable until that has happened.

Pull-up and pull-down resistor

A resistor that holds an input at a defined level when nothing else is driving it: a pull-up towards the supply rail (idle HIGH), a pull-down towards ground (idle LOW). It exists to stop the pin floating, and it is small enough (typically 10 kΩ) to be overridden by the switch without wasting current.

Current limiting

Placing a resistor in series with a component so that the current through it is fixed by Ohm's law rather than by the source's ability to deliver. An LED has no useful internal resistance, so without a series resistor the current is limited only by the wiring — effectively a short circuit.

Contact bounce and debouncing

Bounce is the rapid, unwanted opening and closing of a mechanical switch's contacts for a few milliseconds after it is operated. Debouncing is any electrical or software technique that makes the firmware see exactly one transition per press.

Active-low

A signal whose asserted state is the low voltage. A button wired with a pull-up is active-low: the pin is HIGH while released and LOW while pressed, so the firmware condition must be inverted — the deck's “we must invert the behaviour”.

Ohm's law, notation and the series resistor

One equation does all the work in this chapter. Learn it in three arrangements and every component value in the kit becomes derivable.

1. Ohm's law in three arrangements

\[ V = R \times I \qquad I = \frac{V}{R} \qquad R = \frac{V}{I} \]

Units are volts, amperes and ohms. Milliamps are 10−3 A, so 9 mA is 0.009 A in every substitution — the single most common arithmetic error in this week's tutorial.

2. The series LED branch

A conducting LED fixes the voltage across itself at its forward voltage Vf. The supply voltage is shared out along the loop, so the series resistor must take the remainder:

\[ V_R = V_{CC} - V_f \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R_{series} = \frac{V_{CC} - V_f}{I_f} \]

Two consequences worth stating explicitly. If VCC < Vf there is no solution with a positive current: the diode never becomes forward-biased and the LED stays dark, which is exactly what the deck shows for a 1.5 V cell. And if Rseries is rounded down to a standard value, the current rises — so always round up.

3. Power in the resistor

\[ P = V \times I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R} \]

Power is the rate at which the resistor turns electrical energy into heat. It decides which package you need, not whether the circuit works — and, as the next section shows, it is usually the least demanding constraint in a breadboard circuit.

Worked example — the deck's own numbers

(a) A 3 V supply and a red LED dropping 2.2 V: VR = 3.0 − 2.2 = 0.8 V. With R = 220 Ω, I = 0.8 / 220 = 3.6 mA — a sensible visible current.

(b) The same LED on a 5 V pin with Vf = 2.0 V: VR = 3.0 V. With R = 330 Ω, I = 3.0 / 330 = 9.1 mA, and the resistor dissipates P = 3.0 × 0.0091 = 27 mW.

(c) A 1.5 V cell and a red LED with Vf = 2.2 V: the required VR is negative, so no current flows at all. The LED's forward voltage has not been reached.

Worked problem — sizing a resistor and checking that it survives. A blue LED has Vf = 3.0 V and a rated forward current of 20 mA. You want to run it at 10 mA from a 5 V pin, using the 1/4 W resistors in the kit.

1

Voltage left for the resistor. VR = 5.0 − 3.0 = 2.0 V. Note how little headroom a blue LED leaves on a 5 V rail — the same circuit on a 3.3 V board would not light it at all.

2

Exact resistance. R = VR / If = 2.0 / 0.010 = 200 Ω. Convert milliamps to amperes before dividing.

3

Choose a real resistor. 200 Ω is not in the E12 series, so take the next value up: 220 Ω. The actual current becomes I = 2.0 / 220 = 9.1 mA, about 9 % below target and well under the 20 mA rating.

4

Check the power. P = VR × I = 2.0 × 0.0091 = 18 mW. A 1/4 W resistor is rated 250 mW, so it is running at about 7 % of its rating — no warmth, no derating needed.

5

Check the pin. 9.1 mA is below the 20 mA recommended per-pin current and far below the 40 mA absolute maximum. Had the calculation produced 30 mA, the resistor would have been fine but the pin would not — the constraint that binds first is the GPIO, not the resistor.

Current, power and datasheet limits

A calculation tells you what value to use. The datasheet tells you whether the circuit is allowed to exist. Both have to be checked, and they are not the same check.

LimitValue on a 5 V ATmega328PWhat happens when you exceed it
Current per pin, recommended20 mAThe pin still works, but the output voltage sags away from 5 V and the part runs hot
Current per pin, absolute maximum40 mAPermanent damage. “Absolute maximum” is a destruction limit, never a design target.
Total current through VCC and GND200 mASeveral pins each within their own limit can still exceed the chip's total
LED forward current, typical20 mA continuousBrightness falls, then the junction fails — and without a series resistor this takes milliseconds
Resistor power rating, typical kit part1/4 W (250 mW)Overheating, drifting resistance, eventually an open circuit

It is worth doing the comparison explicitly, because it tells you where to spend your attention. For a 5 V rail and an LED with Vf = 2.0 V, the resistor always drops 3.0 V, so its dissipation is P = 3.0 × I while the pin supplies I.

Read the last row again. At 100 Ω the resistor is dissipating 90 mW — barely a third of its rating — while the GPIO pin is being asked for 30 mA, which is above the recommended 20 mA and uncomfortably close to the 40 mA destruction limit. On a microcontroller board the pin current is almost always the binding constraint, and the resistor's power rating almost never is. Sizing a resistor is not the same as sizing a circuit.
The two rules of current, restated arithmetically. Current divides between parallel branches in inverse proportion to resistance, so for two branches I1 / I2 = R2 / R1, and the branch currents sum to the total. And because all the energy must be used, the voltage drops around any closed loop sum to the supply — which is the same statement as VR = VCC − Vf.
A student drives four LEDs from four pins, each drawing a carefully calculated 19 mA. Every individual pin is inside its recommended limit. What should worry them?
The total current: 76 mA flows through the chip's V_CC and GND pins, and the 200 mA total limit is shared by every output
Nothing: 19 mA is below the 20 mA recommended limit, so the design is safe
The resistors will overheat, because four of them dissipate at once

Worked problem — current dividing between two branches. Two LEDs share one 5 V pin: a red one with Vf = 2.0 V behind a 330 Ω resistor, and a green one with Vf = 2.0 V behind a 220 Ω resistor. Both branches return to the same ground rail.

1

Treat each branch separately. The resistors are in parallel as far as the pin is concerned, so each branch sees the full 3.0 V across its own resistor. Branch 1: 3.0 / 330 = 9.1 mA. Branch 2: 3.0 / 220 = 13.6 mA.

2

Check the division rule. I1 / I2 = 9.1 / 13.6 = 0.67, and R2 / R1 = 220 / 330 = 0.67. The currents divide in inverse proportion to resistance, exactly as the deck states — the lower resistance carries the larger share.

3

Add them up. The pin now sources 9.1 + 13.6 = 22.7 mA. Each branch is individually modest, but the pin is over its recommended 20 mA — the classic mistake of checking the components and forgetting the driver.

4

Fix it properly. Drive the second LED from a different GPIO pin, or raise both resistors (470 Ω and 330 Ω give 6.4 + 9.1 = 15.5 mA). Never solve a current-budget problem by removing the resistors.

The debounce timing model

“Debounce the button” becomes a defensible design decision once you can put four durations in order.

The ordering that makes debouncing work

\[ T_{sample} \;\ll\; t_{bounce} \;\ll\; t_{debounce} \;\ll\; T_{press} \]

Each inequality is a separate claim, and each can fail on its own:

InequalityWhat it meansWhat breaks if it is violated
\(T_{sample} \ll t_{bounce}\)The loop runs far faster than the contacts chatterIf the loop were slower than the bounce, it would miss the chatter — debouncing would be unnecessary rather than wrong
\(t_{bounce} \ll t_{debounce}\)The window is longer than the whole burstBounce leaks through as extra events, and one press counts several times
\(t_{debounce} \ll T_{press}\)The window is far shorter than a human pressA genuine press is swallowed and the button feels broken or intermittent

How many spurious events a missing debounce produces

If the firmware samples every \(T_{sample}\) seconds and the contacts chatter for \(t_{bounce}\) seconds, the number of samples taken during the burst is

\[ N_{spurious} \approx \frac{t_{bounce}}{T_{sample}} \]

With \(T_{sample} = 50\ \mu s\) and \(t_{bounce} = 2\ ms\), that is \(N_{spurious} \approx 40\) samples — and every one of them is a chance for the program to act on a transition that never happened.

A sketch uses a 500 ms debounce window on a button that users press quickly. What is the likely complaint?
The counter still increments several times per press
Some presses do nothing at all, because the window outlasts the press
The pin floats while the window is open

Worked problem — choosing a window with evidence. A capacitive touch pad is substituted for the mechanical button. Its own datasheet specifies a response time of 25 ms and 200 µs of jitter, and users tap it for as little as 60 ms.

1

Re-read the failure modes. A capacitive pad has no contacts, so it does not bounce in the mechanical sense; what it has is a fixed response delay plus a small amount of jitter in when the new reading appears.

2

Substitute the numbers. The quantity that has to be swallowed is 200 µs of jitter, not 2 ms of bounce. The shortest press is 60 ms, and the pad already costs 25 ms of that.

3

Place the window. It must exceed the jitter with margin and stay well under 60 ms minus the sensor delay, so the usable band is roughly 2–20 ms. Choose 10 ms: five times the jitter, and the total delay of 35 ms is still inside the perceptual budget.

4

State the general lesson. The 5 ms figure for a tactile switch is not a constant to memorise; it is the output of the inequality \(T_{sample} \ll t_{noise} \ll t_{debounce} \ll T_{press}\) evaluated for that particular input device. Change the device and the window changes with it.

Recall and formula self-test

Everything on this page reduces to five formulas and a handful of constants. If you can produce them from memory, the tutorial calculations take seconds.

The five formulas

V = R I  ·  I = V/R  ·  R = V/I  ·  Rseries = (VCC − Vf)/If  ·  P = V I

The constants

Uno logic 5 V, ESP32 logic 3.3 V. Red LED Vf 1.8–2.2 V, blue/white about 3.0 V. Pin current 20 mA recommended, 40 mA maximum. Bounce 1–5 ms; debounce window 5–50 ms.

The units trap

Milliamps are 10−3 A. Writing I = 3.0 / 9 instead of 3.0 / 0.009 turns a 333 Ω resistor into a 0.33 Ω one — and a short circuit.

The direction of rounding

Always round the series resistance up to the next standard value, because a smaller resistance means a larger current. Down-rounding silently spends the LED's safety margin.
