The Vocabulary: Input, Output, Technique

Week 2 opens by reframing interaction as a dialogue. The computer perceives through input and answers through output; the user acts on the output and perceives the result. Every other term this week is a refinement of those four words.

The deck's opening diagram: perception sits opposite output, and action sits opposite input. It is one loop read from two sides — “how computers see us” is the input side, and the lecture's stated goal is to change it.

Five definitions to write exactly

Input — sensed information about the environment. Input device — a transducer that senses physical properties of people, places, or things. Output — any emission or modification of the physical environment. Interaction technique — input + output: all hardware and software elements that provide a way for the user to accomplish a low-level task. Low-level interaction task — the low-level primitive inputs required from the user.

TermWhat the definition commits you toWhat it rules out
Input deviceA transducer sensing physical properties of people, places or thingsSoftware that interprets readings; a camera sensing a room is still an input device
Interaction techniqueInput plus output, hardware plus software, aimed at one low-level taskThe bare device: a mouse with no mapping and no cursor is not yet a technique
Low-level taskA primitive input the user must supply (see the six below)Goals such as “book a flight”; goals are composed from primitives
OutputAny emission or modification of the physical environmentScreens only — light, sound, motion and heat are all output
Why the vocabulary is examinable: the definitions are what let you argue about devices without arguing about taste. “Which input device is best?” is unanswerable; “which transducer and transfer function best support this low-level task in this context?” is a question with evidence behind it.
Bill Buxton, quoted in the deck: “Everything is best for something and worst for something else… Without an equally solid understanding of both the strengths and weaknesses of the technology — when, where, why, how, for what, and for whom it is and isn't suitable — one is gambling rather than practicing design.” The deck pairs this with the competitive landscape of devices (one winner per task) and the cooperative landscape (devices combined to cover each other's weaknesses) — the Buxton Collection is the artefact catalogue behind the argument.
A shop counts passers-by with a passive infrared sensor mounted above the door. Which statement matches the lecture's definition of an input device?
The sensor is a transducer sensing a physical property of people and of a place
The counter display in the window is the input device, because it shows the number
Only devices a person deliberately touches count as input devices

The Six Low-Level Interaction Tasks

Interaction techniques are judged by the primitive inputs they let a user supply. The deck names six, and every technique in this unit can be described as a combination of them.

TaskLecture definitionWhere you meet it
SelectionChoosing from a set of alternativesTapping a menu item; dwelling your gaze on an icon
PositionSpecify a position within a rangePointing at a map; dragging a handle
OrientSpecify an angle or 3D orientationRotating a 3D object; tilting a phone to steer
PathSpecify a series of positions and orientations over timeInking a stroke; steering a cursor through a pull-down menu
QuantifySpecify a numeric valueTurning a dial to 21 °C; typing a duration
TextEnter symbolic dataTyping a street address rather than pointing at it

The primitives are not all at the same level

Positioning an object in 2D is a single task with a mouse or a tablet. Change the transducer to a QWERTY keyboard and the same coordinates become two primitives: quantify X and quantify Y. The task did not change; the device did.

One gesture can collapse the sub-tasks

Buxton's phrasing argument: “Any concept or transaction that can be described in a single word or phrase should be able to be articulated by a single gesture.” When the sub-tasks disappear from the user's model, novices start to perform like experts.

Worked example — the drawing competition. The deck sets Etch-a-Sketch against Skedoodle and asks how you can always win. The answer given is a design lesson: let your opponent pick the device while you pick the drawing.

1

Name the low-level task. Drawing a picture is a path task — a series of positions over time — built out of continuous position input.

2

Decompose the Etch-a-Sketch. Two dials, each a one-dimensional control: quantify X gives 1D position and quantify Y gives 1D position. Diagonal and curved strokes require the two hands' worth of coordination from one pair of fingers, so the path is expressed through two coupled quantities rather than through pointing.

3

Decompose a direct pointing device. A stylus on a tablet senses position in two dimensions, so the same path is one position task articulated by one gesture. The primitives have collapsed into a chunk the user can perform automatically.

4

Draw the conclusion the deck wants. Neither device is better in the abstract. Choose the drawing whose structure matches the device you were given — mostly straight horizontal and vertical lines for the dial device, free curves for the pointing device. That is Buxton's point: strengths and weaknesses, not rankings.

A user turns a rotary dial until the display reads 21 °C. Which low-level task is being performed?
Orient — the dial rotates through an angle
Quantify — the user is specifying a numeric value
Position — the dial has a physical position in its range

Properties of Input Devices

Knowing that a device is a transducer tells you nothing about what it is good for. Four properties do most of the work: what property it senses, how many dimensions it senses, whether it is direct or indirect, and whether it is absolute or relative.

Property sensed

Most devices sense linear position, motion or force; rotary devices sense angle, change in angle and torque. The property sensed is not a detail — it determines which transfer function is appropriate, and therefore what the device can control well.

Property sensedDevicesWhat follows from it
Position (absolute)Stylus or graphics tablet, touchscreenA fixed mapping from surface to display, so no cursor is needed; a physical intermediary can disagree with the software's value (the nulling problem)
Motion (relative)Mouse, trackpad, trackballThe cursor makes position visible and can be decoupled from the device; limited physical space forces clutching
ForceIsometric joystick (for example the TrackPoint)Rate control: force maps to cursor velocity, and the stick self-centres, so releasing it stops the motion
Angle / torqueRotary knob or dialOne angular dimension; the same form factor can be isotonic, isometric or elastic depending on resistance

Direct vs indirect

A direct device has a unified input and display surface — a touchscreen, or a display tablet operated with a pen. A mouse is indirect: you move it on the desk to indicate a point on the screen. Direct is not automatically easier. Direct devices have no buttons to carry state transitions, and the finger or pen occludes the very thing being pointed at.

Absolute vs relative

A position-sensing device is absolute; a motion-sensing device is relative. Relative devices cannot disagree with the software, but they need visual feedback and they run out of desk: the user lifts and repositions the device, which is clutching. Absolute devices have no such problem but can be silently out of step with the system's value.

Dimensions are a design decision, not a specification. A mouse senses two dimensions; a knob senses one angular dimension; a six-degree-of-freedom tracker senses three position plus three orientation dimensions. Pairing a mouse with a scroll wheel produces a multi-channel 2D+1D device: two separate input dimensions that the user can address independently.
Watch the wording: “absolute” and “direct” are different axes. A stylus tablet is absolute but indirect (the display is elsewhere); a touchscreen is both absolute and direct; a mouse is relative and indirect. Exam answers lose marks by treating these as synonyms.
A tablet has been used to set a value, then the value is changed in software. The pen now sits at the top-left of the tablet while the indicator sits in the middle of the screen. What does this illustrate?
The nulling problem: an absolute device has no way to resynchronise with a value changed in software
The tablet is behaving as a relative device, so the cursor drifts while the pen is still
The transfer function has not been defined, so no mapping exists from pen to screen

Transfer Functions, Gain, Modes and Chunking

Between the sensor and the tasks sits a mapping. Get the mapping wrong and the same hardware feels broken; get it right and the device stops being noticeable.

Transfer function

The mapping from device input to functionality in indirect interfaces. An appropriate mapping matches the property sensed: position to position for a tablet in absolute mode, velocity to velocity for a mouse, force to velocity for an isometric joystick. A common inappropriate mapping is computing a velocity from the cursor's screen position — for example to scroll a document — which is hard to control and is only necessary because the cursor is clipped to the screen edge. For self-centring devices, a nonlinear rate mapping of the form \(dx = K x^{\alpha}\) is used, with \(K\) and \(\alpha\) found experimentally.

Control-display gain

The deck's equation: CD gain = Vcursor / Vdevice — how far the cursor travels per unit of device movement. Gain is also called the C:D ratio, and it is a rough metric because it conflates device size and display size into one number. In experiments, gain typically has little effect on the time to complete pointing movements; variable-gain functions mainly buy a smaller physical footprint.

Resistance: isotonic, isometric, elastic

Isotonic — free movement, no resistance. Isometric — no movement; resistance as we press. Elastic — resistance changes with movement. Acquisition time (also called homing time) is the average time to pick up or put down an input device — the hidden cost of switching between keyboard and mouse.

Mode and chunking

Mode — a context where user actions (keypresses, mouse clicks etc.) are treated in a specific way. Chunking — the integration of multiple degrees of freedom to support higher-level tasks.

Why modes are not automatically bad

The deck's own example is the pop-up menu: pressing the button, navigating to the item and releasing are three sub-tasks glued together by the tension of holding the button down. The held button is a temporary mode, and it gives constant physical feedback that you are in it. The conclusion: design phrasing so that the concluding action is the natural consequence of the initial one.

Why chunking is the goal

Novices attend to low-level details; experts perform them automatically and think in bigger chunks. Chunking is how a device with many degrees of freedom becomes usable: if position, orientation and scale are integrated into one grasp, the user thinks “place the object here” rather than solving six control problems.

A designer gives a force-sensing isometric joystick a position-to-position transfer function. What is the consequence?
Nothing measurable — transfer functions only change the feel of the device
The mapping contradicts the property sensed: a self-centring force device needs a force-to-velocity rate mapping
The device will begin reporting absolute screen coordinates instead of forces

Device States: Turning a Signal into Events

A continuous signal is not yet an interface. What makes a device usable is its state machine: which states exist, and which transitions produce events the system can act on.

DeviceStates in the deck's diagramTransitions
Mouse0: button up  |  1: button down (dragging)Button down enters dragging; button up leaves it. Two states are enough because the device never leaves the desk.
TouchOut of range  |  0: touch  |  1: trackingTouch begins a contact; release ends it; leaving sensing range is a third condition the system must handle.
StylusOut of range  |  0: stylus hover  |  1: tip touches (tracking)  |  2: draggingLift stylus and tip releases are separate transitions, which is what makes hover previews possible.
Read a state machine as a design tool, not as firmware. Every state is a promise about what the system can distinguish. The mouse can tell “moving” from “dragging” but cannot tell “the user is thinking” from “the user is idle”; the stylus can tell hover from contact, which is what lets an interface preview a target before the user commits to it.

Implicit Interaction and Intentionality

Everything so far assumed the user approaches the system with a clear intention. The pervasive turn is to treat actions that were never aimed at the system as input anyway — and then to be careful about it.

Implicit interaction — Albrecht Schmidt, quoted in the deck

“An action, performed by the user, that is not primarily aimed to interact with a computerized system, but which such a system understands as input.”

IntentionalityWhat it meansLighting example (deck)Curious Rituals example (deck)
IntentionalThe user acts in order to make the system do somethingYou press the light switchYou put make-up on and make a specific face to turn on the car
ExpectedActions we expect the user to carry out — the outcome is anticipated even though it was not commandedYou walk into the room knowing the lights will turn onYou look into the mirror and expect the car to turn on
IncidentalThe system reads an action the user never aimed at itYou walk into the room and, unbeknownst to you, the A/C increasesThe car turns on automatically when you take a seat

The cost of being wrong goes up

An interface that misreads an explicit command can be retried. An interface that misreads an incidental action has already acted on the world — the air conditioner is running, the door is unlocked, the diary entry exists. Incidental interaction needs a fallback and a way to see what the system believes.

The reward is a shorter dialogue

If the system can recognise the situation, the user stops issuing commands. The MediaCup needs no input at all: filling a mug is the interaction, and colleagues become aware that someone is taking a break.

Worked example — unlocking the door, explicitly then implicitly. The deck draws the door as a state machine with states 0, 1 and 2, transitions labelled hand recognised, gesture recognised, hand not recognised, no gesture, and a final state hand off, moving from LOCKED to UNLOCKED. It then asks: what is missing?

1

What the state machine does not say. It describes which states exist and which transitions fire, but not what the system is for. It never states the expected action, the desired outcome, or which phenomena are sensed. Without those three, the diagram cannot be evaluated.

2

Restate it as expected, desired, sensed. Expected: pull the handle to open the door. Desired: unlock when the right person pulls it. Sensed: a fingerprint sensor built into the handle. The interaction is now implicit: pulling a handle is an action aimed at the door, not at a computer.

3

Answer the deck's follow-up (“any problems?”). Three are immediate. A wrong inference lets the wrong person in or locks out the right one, and the failure is physical. Wet, gloved or injured hands break the sensing the user never agreed to rely on. And there is no visible mode: the user cannot tell whether the door is thinking, locked, or broken.

4

Design the recovery. Keep the explicit path as a fallback (a key, a code, a phone), make the door's state legible (a lock that audibly clicks), and make the sensing range match the expected action — the handle the user already grips — rather than adding a new surface to aim at.

A meeting room raises the blinds when the light sensor reads above 400 lx, whether or not anyone is in the room. Where does this sit on the intentionality spectrum?
Intentional — someone deliberately let the light in
Expected — the room's occupants anticipate the blinds opening
Incidental — the system acts on a phenomenon nobody aimed at it

Expected, Sensed, Desired

The checkable version of the last section: write down three sets and compare them before building anything. The mismatches are where the design work is.

The three sets (Benford et al., Week 2 reading)

Expected — actions we expect the user to carry out. Sensed — phenomena the system is able to measure. Desired — what we would like the application to do.

RelationshipWhat it looks likeDesign response
Expected ∩ SensedThe natural movement is measurableThis is ordinary interaction design: make the match as tight as possible in range, speed and accuracy.
Expected, not sensedA natural movement the sensors missDo not treat it as dead space. Lifting a mouse to re-centre is clutching; the same gap gives rest spaces, physical preparation and follow-through, and expressive latitude.
Sensed, not expectedSomething measurable that users would not naturally doUse it as error detection (a sensor fault, a device being misused) or as a deliberately hidden advanced mode.
Desired, but neitherThe application wants what the interface cannot yet ask forThe redesign signal: change the interface, change the sensors, or change the ambition.

MediaCup: an everyday object as a context source

The deck's MediaCup slide lists temperature, motion detection, communication with nearby cups, predictive machine learning and battery, and asks the three questions of any context-aware system: what to infer, how to react, and how it informs design. In the reading, the cup's sensors sample acceleration every 50 ms and temperature every 3 s, heuristics map those readings to cues — stationary, drinking, fiddling; filled up, cooled off, actual temperature — and the cues are broadcast over infrared roughly every 15 s. The purpose is not the drink: it is colleague awareness, which is exactly the kind of low-salience, incidental output the last section described.

Users naturally rest their hand on the table between strokes, and the sensor reports nothing at all while they do. Which relationship is this, and what should the designer do?
Sensed but not expected — treat the stillness as a sensor fault and raise a warning
Expected but not sensed — keep it unsensed and treat it as a rest space and a moment of preparation
Desired but neither expected nor sensed — change the application so the pause becomes the input

Context Recognition: What the System Has to Know

Incidental interaction only works if the system can recognise the situation it is in. The deck puts three definitions of context side by side and then maps which signals can be sensed and which have to be inferred.

Abowd — the minimal set

Who, what, where, when, why: a good minimal set of necessary context. Practical and easy to remember, and it maps directly onto the categories in the table below.

Dey — the operational definition

Any information that can be used to characterise the situation of an entity, the entity being any person, place or object relevant to the interaction. Dourish adds the counterweight: context is a relational property, defined dynamically rather than fixed in advance.

CategoryTypical signalsSensed or inferred
IdentityWho is present; who is actingBoth — sensed from a tag or device, inferred from a voice or gait
SpatialLocation, co-location, orientationSensed
TemporalClock time, duration, scheduleSensed (time) and inferred (schedule)
EnvironmentalLight, sound, temperatureSensed
SocialWho is with whom; a group activityInferred from proximity and co-presence
Resource availabilityNetwork bandwidth, power, storageSensed from the computing environment
ComputingAvailable screens, speakers, memorySensed
PhysiologicalHeart rate and other body signals — the 2002 Commonwealth Games baton flashed an electronic flame in time with its bearer's heart rateSensed
ActivityDriving, walking, drinking coffeeInferred — the MediaCup's acceleration cues are the textbook case
Chalmers' seven uses of context (the reading behind the deck's “using context” slide): context display, contextual augmentation, context-aware configuration, context-triggered actions, contextual mediation, contextual adaptation of the environment, and context-aware presentation. The last five are usually grouped as contextual adaptation. If an exam question asks what a system does with context, answer with the use, not with the sensor.
Task is not activity. A task is a practical goal — enjoying the museum, making deliveries. An activity is what the body is doing — walking, driving. Systems infer activities well and tasks badly, which is why “the system knows what I am doing” usually means “the system knows how I am moving”.
A system uses an accelerometer to decide that its wearer is walking, and dims notifications accordingly. Which pair of labels is correct?
The activity is sensed, and the dimming is context
The activity is inferred from sensed signals, and the dimming is a context-triggered action
Walking is the user's task, so the system is performing task recognition

Proxemic Interaction: Distance as Context

The deck closes by asking how proxemics could be used as contextual information. The reading's answer is a set of dimensions a system can measure continuously, and a set of zones that tell it how to behave.

Proxemics

Edward Hall's term (1966) for the ways people use interpersonal distance to mediate engagement and intimacy: closer distances lead to closer engagement. Proxemic interaction applies the same idea to the distance between people, devices and the fixed and semi-fixed features of a room.

Proxemic dimensionWhat it measuresDesign question it answers
DistanceHow far apart two entities are — continuous, or bucketed into zonesShould this surface show anything to this person at all?
OrientationFacing toward, somewhat toward or away; a yaw angle between objectsIs the person addressing this display, or merely standing near it?
MovementChange of distance and orientation over time, and speedAre they approaching, passing through, or leaving?
IdentityWho the entity is and what attributes they carryWhose media, notes or preferences belong on this surface?
LocationThe physical place, including fixed and semi-fixed featuresWhat does this room afford, and where are the thresholds?

Ambient: the outermost zone

At the far edge, the right output is barely an output: a display that can be read without being attended to, carrying information that is useful precisely because it does not interrupt. This is the calm-technology argument from Week 1, made measurable.

Explicit: the innermost zone

Close up, and only then, the surface becomes personal: it identifies you, reveals your content and accepts direct manipulation. The reading cites four interaction zones — ambient display at the outermost, explicit personal interaction at the innermost — so that behaviour changes with approach instead of with a login.

Why proximity is a good signal: it is continuous, it is already meaningful to people, and it does not require the user to issue anything. The design obligation that comes with it is the one from the ESD framework: decide what happens when the inference is wrong, and make sure the user can see which zone the system thinks they are in.
A lobby screen recognises a staff member's phone at 20 feet and immediately shows their personal calendar. Which proxemic mistake is being made?
None — once identity is known, distance stops mattering
It treats the outermost zone as if it were the innermost: personal content is shown at a public distance, to anyone nearby
It should have used movement instead of distance to decide what to show

Tutorial exercise — assumption storming a proxemic unlock. The Week 2 tutorial treats every design as a stack of assumptions and sorts them by type (problem, solution, implementation) and by risk against confidence. Worked pass on the idea: a door that unlocks as the right person approaches it.

1

Problem assumptions. That carrying keys or phones is a real burden; that people approach their own door in a predictable way; that the current lock is the thing making entry slow. Plausible, but each is a statement of fact without evidence yet.

2

Solution assumptions. That proximity is the right signal (rather than identity alone, or a deliberate gesture); that distance alone is enough or that orientation and movement are also needed; that an unlocked door which the person does not open is safe.

3

Implementation assumptions. That the sensing range can be tuned to the right zone; that the lock can be driven reliably from a battery; that a false accept is detectable after the fact; that the whole thing survives a Sydney summer on a north-facing door.

4

Place them on the risk-by-confidence grid. High risk and low confidence is where the research goes first: a false accept (someone the system mistakes for you) is high risk and the confidence in current sensing is low, so it is the first thing to test. Low risk and high confidence — that the door has a handle — can be assumed and ignored.

5

Turn the survivors into research questions. “At what distance do residents expect the door to unlock?” is a study with a method (observation at the door, interviews, a logged prototype) and a deliverable number. That is the tutorial's research plan: every objective paired with a method and a tool, and every assumption either tested or explicitly parked.

Week 2 Recap

A device is a transducer; a technique is input plus output

Input is sensed information about the environment; an input device senses physical properties of people, places or things; an interaction technique is everything that lets the user accomplish a low-level task.

Vocabulary

Six low-level tasks, and the device changes their shape

Selection, position, orient, path, quantify, text. Position is one task on a mouse and two primitives (quantify X, quantify Y) on a keyboard — the same task, a different transducer.

Decomposition

Properties, not rankings

Property sensed (position, motion, force, angle/torque), number of dimensions, direct vs indirect, absolute vs relative. The property sensed dictates the transfer function: position-to-position, velocity-to-velocity, force-to-velocity.

Device properties

Implicit interaction is a spectrum, and the cost of error is physical

Intentional, expected, incidental. Write the expected action, the desired outcome and the sensed phenomena, then compare the three sets: expected but not sensed is a resource, sensed but not expected is a warning or a hidden mode.
