Foundations: the design space of input devices

Week 2 is about choosing and shaping transducers, so this page does the job a formula sheet does elsewhere: it fixes the definitions the lecture relies on, turns the device vocabulary into axes you can plot a device on, gives you the models that quantify pointing (Fitts, Hick, the steering law, CD gain and rate mappings), and reduces expected / sensed / desired to set relationships you can write down in an exam.

Definitions verified against the lecture deck Models from the Week 2 readings Notation is a revision aid, not lecture notation

Definitions that must be exact

Marks in this unit are lost on definitions more often than on ideas. Write each of these as one sentence, with the consequence included.

Input, input device, output

Input is sensed information about the environment. An input device is a transducer that senses physical properties of people, places, or things. Output is any emission or modification of the physical environment.

Interaction technique

All hardware and software elements that provide a way for the user to accomplish a low-level task — the lecture writes it as input + output. A device on its own is not a technique: without a mapping and feedback there is nothing for the user to accomplish.

Mode

A context where user actions (keypresses, mouse clicks etc.) are treated in a specific way. The definition is about how input is interpreted, not about the system having internal state — every system has state.

Chunking

The integration of multiple degrees of freedom to support higher-level tasks. Chunking is how a many-degree-of-freedom device becomes usable: the user performs one task instead of solving one control problem per dimension.

Transfer function

The mapping from an input signal to a functionality in indirect interfaces. An appropriate mapping matches the property sensed: position-to-position (tablet), velocity-to-velocity (mouse), force-to-velocity (isometric joystick). Gain is one parameter of a transfer function, not a synonym for it.

Implicit interaction

An action, performed by the user, that is not primarily aimed to interact with a computerized system but which such a system understands as input (Albrecht Schmidt, quoted in the deck). Pressing a button is never implicit, however the reading is interpreted.

Design space & notation

Exam questions in this unit usually ask you to place a device somewhere and justify it. The morphological view from the reading helps: a device is a point in a parametrically described design space, and the useful axes are the ones that change what the device can do.

Axis 2 — directness and absoluteness

Direct and absolute

  • Input and display share one surface
  • Pointing is literal; no cursor is required
  • Costs: no buttons for state transitions, and the hand occludes the target

Indirect and relative

  • The display is elsewhere; a cursor carries the position
  • Device and cursor can be decoupled, so clutching is possible
  • Costs: clutching, quantisation and limb-precision problems

The models behind pointing

Four relationships are worth being able to write from memory: how long a target takes to acquire, how long a choice takes, how long a constrained path takes, and how the device's motion is scaled on the way to the screen.

1. Fitts's law — discrete target acquisition

With \(A\) the amplitude of the movement and \(W\) the target's width along the direction of motion, the average movement time is a logarithmic function of their ratio:

\[ MT \;=\; a + b\,\log_2\!\left(\frac{A}{W} + 1\right), \qquad ID \;=\; \log_2\!\left(\frac{A}{W} + 1\right) \]

The constants \(a\) and \(b\) are fitted by linear regression and depend on the device and the task, so they should never be transplanted between studies. \(ID\) is in bits and is device-independent: it describes the target, not the hand.

2. Hick's law — choice among alternatives

For \(n\) equally probable alternatives, decision time grows with the entropy of the choice:

\[ H \;=\; \log_2(n + 1) \]

The two laws have almost the same form. Read the Fitts task as a decision along the amplitude \(A\) between \(n = A/W\) targets of width \(W\) and they coincide — which is why the reading suggests they may be the same law seen twice.

3. The steering law — constrained paths

Steering through a tunnel of length \(A\) and width \(W\) is not a Fitts task, because the cursor must stay inside the tunnel the whole way. Movement time is then linear in the ratio:

\[ MT \;=\; a + b\,\frac{A}{W} \]

Pull-down menus are the canonical case: the user is not acquiring a target, they are staying inside a corridor. That is why menu design cares about corridor width and why a nested submenu widens the path it must then re-enter.

4. Gain and rate mappings

The deck's control-display gain, and the nonlinear rate mapping used for self-centring force devices:

\[ G_{CD} \;=\; \frac{v_{\text{cursor}}}{v_{\text{device}}}, \qquad dx \;=\; K\,x^{\alpha_r} \]

Gain confounds device size with display size, which is why it is a suspect experimental factor; in practice it mainly buys a smaller physical footprint. In the rate mapping, \(x\) is the input signal, \(dx\) the resulting rate, \(K\) a gain factor and \(\alpha_r\) the nonlinear parameter — both must be found experimentally for the specific device.

5. Acquisition cost is additive

The time to perform a task with a device you are not already holding includes the time to get to it and back:

\[ t_{\text{task}} \;=\; t_{\text{point}} \;+\; t_{acq} \;+\; t_{home} \]

For workflows that alternate typing and pointing, the pointing term usually dominates the two homing terms — which is why bolting a pointing stick into a keyboard does not automatically improve overall performance.

Worked calculation. A designer shrinks a toolbar button from 40 px to 20 px wide while the pointer has to travel 360 px to reach it. By how much does the index of difficulty grow, and what does the gain tell you if the device moves 120 px while the cursor moves 360 px?

1

Write the model. \(ID = \log_2(A/W + 1)\) with \(A = 360\) and \(W\) the target width along the direction of motion.

2

Before. \(ID = \log_2(360/40 + 1) = \log_2 10 \approx 3.32\) bits.

3

After. \(ID = \log_2(360/20 + 1) = \log_2 19 \approx 4.25\) bits.

4

Interpret. Difficulty rose by about 0.93 bits, roughly 28 %. The extra time is \(b \times 0.93\) seconds, where \(b\) is the fitted slope for that device — you cannot give a time in milliseconds without \(b\), and inventing one is how answers lose marks.

5

Gain. \(G_{CD} = v_{\text{cursor}}/v_{\text{device}} = 360/120 = 3\). The cursor moves three times as far as the device. Halving the gain would reduce the desk space needed and slow the cursor; the model says the effect on movement time is usually small, so gain is a footprint decision more than a speed decision.

6

Sanity check with Hick. If the toolbar instead became a menu of 19 equally probable items, the choice cost would be \(H = \log_2(19 + 1) = \log_2 20 \approx 4.32\) bits — almost exactly the Fitts difficulty of the narrow button. That coincidence is the reading's point, not a rounding error.

Why is steering through a pull-down menu modelled with the steering law rather than Fitts's law?
Menus do not involve target acquisition of any kind
Because staying inside the corridor is a continuous constraint, and the steering law is linear in A/W rather than logarithmic
Because the hand moves faster in a menu than between two targets

Expected, sensed and desired as set relationships

The ESD framework is easiest to use when you write it as sets and then read off the four regions. Every region has a different design response, and no region is empty in practice.

The three sets and their four regions

Let \(E\) be the movements users can and will perform, \(S\) the movements the sensors can measure, and \(D\) the movements the application needs. The analysis is the comparison of \(E\) and \(S\), checked against \(D\):

\[ E \cap S, \qquad E \setminus S, \qquad S \setminus E, \qquad D \setminus (E \cup S) \]

The first three are properties of the interface; the fourth is a property of the ambition, and it is the one that sends you back to the design.

RegionReads asDesign response
\(E \cap S\)Natural and measurableTighten the match in range, speed and accuracy; this is ordinary interaction design
\(E \setminus S\)Natural but unmeasurableExploit it: clutching, rest spaces, preparation, follow-through, expressive latitude
\(S \setminus E\)Measurable but unnaturalError detection, or a deliberately hidden advanced mode
\(D \setminus (E \cup S)\)Wanted but neither natural nor measurableRedesign: change the interface, the sensors, or the ambition

Intentionality is a spectrum, not a switch

Intentional (press the light switch), expected (walk in knowing the lights will come on), incidental (the A/C changes and you never notice). As you move along it, the dialogue gets shorter and the cost of a wrong inference gets larger, because the system has already acted.

Proxemic zones as arithmetic

Hall's interpersonal zones are intimate (under 1.5 ft), personal (1.5–4 ft), social (4–12 ft) and public (12–25 ft) — approximately 0.46, 1.22, 3.66 and 7.62 metres at the boundaries. Treat them as conventions to calibrate, not as constants: what matters is that a single continuous measure yields discrete behavioural zones.

The examinable sentence: proximity gives a system a continuous, socially meaningful signal that requires no command from the user; the design obligation is to decide what happens when the inferred zone is wrong, and to make the inferred zone visible enough that the user can correct it.
A gesture interface senses a wild arm swing that users would never perform deliberately, and the application has no use for it. Which region is it in, and what is the standard response?
\(E \setminus S\) — build a rest space so the movement stops being reported
\(S \setminus E\) — treat it as error detection, or expose it as a deliberately hidden mode
\(D \setminus (E \cup S)\) — change the application's ambition

Classification and assumption storming

Two routines to rehearse: placing a device in the design space with the checklist, and stress-testing the idea behind it with the tutorial's assumption storming.

Sort assumptions by type

Problem — does the problem really exist? Solution — is this the right way to solve it? Implementation — can you build it before you run out of time or money?

Place them on the grid

Two axes: risk (how bad if the assumption is false) and confidence (how sure you are that it is true). High risk with low confidence is where the research starts.

Challenge the placement

Is that really a known known, or an assumption wearing a costume? Can you find evidence for or against it quickly? Disagreement inside the group is a signal, not a delay.

Pair every objective with a method

The tutorial's research plan is a two-column table: objective on the left, method and tool on the right. An objective with no method is a wish.

Worked classification. Place three devices with the checklist, in two sentences each.

1

Touchscreen. Property sensed: linear position, two dimensions, absolute (\(\alpha = 1\)) and direct (\(\delta = 1\)), so position-to-position is the natural mapping. States: out of range, touch, tracking — the extra condition is contact lost without a release. Verdict: excellent for selection and position, weak for text and for anything needing a state transition, because there are no buttons and the finger occludes the target.

2

Isometric joystick. Property sensed: force, two dimensions, relative (\(\alpha = 0\)) and indirect (\(\delta = 0\)), self-centring. The property sensed dictates a force-to-velocity rate mapping; a position-to-position mapping would leave the user with no control of speed. Verdict: the right device when there is no room for a mouse, at the cost of a learning period and lower precision.

3

Six-degree-of-freedom tracker. Property sensed: position (3) and orientation (3), \(\alpha\) depends on the mode, indirect. States and mappings multiply, so the design question is chunking: until the six dimensions are integrated into one higher-level task, the user is operating six controls. Verdict: powerful for 3D manipulation and unusable without chunking.

4

The sentence that earns the mark. “The device senses p in n dimensions; because it is absolute/relative and direct/indirect, the appropriate transfer function is T; its states are …; and the tasks it serves well are …, with the trade-off that ….” Fill every slot and there is nothing left to guess.

Worked answer — assumption storming a mid-air gesture unlock. Idea: a door that opens when you wave your hand in a recognisable pattern. Run the tutorial's routine over it.

1

Problem assumptions. That keys, cards and phones are a real burden; that people arrive with their hands free; that the current lock, rather than the walk from the car, is what makes entry slow. Evidence needed: observed arrivals, hands-full frequency, time spent at the door.

2

Solution assumptions. That a mid-air gesture is a natural action here (it is not: opening a door is a push, not a wave); that a gesture can be recognised reliably in the dark, in rain, and while carrying shopping; that the gesture is not observable and replayable by someone standing behind you — which is the security flaw a PIN pad does not have.

3

Implementation assumptions. That a sensor can cover the approach zone with acceptable false accepts and false rejects; that the door can be driven and powered on site; that the gesture can be re-enrolled when recognition degrades.

4

Place them on the risk-by-confidence grid. “A bystander can replay the gesture” is high risk and low confidence in the current design: it is the first thing to test, and the reason to reconsider the gesture altogether. “A door has a hinge” is low risk and high confidence, so it is ignored.

5

Rewrite as research objectives with methods. “Find out whether users prefer a gesture to a key at this door” — intercept interviews at the entrance. “Measure false accepts with a bystander imitating the gesture” — a staged trial with a logged prototype. “Find out how the door should signal that it recognised you” — a Wizard-of-Oz study with a concealed operator. Each objective now has a method, which is what the tutorial's research plan requires.

6

Re-run ESD on the survivor. If the gesture survives, write it down: expected = approach and push the door; sensed = hand motion in the approach zone; desired = unlock for the right person only. Then check the mismatches, and note that “push the door” was in \(E\) all along — which is exactly the implicit redesign from the study guide.

In the tutorial's research plan, what is wrong with the objective “understand how people feel about the door”?
Nothing — feelings are the proper subject of interaction design
It cannot be paired with a method: there is no study, population or measure implied by it
It is a problem assumption, and problem assumptions must be removed before research begins
