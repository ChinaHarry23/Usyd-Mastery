Foundations: the vocabulary, notation and budgets behind haptic interaction

Week 7 is only lightly mathematical, so this page does the equivalent job: it pins down the definitions the lecture relies on, gives you notation for describing a haptic signal and a conservative power budget for driving an actuator, and ends with a checklist that turns “add a vibration motor” into a defensible design decision.

Definitions that must be exact

The Week 7 deck opens with a block of definitions because these words are used loosely everywhere else. Marks are lost on the distinctions, not on the topic.

Haptic interaction / haptic feedback

Interactive experiences involving the sense of touch. Haptic feedback is anything relating to the sense of touch — the widest of these terms.

Cutaneous, tactile and vibrotactile

Cutaneous pertains to the skin itself or the skin as a sense organ, and includes pressure, temperature and pain. Tactile is the same sense narrowed to pressure rather than temperature or pain. Vibrotactile is vibration delivered to the skin — the only one of the three that names a stimulus rather than a sense.

Kinaesthetic, force feedback and proprioceptive

Kinaesthetic relates to the feeling of motion and to sensations originating in muscles, tendons and joints. Force feedback relates to the mechanical production of information sensed by the human kinaesthetic system. Proprioceptive is the umbrella term for sensory information about the state of the body, including cutaneous, kinaesthetic and vestibular sensations.

Vestibular

Pertaining to the perception of head position, acceleration and deceleration. It is the one channel in this list that does not come from the skin, the muscles or the joints.

Axis 3 — which motor, decided by the information

Two vibration motor families

  • ERM — eccentric rotating mass, powered by a DC motor. The rotation of the off-centre mass makes it vibrate.
  • LRA — linear resonant actuator: a mass on a spring driven by a coil, moving up and down.

Both can easily draw 100 mA, so the deck's advice is a transistor plus an external supply.

Three motor families and their control

  • Servo — precise position, speed and torque; three wires; motor + pot + control circuit.
  • Stepper — open loop, full rotation, 200 steps per rotation; one pulse, one step.
  • DC — simplest, rotates 360 degrees, angle not controllable.
Your device must rotate a camera mount to an exact angle, 1.8 degrees at a time, without any position sensor. Which motor does the lecture's guidance point to?
DC motor, because it is the simplest option
Stepper motor, because open-loop steps give exact small increments with no feedback
Servo motor, because it has a potentiometer for feedback

1. A vibration is a carrier plus an envelope

Separating the two is what makes the deck's six parameters usable. Frequency and waveform describe the carrier; duration, rhythm and ADSR describe the envelope; amplitude couples them.

\[ H(t) \;=\; A(t)\,\sin(2\pi f t), \qquad A(t) \;=\; \text{attack} \to \text{decay} \to \text{sustain} \to \text{release} \]

Practical consequence: two signals with the same frequency can carry completely different meanings, because meaning is carried by the envelope — a tap, a double pulse and a long rumble all use the same carrier.

2. Detection is a probability, not a boundary

There is no stimulus strength below which nothing is ever felt and above which everything is. Detection rises smoothly, which is why the threshold is defined at a criterion:

\[ P(\text{detect} \mid s) = 0.5 \;\Rightarrow\; s = s_{50} = \theta_t, \qquad P(\text{detect} \mid s) = 0.75 \;\Rightarrow\; s = s_{75} \]

In the lecture's 2AFC procedure the guessing rate is 0.5, so \(s_{50}\) is exactly the point at which performance leaves chance. Anything below \(s_{50}\) is manipulation the user cannot report — and in a retargeting prototype, that is the design space.

3. Adaptation means a constant signal stops counting

Under a constant stimulus the perceived intensity decays with a time constant \(\tau\), so the effective signal is the change in the envelope, not the envelope itself:

\[ H_{\text{felt}}(t) \;\propto\; \left| \frac{dA}{dt} \right| \quad \text{rather than} \quad A(t) \]

This is the formal reading of the deck's list. A steady amplitude is a signal that disappears; rhythm and ADSR keep \(\frac{dA}{dt}\) non-zero, which is why they are design parameters rather than decorations. A long steady buzz is the special case you should avoid.

4. Stepper resolution is exact arithmetic

A stepper turns one step per pulse, so the smallest angle you can command is fixed by the motor rather than by your code:

\[ \Delta\theta_{\text{step}} \;=\; \frac{360^\circ}{N}, \qquad N = 200 \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta\theta_{\text{step}} = 1.8^\circ \]

Open loop means the count is the position: no feedback is needed, and none is available if the motor is overloaded and slips. That is the trade a stepper makes, in contrast to a servo, which closes the loop internally with its potentiometer and returns an angle rather than a count.

Putting the four together. A haptic signal is a carrier shaped by an envelope \(A(t)\); it is perceived only above a probabilistic threshold \(s_{50}\); its envelope must keep changing or adaptation \(\tau\) will erase it; and if it is produced by a stepper, the resolution of the position it encodes is exactly \(360^\circ/N\).
Two vibration patterns use the same frequency of 200 Hz and the same peak amplitude. One is felt as a tap and the other as a rumble. What distinguishes them?
The carrier frequency differs between the two patterns
The envelope and rhythm differ: attack, decay, sustain and release
The acuity of the stimulated site differs

Worked example — reading a psychometric curve. You run the lecture's 2AFC procedure: after each trial the participant is asked “was there an offset? Yes / No.” You test angular offsets of 0, 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30 degrees.

1

Set the baseline. With two alternatives and no offset present, a participant who perceives nothing still answers correctly half the time by guessing. So the floor of the curve is 0.5, not 0.

2

Locate the threshold. The offset at which the probability reaches 0.50 is \(s_{50}\), the detection threshold. Using the illustrative curve on the study page, that falls around 14 to 15 degrees: above that point the manipulation is being reported more often than chance.

3

Choose the criterion you will design against. \(s_{75}\), the conventional “clearly perceived” point, sits higher up the curve. Designing at \(s_{50}\) means half your users notice; designing well below it means the manipulation survives.

4

Report the limits of the measurement. The deck's curve is described as the default case: one direction of movement, one speed, one posture. Change the conditions and the curve moves, so any offset you quote needs the conditions attached to it.

5

State what the number licenses. Offsets below \(s_{50}\) can be applied without the user reporting them, which is exactly what lets one physical prop stand in for several virtual objects — provided the user keeps their eyes on the virtual hand and moves slowly, as the technique requires.

The motor power chain

This is the arithmetic behind the deck's rule of thumb. Nothing here is exotic; the point is to be able to justify, in numbers, why a motor never comes off a microcontroller pin.

The lecture's rule of thumb, in one sentence

If a load draws more than a trivial amount of current, switches on and off frequently, or is inductive (like a motor), it is safest to give it its own supply and use the microcontroller only to control it — via a transistor — rather than power it directly.

QuantityWhat it isWhere the number comes from
\(V_{\text{motor}}\)The motor's own supply voltageThe battery or bench supply: the deck's example is 9 V
\(V_{\text{logic}}\)The microcontroller's logic levelThe board's supply — below the motor rail in the lecture's example
\(I_{\text{motor}}\)Current the motor drawsDatasheet; a vibration motor can easily draw 100 mA
\(I_{\text{pin}}\)Current a GPIO pin can safely sourceDatasheet, and always far below \(I_{\text{motor}}\) for a motor
\(R_{\text{DS(on)}}\)Transistor on-resistanceDatasheet; sets the transistor's power loss when it is switching
\(V_f\)Diode forward voltageDatasheet; a flyback diode is normally reverse-biased and conducts only during the back-EMF spike

1. Why the pin cannot drive the motor

The motor's demand and the pin's capability are two different orders of magnitude:

\[ I_{\text{motor}} \;\gg\; I_{\text{pin}} \]

A vibration motor at 100 mA against a pin rated for tens of milliamps is already a factor of several. The consequence is not a degraded experience but a damaged output driver, which is why the deck says the motor needs an external power supply controlled by a transistor.

2. The transistor is a current amplifier, not a power source

Current through the motor is supplied by the battery; the gate only decides whether that path is open. In the ideal switch model the dissipated power is

\[ P_{\text{sw}} \;=\; I_{\text{motor}}^2 \, R_{\text{DS(on)}} \]

Compare that with the power the motor itself consumes, \(P_{\text{motor}} \approx V_{\text{motor}} I_{\text{motor}}\): a good MOSFET makes \(P_{\text{sw}}\) a small fraction of it, so the switch stays cool while the motor does the work.

3. Why an inductive load needs a flyback diode

A motor is an inductor. When the transistor opens, the collapsing magnetic field pushes current back, producing a spike of the opposite polarity across the switch. The diode provides the only path for it:

\[ v_L \;=\; -L\,\frac{di}{dt} \]

The faster you interrupt the current, the larger the spike. The diode clamps it by giving the current somewhere to go, which is exactly the deck's formulation: the diode safely dissipates the back electromotive force when the motor is powered down. Without it, the spike burns the MOSFET.

4. Common ground is a shared reference, not a shared supply

The gate signal is a voltage measured relative to ground. For the microcontroller to switch the transistor, the two circuits must agree on where zero is:

\[ \text{GND}_{\text{battery}} \;\equiv\; \text{GND}_{\text{MCU}}, \qquad \text{VCC}_{\text{battery}} \;\not\equiv\; \text{VCC}_{\text{MCU}} \]

Connect the GND of the battery and the microcontroller; do not connect their VCC. Joining the positive rails would push motor current and back-EMF through the microcontroller's supply, which is the failure the separate rail exists to prevent.

What the numbers say in one line. A motor takes \(I\) that a pin cannot give, so it gets its own \(V\); a transistor lets the logic decide without carrying the load; a diode handles the \(L\,di/dt\) the switch cannot; and a common ground lets the logic's decision mean something to the motor's circuit.
A student wires a motor, a transistor, a diode and a 9 V battery, but forgets to connect the battery's ground to the microcontroller's ground. What happens?
The gate signal has no common reference, so the transistor never switches reliably
The 9 V rail and the logic rail short together and destroy the microcontroller
Nothing at all — the flyback diode supplies the missing reference

Reading the drive circuit

The lecture builds the circuit one component at a time, each time asking what is still wrong. Use this diagram to check that you can name every part and say what it is for.

The lecture's build-up, in order

// each line is a question the previous circuit could not answer
1. battery + motor            -> it spins, but nothing controls it
2. + microcontroller          -> we want to control it ... digitally
3. + transistor               -> the voltage is too high for the pin
4. + flyback diode            -> current flows back when we power down;
                                 it will burn the MOSFET
5. + common ground            -> GND together, VCC never together

Powering from the internal supply

Possible only while the motor's current stays within what the board's regulator and the pin can supply. The lecture treats this as the case that does not hold for motors: a vibration motor can easily draw 100 mA, which is why the transistor and the external source appear.

Powering from an external supply

Connect the GND of the battery and the microcontroller. Do not connect their VCC. The control wire still comes from the microcontroller — for a servo, that is the orange wire while the red wire moves to the external supply.

A servo is stronger than the microcontroller's regulator can supply, but you still want to command its angle from code. What does the lecture say to do?
Keep the control wire on the microcontroller and move the red (Vcc) wire to an external supply
Move the orange control wire to the external supply and keep the red wire on the board
Add a flyback diode and keep both wires where they are

Worked example — sizing a haptic prototype's power budget. A wearable cues direction with three ERM vibration motors, one per shoulder and one at the sternum.

1

Write down the load. The deck's figure: a vibration motor can easily draw 100 mA. Three of them, potentially on at once, is a peak of roughly 300 mA.

2

Compare against the pin. A GPIO pin is rated for tens of milliamps at most. Even one motor already exceeds it, so no motor may come off a pin. The comparison \(I_{\text{motor}} \gg I_{\text{pin}}\) is the whole argument.

3

Apply the rule of thumb. The load draws real current, switches on and off frequently, and is inductive. All three conditions point the same way: give the motors their own supply and let the microcontroller control them through transistors.

4

Protect each switch. One flyback diode per motor, across the motor terminals, so the inductive kick has a path when that channel switches off. Sharing a single diode between channels does not protect the switch that is actually opening.

5

Tie the references. Battery GND and microcontroller GND together, VCC rails apart. Then check the design against the week's perception side as well: three spatially separated motors give you a categorical direction cue, and the ADSR envelope is what keeps each cue perceptible rather than fading under adaptation.

Haptic design checklist

Most Week 7 exam questions reduce to “design (or critique) a haptic feedback for this situation”. Run the seven steps in order and the justification writes itself.

StepLook forMarks are lost when…
1. InformationIs it a discrete event, a continuous property, or a spatial cue?You describe the hardware before saying what it communicates
2. Receptor classThermo-, mechano-, noci- or proprioceptorsYou choose vibration for weight or temperature
3. Channel and actuatorVibrotactile, thermal, force, passive propYou name a device without naming the channel it writes to
4. Signal parametersThe six parameters and the ADSR envelopeYou specify “a buzz” with no envelope, guaranteeing adaptation
5. Power chainOwn supply, transistor switch, diode, shared groundYou power a motor from a GPIO pin
6. The bodyAcuity at the site, threshold, adaptationYou design below the detection threshold or above what the site can resolve
7. Failure modeMissed cue, nuisance buzz, discomfort, lost controlYou claim the design has no trade-offs

Audit question one

Which receptor class does this design write to, and is that the class that carries the information? If the answer is “mechanoreceptors” and the information is weight, the design is already wrong.

Audit question two

Does the signal change over time? If the envelope is flat, adaptation will remove it from perception no matter how strong it is.

Audit question three

Where does the current come from? If the answer involves a GPIO pin and a motor in the same sentence, the power chain is not finished.

Audit question four

What does the user lose? Discomfort, missed cues, false urgency and lost control are the costs this week's devices typically impose.
