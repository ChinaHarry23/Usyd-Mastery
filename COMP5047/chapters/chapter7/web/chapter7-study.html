What Do We Feel Through Touch?

Haptic interaction means interactive experiences involving the sense of touch. Before designing feedback you have to know which sensors you are actually addressing, because “touch” is not a single channel.

The lecture's opening definition: haptic interaction is interactive experiences involving the sense of touch. Everything in this week — vibration motors, force feedback, thermals, retargeting — is a way of writing information onto the human somatosensory system.

Receptor classWhat it transducesWhat it lets you design
ThermoreceptorsChanges in skin temperatureWarm/cold cues: material identity, presence of a person, warning of heat
MechanoreceptorsPressure, vibration, slip, stretchThe whole vibrotactile vocabulary, plus the feeling of grip and slippage
NociceptorsPain — mechanical, thermal, chemicalAlmost nothing directly; they define the safety envelope you must not cross
ProprioceptorsMuscle length and tension, limb positionsForce feedback, resistance, weight, and where your own hand is
Why the split matters for design: a vibration motor writes to mechanoreceptors only. If the information you want to convey is “this object is heavy” or “this surface is hot”, no amount of vibration amplitude will carry it faithfully — you have chosen the wrong receptor class. This is exactly the gap the lecture sets up with the list of questions a hand asks an object.

The questions a hand asks

  • What does it weigh?
  • Is it rough or smooth?
  • Is it solid or squidgy?
  • Is it cold or hot?

Each question is answered by a different combination of receptors, which is why a single actuator rarely feels convincing.

Three properties of any touch channel

  • Acuity — spatial resolution: how close can two stimuli be and still be told apart?
  • Sensitivity — detection threshold: how weak can a stimulus be and still be felt?
  • Adaptation — how the response changes over time under a constant stimulus
Exam framing: never describe a haptic device without naming the receptor class it targets. “A vibration motor on the wristband” is a description; “a vibration motor driving cutaneous mechanoreceptors, which is why it can signal urgency but not weight” is an answer.
A controller resists your finger as you press into a virtual sponge. Which receptor class is doing the work of telling you the sponge is firm?
Thermoreceptors
Nociceptors
Proprioceptors, because resistance is felt through muscle tension and limb position

Some Definitions

The deck opens with a block of definitions because the words are used loosely in industry and precisely in HCI. Learn them as a set — the distinctions are what exam questions test.

How the four nest inside each other

Cutaneous is the skin as a sense organ (pressure, temperature, pain). Tactile narrows cutaneous to pressure alone. Kinaesthetic moves the source inward to muscles, tendons and joints. Vestibular moves it to the inner ear, to head position and acceleration. Proprioceptive is the umbrella: everything about the state of the body, including all of the above. Force feedback is the engineering counterpart — mechanically produced information aimed at the kinaesthetic system.

TermSource of the sensationTypical device
VibrotactileSkin surface, mechanoreceptorsERM or LRA motor in a phone, watch or controller
ThermalSkin surface, thermoreceptorsPeltier element, fan, heating pad
Cutaneous / tactileSkin surface, pressure and slipTextured surface, pneumatic bladder, pin array
Kinaesthetic / forceMuscles, tendons, jointsGrounded force-feedback arm, exoskeleton, electrical muscle stimulation
VestibularInner ear, head motionMotion platform, Galvanic vestibular stimulation
A researcher says a device produces “haptic feedback”, then clarifies that it produces “force feedback”. What has been narrowed?
From the whole sense of touch to mechanical information sensed by the kinaesthetic system
From weak vibration to strong vibration, measured in amplitude
From research-grade hardware to commercially shipping hardware

Acuity, Sensitivity and Adaptation

Every haptic design decision is bounded by what a person can actually detect. The deck names three properties of the touch channel — and the research literature measures them with a standard method.

Acuity — spatial resolution

How far apart two points must be before they are felt as two. This sets the pixel pitch of any tactile display: pins closer together than the local acuity are wasted hardware, and pins further apart produce gaps the user reads as texture.

Sensitivity — detection threshold

The weakest stimulus that is reliably felt. Below threshold, a motor spends current producing nothing. Threshold also depends on where on the body you stimulate and on what the person is doing at the time.

Adaptation

Adaptation is how the response changes over time under a constant stimulus. A steady vibration fades from notice; the same vibration restarted a moment later is felt again. Practical consequence: haptic signals must change to keep being perceived, which is why the deck lists duration and rhythm alongside frequency and amplitude.

Measuring a threshold: the 2AFC method from the lecture

In the retargeting experiments the deck describes, participants were asked after each trial: “was there an offset? Yes / No.” That is a two-alternative forced choice task. Repeating it across offset magnitudes gives the probability of perceiving the offset as a function of its size.

  • At small offsets the probability sits near 0.5 — a guess, because the two alternatives are indistinguishable.
  • The curve rises with the size of the manipulation.
  • The 50% point is the detection threshold; the 75% point is the conventional criterion for “clearly perceived”.

Everything below threshold is design space: manipulation the user cannot report, which is precisely what makes haptic retargeting work.

Read the deck's graph carefully. The main condition and the no-offset control share the same axes — probability of perceiving the offset from 0 to 1 on the vertical, angular offset on the horizontal, sampled at 0, 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30 degrees. The control condition stays flat at the guessing rate; the main condition climbs. That difference is the result.

Worked example — choosing a manipulation size for a retargeting prototype. You have a passive prop that sits 10 cm to the right of where the virtual object appears, and you must decide whether the illusion will survive.

1

Convert the offset into the unit the psychophysics is measured in. The deck's curve is plotted against angular offset in degrees, not centimetres, because the same displacement subtends a larger angle when the hand is closer to the body. A 10 cm shift at arm's length is a different manipulation from a 10 cm shift close to the shoulder.

2

Locate that angle on the curve. If it falls where the detection probability is still near the guessing rate, most users will not report an offset and the illusion holds. If it lands near the top of the curve, nearly everyone reports it and the illusion fails.

3

Do not design at the 50% point. Half of your users noticing is a coin flip, not a working prototype. The 75% point is the conventional “clearly perceived” criterion, so stay meaningfully below it — and say so, rather than quoting a single number with no margin.

4

State the assumptions. The curve was measured for a particular movement direction (the deck's wording is that this is the graph of the default case), a particular speed, and a particular posture. Change any of them and you need your own curve. The deck's own follow-up questions — how far apart can these objects be, how large can this offset be, what is the space of interaction now — are exactly the questions a designer must answer empirically.

In a 2AFC experiment, participants report perceiving an offset on 50% of trials at a 14° rotation. What is the correct reading?
The user detects the offset perfectly at 14°, so 14° is the limit
14° is the detection threshold: the point where performance leaves the guessing rate
14° is a safe design margin, because half of the users still fail to notice

Vibrotactile and Thermal Feedback

Vibration is the channel almost every consumer device uses. The deck's framing is blunt: there is a large gap between the experiences users want and the parameters we can actually set.

Vibration — what users want

  • “Oh, this message is urgent!” — urgency and priority
  • “Ouch! Someone shot me!” — events inside a virtual world
  • “Look, mate: I am touching snow!” — material and texture
  • “Cool, this is what riding a motorcycle feels like” — motion and vibration of a vehicle

Notice that only the first is a notification. The other three are attempts to describe the world through the skin.

Vibration — what we have got

  • Frequency
  • Duration
  • Amplitude
  • Waveform
  • Rhythm
  • ADSR — attack, decay, sustain, release

Six knobs. The design work is choosing trajectories through that six-dimensional space, not adding more motors.

ADSR as a haptic design tool

Attack is how fast the vibration reaches full strength, decay how it falls to the sustained level, sustain the level it holds, and release how it fades out. Because of adaptation, a signal that never changes is a signal that stops being felt: the envelope is what keeps the user perceiving it. A sharp attack with a short release reads as a tap; a slow attack with a long sustain reads as pressure or a rumble.

Thermal feedback: a real channel, still early

Three ways to move heat into or out of skin appear in the deck: a fan (convective cooling), a Peltier element (solid-state heating and cooling, reversible by reversing the current) and heating pads (resistive heating only). The lecture's own assessment is that it is still early days for our understanding of how to design thermal feedback for VR. The live design question is not whether we can change skin temperature but how you design so that people feel they are touching virtual objects.

Language the markers reward: describe what a channel is for in terms of the information it carries. Vibration is a superb channel for discrete, urgent, non-spatial events; it is a poor channel for continuous properties such as weight or temperature. Thermal feedback is the reverse in some respects — slow, low bandwidth, but semantically rich, because temperature is how we identify materials.
A game wants the player to feel “the sword is heavy”. A wristband with an ERM vibration motor is fitted. What is the fundamental limitation?
The motor is not powerful enough; a bigger ERM would solve it
Weight is sensed by the kinaesthetic system, and vibration only stimulates cutaneous mechanoreceptors
The sampling rate of the motor driver is too low to encode weight

Force Feedback: Grounded, Ultrasound and Electrical

Once the information you want to convey is mechanical — resistance, weight, compliance, contact — vibration is no longer the right tool. The deck walks through the families of devices that produce actual forces.

ApproachWhere the force comes fromWhat it costs you
VibrotactileA motor shaking the skinCannot carry weight, compliance or temperature
Grounded force feedbackA linkage anchored to the real worldTethered, expensive, limited workspace
UltrasoundFocused acoustic pressure in mid-airSmall workspace, weak forces, no grasp
Electrical muscle stimulationThe user's own muscles, driven by electrodesYou lose control: the system moves you rather than yielding to you
Passive haptics / substitutional realityReal objects, substituted for virtual onesOne prop per virtual object unless you retarget
Haptic retargetingA single prop, plus a redirection of the handNeeds visual attention and slow, scripted movement
The design question the deck keeps circling: how do you design to let people touch virtual objects? Grounded devices answer it accurately and expensively; passive props answer it cheaply and rigidly; retargeting answers it cleverly and only under scripted conditions. Being able to state the trade-off is worth more than being able to name the devices.
A VR demo gives the user a single physical cup. In one virtual scene it is a mug, in another a torch, in another a sword hilt. Which technique makes this work?
Substitutional reality with one prop per object
Haptic retargeting: the reach is redirected so one prop serves several virtual objects
Electrical muscle stimulation

Two Design Camps: Accuracy and Naturalness

The deck splits haptic research into two crowds that routinely talk past each other. Naming the split, and what each side optimises, is one of the most markable ideas in the lecture.

The accuracy crowd

Builds specialist controllers that optimise the experience of a specific property of a virtual object — how heavy it is, whether it is rough or smooth, solid or squidgy, cold or hot. The CLAW controller (Choi et al., CHI '18) is the deck's example for grasping, touching and triggering; dynamic bar charts (Taher et al., CHI '15) simulate forces within the hand.

The naturalness crowd

Seeks to make interactions as “everyday-lifelike” as possible, using whatever the body and the room already provide. This is the camp that reaches for substitutional reality, passive props and retargeting rather than for more actuators.

Commercial controllersThe gapResearch controllers
What they haveTracking, joystick, buttons, trigger, grip button, vibration motor. Lightweight, handheld, compact, robustThe deck's diagram puts a question mark here and asks how to fill itIdeas and lessons about simulating forces and object properties with high fidelity
What they lackResolution: the vibration motor is the only haptic channel and it is coarseRobustness and generalizability: each device is a specialist build
What to takeTake the constraints — robustness and generalizability — from commercial design, and the ideas and lessons from research. The deck's answer to “could you do it?” is yes.
The deck's own aesthetics: a haptic demo that reproduces one object beautifully is a result, and a controller that works for everything but convinces nobody is also a result. The interesting engineering is the space between them, and the lecture's framing of that space is the reason motors come next: the general-purpose answer has to be built out of actuators.

Haptics in VR: Substitution and Retargeting

Virtual reality makes the problem sharp. There is nothing to touch, so either you bring physical objects into the virtual scene, or you make the user's body believe something that is not there.

Substitutional reality (the deck's definition)

A class of mixed-reality environments in which a substitution process adapts a virtual environment to the physical world. By substituting each physical element with a virtual counterpart, the virtual environment is able to leverage physical properties to create convincing bodily experiences. The problem: you need a physical prop wherever each virtual object is, which massively reduces the opportunities for design.

Alternative 1 — move the objects

Keep the props few and move them to wherever the current virtual scene needs them. The deck presents this as the first way out of the one-prop-per-object trap.

Alternative 2 — move the user's hand

Leave the prop alone and redirect the reach. This is haptic retargeting, and the deck spends most of this section on it because it needs no moving parts at all.

The limitations belong in your answer. The deck's own caveats: targeting requires constant, focused visual attention, and you need to move relatively slowly. It also asks whether retargeting is the holy grail of VR haptics and answers honestly — it is one technique among many, notable because it is rare for a new interaction technique to receive this much attention, and the open questions (how far apart can these objects be, how large can this offset be, what is the space of interaction now, what are the limits) are still research questions.
Why does haptic retargeting require the user to move slowly and to look at their hand?
The illusion relies on vision overriding proprioception, and fast or unattended movement exposes the discrepancy
The tracking system cannot sample fast enough to follow a quick reach
The physical prop must be moved into place before the reach completes

Worked example — designing a haptic retargeting trial. You have two virtual objects 25° apart on a virtual table and exactly one physical prop.

1

Choose which object keeps the prop. Put the prop where the user will reach most often, and retarget the others towards it. Retargeting costs visual attention, so spend it on the occasional object, not the frequent one.

2

Check the offset against the detection curve. 25° is far enough that the deck's own curve is climbing steeply, so expect many participants to report the manipulation. Either reduce the separation of the virtual objects, or accept that the illusion will fail for part of the population — and say which.

3

Measure it the way the lecture did. Run a 2AFC task: after each trial ask “was there an offset? Yes / No”, with a no-offset control condition. Without the control you cannot separate genuine perception from a response bias towards saying yes.

4

Report the limitations with the result. Constant focused visual attention and slow movement are part of the technique, not artefacts of your prototype. A retargeting demo that hides its constraints will not survive a reviewer or an examiner.

Motors, Drivers and Safety

The lecture's answer to “how do we build something general purpose?” is actuators. This section is the engineering half of the week: which motor to choose, and how to drive it without destroying your microcontroller.

MotorControl you getUse it when
Servo motorPrecise control over position, speed and torque, typically over about 180°Robotics, CNC machines and automation — anywhere the angle matters. Motor + potentiometer + control circuit in one package; three wires (red Vcc, brown ground, orange control)
Stepper motorOpen-loop control with precise movement in small steps, full rotation, 200 steps per rotation3D printers, CNC machines, cameras. Controlled with a motor driver: send a pulse and it rotates exactly one step
DC motorRotates 360°, but you cannot precisely control its anglePrecise positioning is not crucial: fans, conveyors, and — with an eccentric mass — vibration
Vibration motorOn/off vibration, shaped through PWM and the ADSR envelopeThe output channel of nearly every haptic consumer device

Why a motor cannot be wired straight to a pin

// The lecture's two challenges for a DC motor
// 1. It typically requires more current than a microcontroller can provide
//      -> needs an external power supply, controlled by a transistor
// 2. You need to control the current that flows back as it powers down
//      -> needs a diode or a motor controller chip

// Stepper motor: open loop, 200 steps per rotation
sendPulse();          // -> rotate exactly one step

// Servo: angle command, not a pulse count
servo.write(angle);   // 0..180 typical range

The transistor and the flyback diode

A transistor functions like an electronically controlled switch. Its gate is like a water tap: apply voltage to turn the current on or off. Its source is the source of electrons, typically connected to ground, and its drain is where the electrons go, typically connected to the higher potential. NMOS transistors turn on with a positive gate voltage and are used to pull the output down to ground; PMOS transistors turn on with a negative gate voltage and pull the output up to the supply voltage.

A flyback diode safely dissipates the back electromotive force when the motor is powered down. The transistor works like a switch controlled by the microcontroller, allowing current from the battery to flow; the diode protects that switch from the current the motor pushes back.

Rule of thumb from the deck: if a load draws more than a trivial amount of current, switches on and off frequently, or is inductive (like a motor), it is safest to give it its own supply and use the microcontroller only to control it — via the transistor — rather than power it directly. Vibration motors are a case in point: they can easily draw 100 mA, so a transistor plus an external supply is good practice.
You wire a 9 V battery to a DC motor and connect the battery's positive terminal to your microcontroller's 5 V pin “to share power”. What does the lecture's wiring rule say about this?
It is wrong: grounds are shared, never VCC — the motor needs its own supply with only the ground in common
It is correct: both circuits need the same positive rail or the transistor will not switch
It is fine as long as a flyback diode is installed across the motor

Worked example — choosing a motor for a haptic prototype. You are building a wearable that signals the direction of a detected object: left, right or ahead.

1

Ask what the information is. Direction is a three-way categorical signal delivered to the skin. It is not a continuous property like weight or temperature.

2

Pick the channel, then the motor. Three discrete, urgent events map onto the vibrotactile channel, so the answer is vibration motors positioned so that each one is spatially distinguishable. A servo or stepper would be solving position control you do not need.

3

Check the current budget. Each ERM or LRA can easily draw 100 mA, which is more than a microcontroller pin should supply and more than a small board's regulator may tolerate for three of them at once. Give the motors an external supply, switch them with transistors, and tie the grounds together.

4

Protect the switch. Motors are inductive: when a channel switches off, the collapsing field pushes current back. Fit a flyback diode across each motor, and remember the deck's rule — a load that is inductive, draws real current, or switches often gets its own supply.

5

Design the signal, not just the wiring. Use frequency, duration, amplitude, waveform, rhythm and ADSR to make left, right and ahead distinguishable without being annoying. Because of adaptation, give each cue an envelope with a clear attack and release rather than a steady buzz.

Week 7 Recap

Haptics is much wider than vibration

It includes technology to simulate touch, mechanical forces, friction, temperature and much more — and it also includes the passive haptics of the environment you are already in.

Scope of the field

Know which receptor you are writing to

Thermo-, mechano-, noci- and proprioceptors. Vibration addresses mechanoreceptors; weight and resistance address the kinaesthetic system.

Perception

Six knobs and one envelope

Frequency, duration, amplitude, waveform, rhythm and ADSR. Adaptation means a signal that never changes stops being felt.

Vibrotactile design

Motors get their own supply

Transistor as a switch, flyback diode for the back-EMF, common ground and never common VCC — and 200 steps per rotation for a stepper.
