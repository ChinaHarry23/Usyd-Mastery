DEMO101 — Building a Study Hub

Foundations: Scoring and Spacing

Progress is arithmetic on top of honest units: a score that counts answers, a mastery estimate that moves after every attempt, a review schedule that grows geometrically, and an expected score that tells you how much of a result was luck.

Scoring a quiz

A score is a count divided by an opportunity count. Everything else is a choice about weights.

For a chapter with \(n\) entries of which \(c\) were answered correctly, the raw score is the ratio

\[ S = \frac{c}{n}. \]

Chapters in this pack carry six entries each, so one correct answer is worth \(1/6 \approx 0.167\). Splitting the entries by type gives two sub-scores that matter more than the total, because the study page and the foundations page ask different kinds of questions:

\[ S_{\text{study}} = \frac{c_s}{n_s}, \qquad S_{\text{math}} = \frac{c_m}{n_m}. \]

When a course wants a single headline number, it combines the sub-scores with weights. With non-negative weights summing to one, the weighted score is a convex combination and therefore cannot leave the interval between its parts:

\[ M = \sum_{j} w_j S_j, \qquad \sum_j w_j = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \min_j S_j \le M \le \max_j S_j. \]

That bound is a useful sanity check on a dashboard. If a reported mastery sits outside the range of its own components, the weights are not a partition of one, and the headline number is measuring something other than what it claims.

Weighted score

A convex combination of sub-scores. Equal weights reproduce the plain average; unequal weights state a claim about which parts of the course matter more.

Mastery after each attempt

Mastery is not recomputed from scratch. It is nudged, and the size of the nudge is what makes it stable.

Track a mastery estimate \(m \in [0, 1]\) for each concept. A correct recall moves the estimate towards certainty by a fraction \(\alpha\) of the remaining distance; a miss moves it down by a fraction \(\beta\) of its current value:

\[ m^{+} = m + \alpha (1 - m), \qquad m^{-} = m (1 - \beta). \]

Both updates keep the estimate inside \([0,1]\), so no amount of evidence can push mastery below zero or above one. Repeated correct recalls approach the ceiling geometrically:

\[ 1 - m_k = (1-\alpha)^k (1 - m_0) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_k \to 1 \text{ as } k \to \infty. \]

The asymmetry is deliberate. With \(\alpha = 0.3\), three correct recalls from \(m_0 = 0\) reach \(1 - 0.7^3 = 0.657\), while a single miss with \(\beta = 0.2\) takes \(0.657\) down to \(0.526\). Forgetting costs less than remembering earns, which is the right bias for a study tool: the estimate should be slow to punish a bad day and quick to reward a good one.

Why not store the raw counts instead

Raw counts are honest but slow: a concept answered correctly eight times in week one will look weak forever. A nudged estimate forgets gradually, which matches how memory actually behaves.

Spacing the reviews

Reviews are scheduled at growing intervals. The growth rate decides how many sessions a term needs.

Let \(I_0\) be the first interval in days and \(r > 1\) the growth factor, so that review \(k\) happens at interval

\[ I_k = I_0 \, r^{k}. \]

With \(I_0 = 1\) day and \(r = 2\), the sequence runs 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 days, and a term of ninety days is covered by seven reviews. The cumulative time invested in those reviews grows geometrically too, and its total is a finite sum:

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} I_0 r^{k} = I_0 \, \frac{r^{n} - 1}{r - 1}. \]

Solving for the number of reviews needed to reach a target horizon \(T\) inverts the geometric growth into a logarithm:

\[ I_0 r^{k} \ge T \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k \ge \log_r\!\left(\frac{T}{I_0}\right). \]

The logarithm is the reason spacing works at all. Doubling the growth factor does not halve the number of sessions; it removes about one. Going from \(r = 2\) to \(r = 4\) over a ninety-day horizon takes the count from seven reviews to four, and each of the four is doing noticeably more work than its counterpart in the denser schedule.

Logarithmic review count

Reviews needed to span a horizon \(T\) grow as \(\log_r(T/I_0)\). Longer terms cost extra sessions slowly, which is what makes a spaced schedule affordable.

The guessing floor

Four options mean a quarter of the marks are available without knowing anything. Say so out loud before celebrating a score.

Suppose a reader knows \(k\) of the \(n\) entries and guesses on the rest. Each guess is correct with probability \(1/4\), so the expected score is

\[ \mathbb{E}[C] = k + \frac{n - k}{4} = \frac{n}{4} + \frac{3k}{4}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[S] = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{3k}{4n}. \]

With \(n = 6\) entries of four options each, a reader who knows nothing still expects \(1.5\) correct answers, or a score of \(0.25\). Knowing half the material lifts the expectation to

\[ k = 3: \quad \mathbb{E}[C] = 1.5 + 2.25 = 3.75, \qquad \mathbb{E}[S] = 0.625. \]

The gap between the two numbers is the signal: the guessing floor is \(0.25\), and every point above it has to be earned. This is also the argument against scoring schemes that reward a blank answer. If a wrong guess and a blank both score zero, guessing is free and the floor rises; if a wrong guess is penalised by \(1/3\) of a mark, the expected value of a pure guess becomes

\[ \frac{1}{4}(+1) + \frac{3}{4}\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right) = 0, \]

which removes the floor entirely and makes the score mean what readers assume it already means.

Report the floor with the score. Six entries out of six is impressive. Two out of six is the expected result of a coin-flipping reader who is not even reading the options, and a dashboard that presents both numbers the same way is lying by omission.

Worked answer — mastery after five attempts, and a review count. Take \(\alpha = 0.3\), \(\beta = 0.2\) and \(m_0 = 0\). A reader answers a concept: correct, correct, missed, correct, correct. Then decide how many reviews are needed to span a ninety-day term with \(I_0 = 1\) and \(r = 2\).

Step 1: run the updates. Applying \(m^{+} = m + 0.3(1-m)\) and \(m^{-} = 0.8m\) in order:

\[ 0 \to 0.300 \to 0.510 \to 0.408 \to 0.586 \to 0.710. \]

The two correct answers after the miss recover the loss and leave the estimate above its pre-miss value, which is the intended behaviour: evidence accumulates, mistakes fade.

Step 2: check the ceiling. After five attempts the residual distance from certainty is

\[ 1 - 0.710 = 0.290. \]

Compare that with a run of four correct answers and no miss, which would leave \(0.7^4 = 0.240\). The single miss costs about five points of progress and is then partly recovered, which is exactly the trade the parameters were chosen to make.

Step 3: count the reviews. The interval after \(k\) growth steps is \(I_0 r^{k} = 2^{k}\) days. Requiring the last interval to reach the horizon gives

\[ 2^{k} \ge 90 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k \ge \log_2 90 \approx 6.49 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = 7. \]

Counting the first review as day 1, the sessions land on days \(1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128\). Seven of them fall inside the ninety-day term and the eighth does not, so the practical schedule is seven sessions.

Step 4: sanity check. The same computation with \(r = 4\) gives \(k \ge \log_4 90 \approx 3.26\), so the sessions land on days \(1, 4, 16, 64\): four of them inside the term. Twice the growth factor for three fewer sessions, which is worth it only if recall survives the longer gaps — a question for the reader, not for the formula.
