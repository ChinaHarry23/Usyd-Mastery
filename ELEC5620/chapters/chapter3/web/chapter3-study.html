Requirements Engineering: Discovering the Purpose

The lecture opens with a claim that reframes everything else in the unit: software success is the degree to which the software meets the purpose for which it was intended — so requirement modelling is the process of discovering that purpose.

Definitions from the deck

Software success — the degree to which the software meets the purpose for which it was intended. Requirement modelling / engineering — the process of discovering that purpose: identifying stakeholders and their needs, and documenting them in a form that is amenable to analysis, communication and implementation.

Scott W. Ambler, quoted in the deck: “It is the role of project stakeholders to provide requirements, it is the role of developers to understand and implement them.” The lecture pairs this with human-centred design, which “puts the people we are designing for at the heart of the process”.

Two inherent difficulties

  • Stakeholder goals may not be explicit, and may be difficult to articulate.
  • Satisfaction of those goals may be constrained by factors outside the stakeholders' control.

Henry Ford, quoted in the deck

“If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses.” Elicitation records what stakeholders say; modelling is what lets you examine it, communicate it and agree on it. The deck cites Nuseibeh and Easterbrook for that framing.

Benefit of good requirementsWhat it looks like in a project
Clear expectationsBetter reception by customers, because what will be delivered is legible before it is built
Elimination of needless developmentFeatures nobody asked for are cancelled before they are coded
Reduced project riskConflicts and omissions surface while they are still cheap to fix
Actual faults caught earlierThe defect is found in a model, not in an integration test
Faster developmentLess rework and less guessing about intent
Improved maintenanceThe reason a behaviour exists is recorded, not folklore
Simpler formulation of testing strategiesEach requirement names what would demonstrate it
Robust designNon-functional constraints are known before the architecture is fixed
The iron triangle: time, effort (quality) and money. The deck introduces it with the benefits list because requirements work is the cheapest place to move a project's outcome — a defect removed during elicitation never consumes any of the three.
Which list is the deck's list of issues with requirement statements?
Clear expectations, reduced risk, faster development, improved maintenance
Ambiguity, incompleteness, conflict, difference in priority, and perceived stakeholder urgency
Eliciting, modelling, communicating, agreeing, evolving

Three Approaches to Specifying Requirements

The lecture compares three ways of writing requirements down. They are not rivals: the deck's conclusion is that a pragmatic combination of all three is usually the best approach.

ApproachWhat the system isViewWhere it hurts
“Ad hoc” analysisA mixture of different kinds of requirement — what, how, how wellInformal prose and sketchesUnsystematic and error prone: omissions, conflicts, inconsistencies, ambiguities
Feature analysisA collection of desired properties (features)Capability-basedStatic: no clear indication of how features are used or realised, so conflicts are hard to detect
Use case analysisA set of functional capabilities that realise desired stakeholder goalsOperationalAbstract by design: it needs interaction modelling before it becomes a design

Only one of the three is usually chosen deliberately. The deck is explicit that the ad hoc approach is “usually the most common starting point” — which is exactly why the other two are worth learning. Feature analysis gives you a structured inventory of capability; use case analysis gives you the operational story of who achieves what, and when.

A team writes requirements as a long prose document that mixes desired behaviour, an implementation decision and a response-time target. Which approach are they using, and what is the main risk?
Ad hoc analysis, and the risk is omissions, conflicts and ambiguities that are hard to detect later
Feature analysis, and the risk is that the feature model will not support a product family
Use case analysis, and the risk is that no actor has been identified

Feature-Oriented Modelling

Feature modelling is the capability-based approach: it lists what the product can do, and how those capabilities may be combined, before anyone asks how they work.

Feature

A prominent and user-visible aspect, quality or characteristic of a software system; a property of a domain concept that is relevant to some domain stakeholder and is used to discriminate between concept instances (Czarnecki & Eisenecker, quoted in the deck).

Feature-Oriented Domain Analysis (FODA)

Originated by the Software Engineering Institute in 1990 for analysing domains, their concepts and their relationships. The deck notes the similarity to class modelling — but more general. Requirements analysis is treated as a kind of domain analysis: the domain of the system under construction.

Feature model, feature diagram

A feature model is a compact representation of all the products of a software product line (SPL) in terms of features. Feature diagrams are its visual representation, used throughout product-line development and often as input to generate documentation, schema definitions or code snippets.

Parent–child relationshipNotationMeaning
MandatoryFilled circle at the parent end of the edgeThe child feature must be selected.
OptionalHollow circle at the parent end of the edgeThe child feature can be selected or not selected.
OrFilled arc across the sibling edgesAt least one of the sub-features must be selected.
Alternative (xor)Hollow arc across the sibling edgesExactly one of the sub-features must be selected.
Cross-tree constraintDashed arrow labelled with the conditionA dependency between features in different branches, e.g. credit card → high security.

The e-shop example from the tutorial. The root feature E-Shop identifies the product line. The system implements catalogues, payment modules, security policies and optional search tools. It must implement a high-standard or standard security policy (choose exactly one); it supports bank transfer, credit card or both payment modules; and a cross-tree constraint forces any system that includes credit-card payment to implement the high-security policy.

Why architects care: the deck's summary notes that feature modelling is particularly useful as a starting point for requirements work and for cases involving significant variability — that is, product families — which makes it “of major importance to system architects”. Its limit is that it is static: it never shows a sequence of interactions.
In a feature diagram, three sibling sub-features are grouped under a hollow arc. What has the modeller asserted?
At least one of the three must be selected
Exactly one of the three must be selected, and the others must not be
All three are optional, so any subset may be selected

The Use Case Framework: Actors, Goals, Boundary

The lecture's definition is deliberately small: a use case is an interaction with a system for the purpose of achieving a clearly defined goal — something which is of value to the actor.

Two terms the exam will ask for

Actor — an entity (person or system) which interacts with a system to achieve one or more goals. Use case — a collection of possible interactions between actors and a system relating to one or more goals.

What a use case gives you

  • Context for the requirements
  • A basis for agreement between stakeholders and developers
  • A collection of possible scenarios, not one fixed path

Where it sits

The deck's key phrase: a use case “occurs on the boundary between the informal (i.e., what users want) and formal (i.e., what engineers implement)”. That is why the diagram is the easy part and the specification is the hard part.

ElementNotationNaming rule from the tutorial
ActorStick figure, outside the boundaryNamed by a noun; plays a role in the business
Use caseEllipse (or circle) inside the boundaryNamed verb + noun (or noun phrase) — “Do Something”
Association / communication linkSolid line joining actor to use caseShows participation; the actor and use case communicate using messages
System boundaryRectangle around the use casesDefined by the requirements document; represents the scope of the system
Actors are roles, not people. The tutorial's example: a professor can be an instructor in one system and a researcher in another — two roles, two systems. An actor also may be a person, an organisation, or an outside system that interacts with your application; what makes it an actor is that it is an external object that produces or consumes data.
Two structural rules worth memorising: every actor must be linked to at least one use case, while some use cases may not be linked to any actor; and a use case may be extended. The second rule is what makes the next two sections necessary.
A payroll system automatically reports superannuation contributions to the tax office's reporting service. Should the tax office service appear on the use case diagram, and as what?
Yes — as a second actor, because an outside system that interacts with yours is an actor
Yes — as a use case named “Report contributions” inside the boundary
No — the diagram only shows human users of the system

Anatomy of a Use Case: Header and Scenarios

The UML ellipse is a shorthand. The deliverable is the specification behind it, and the lecture follows a slightly modified version of Alistair Cockburn's pragmatic template.

The deck's header example: Withdraw Cash from ATM

NameGoalLevelPreconditionSuccess conditionFailure conditionTrigger
Withdraw cash from ATM Obtain cash System User has ATM-readable card Cash dispensed to user Cash not dispensed despite valid request Insertion of card in ATM

One header per use case, followed by a collection of scenarios corresponding to successful runs through it. The deck gives this single success scenario:

1. User logs into ATM 2. User selects withdrawal service 3. User specifies amount 4. ATM dispenses specified amount 5. User logs out

Cockburn's five structural commitments

  • Use cases are motivated by — and defined around — actor goals.
  • Use cases start with an action by an actor: the trigger.
  • Use cases always terminate with a clear result: the goal has or has not been achieved.
  • There are success scenarios (usually one “main” success scenario) and failure scenarios — complications.
  • In general, each step in a scenario can succeed or fail.
How failures are named and where they attach: the condition that led to a step heads the corresponding failure scenario, and failure scenarios branch off from the point of failure. A failure scenario is therefore indexed by the step it interrupts — 5.a, 7.c — not numbered as a separate flow.
A use case specification says the failure scenario is “4.a {ATM has insufficient cash on hand}”, containing “1. Notify user, 2. Log out user”. What does the 4.a tell you?
It is the extension scenario for the condition that can interrupt step 4 of the main success scenario
It is an optional fourth step that runs only when the ATM has cash
It is a nested use case named “4.a”

Include, Extend and Generalization

Three relationships between use cases carry most of the marks in this week's questions, and they are routinely confused. Each one answers a different question.

RelationshipQuestion it answersLine & arrowheadEffect on the base use case
«include» Which lower-level use case does a step of this scenario actually represent? Dashed arrow pointing to the included (child) use case The base use case relies on the child's functionality: the main use case includes actions from it
«extend» What optional behaviour may be woven in at a defined point? Dashed arrow pointing to the base use case An optional step is added “without affecting the specification of the original use case”
Generalization Which use case is a specialisation of which? Solid line with a hollow triangle pointing at the parent The child is an enhancement of the parent and inherits its behaviour
The arrowhead is the exam trap. For extend, the arrow leaves the extending use case and its tip points at the base use case: in the deck's ATM example the arrow runs from Update Branch Balance to Withdraw Cash, stereotyped «extend» and labelled with the condition [service successful] and the extension point X1. For include it is the other way round: the arrow leaves Withdraw Cash and points at Login. When in doubt, ask which use case needs the other.

Extension points

Extending requires the notion of extension points in the use case — points where an extension can be woven in. The base use case advertises them (the deck's Withdraw Cash lists extension points: X1) and the extending use case names the point and its condition.

Why nesting earns its keep

A step in a scenario may represent a sub-use case at a lower level, with its own sub-goals and actors. Login is the canonical example: {ATM card inserted}, then 1. Ask user PIN, 2. User enters PIN, 3. Validate PIN. Pulling it out means every use case that needs a login does not restate it.

The deck's own summary of where this leads: use cases are “useful as a front end to the design phase” and “typically complemented by UML interaction modeling (e.g., sequence diagrams)” — with the warning that the two are not the same: use cases are more abstract.

Worked example — choosing the right relationship. Three proposals for the secure-access system of the tutorial's ad hoc diagram. Decide the relationship for each, and say why.

1

“Every access attempt must end by writing an entry into the door log.” The logging step is not optional and it is not an enhancement — the scenario cannot complete without it. Model the log write as a lower-level use case, Record Access Attempt, reached by «include» from Enter Secure Room. The arrow points at Record Access Attempt.

2

“When a person in the secure area presses the panic button, the system sends a message to the police instead of sounding the alarm.” The main success scenario is unaffected; this is behaviour woven in at a defined point. Model Summon Police as an «extend» of Occupy Secure Room, with condition [panic button pressed] and an extension point advertised on the base use case. The arrow points at Occupy Secure Room.

3

“A contractor may open the door with a one-time code as well as with a fingerprint.” The one-time-code path is a specialisation of the same goal, not an optional add-on and not a sub-step. Model Enter Secure Room by One-Time Code as a generalization of Enter Secure Room, with the hollow triangle pointing at the parent.

4

The one-line test. If the base cannot complete its goal without it, it is include. If the base is complete and correct without it, and it is triggered by a condition at a defined point, it is extend. If it replaces the base while achieving the same goal, it is generalization.

In a diagram, a dashed arrow runs from Update Branch Balance to Withdraw Cash, stereotyped «extend». What does this assert?
Withdraw Cash includes Update Branch Balance, so withdrawing always updates the balance
Update Branch Balance may be woven into Withdraw Cash at extension point X1 when its condition holds
Update Branch Balance generalizes Withdraw Cash

Scenario Walkthroughs and Extension Scenarios

A use case is a collection of possible scenarios. Walking one through step by step is how you find the failure conditions you would otherwise ship.

Success and failure scenarios

The flow starts from the precondition. Step 1 runs; if it succeeds, step 2 runs; if a failure condition holds at step 2, control branches to STEP F2.1, which itself ends in the goal being achieved or not. The deck's diagram ends in exactly three ways: SUCCESS, FAILURE, or SUCCESS after recovery.

Extension scenarios

An extension scenario is initiated when a failure condition holds for a step in the scenario under consideration. In the deck's ATM example, {Insufficient cash on hand} branches from step 4: 1. Notify user, 2. Log out user.

Keeping it simple and agile (the deck's own advice): a use case should be easy for stakeholders to understand so that they can reason about it; avoid implementation detail and keep it informal, because not all stakeholders are technical experts. An easy-to-read textual form beats a precise one nobody reads.
Worked walkthrough — the deck's AI health agent. The lecture's running example is a patient consulting an AI health agent: the actor is the Patient, the level is user-goal, the main success scenario runs from the patient initiating the conversation to the agent providing treatment advice or booking an appointment, and three extension scenarios are given: 5.a {unclear or incomplete description} (ask for clarification until sufficient information is gathered), 7.c {symptoms are critical/emergency} (call emergency services immediately instead of booking), and 8.a {Patient does not understand advice} (rephrase and offer educational resources). Step 9 is a nested use case in its own right: AI Agent books an appointment with a doctor, with its own three steps.
Why does the deck insist that in general each step in a scenario can succeed or fail?
Because every step is a potential branch point, so enumerating conditions step by step gives the failure scenarios a systematic structure
Because failure scenarios must be nested inside the main success scenario as extra numbered steps
Because each step must then be decomposed into its own use case in the diagram

The Use Case Diagram as a Blueprint

The tutorial's framing: a use case diagram is a graphical description of how a user might interact with a system — a high-level blueprint that shows the use cases and the different types of users, and a successful one describes the activities and variants used to reach the goal.

What the diagram is good at

  • Providing a higher-level view of a system
  • Showing the use cases and the different types of user a system has
  • Being a high-level blueprint: it should describe the activities and variants used to reach the goal
  • Being read by non-technical stakeholders, which is why the UML rendering is only a shorthand

What it cannot do

  • Specify anything: the important and hard part is specifying the use case itself
  • Show ordering within a scenario — that needs interaction modelling such as a sequence diagram
  • Replace the textual template; the ellipse carries a name, not the goal, precondition, trigger or failure conditions
  • Show non-functional constraints, which have no notational home on the diagram

Tutorial exercise — the ad hoc diagram, drawn properly. The tutorial's ad hoc example is the secure access room: a room in which only authorised people are allowed to linger, with a fingerprint scanner, a fingerprint database, a door, a panic button, a horn, a motion detector, a window, and a police station. Turn its informal specification into use case structure.

1

Fix the boundary. The system under consideration is the access control software. The fingerprint database is an external system it consults; the police station is a second actor; the door, horn, scanner and motion detector are devices the system drives, not actors. Anything that only produces or consumes data across the boundary stays outside it.

2

Name the actors by role. Authorised User (the person who opens the door and scans a finger), Last Person Leaving (who pushes the Enable button), and Police Station (which receives the telephone message). One person can play more than one of these roles — that is the point of naming roles rather than people.

3

Write use cases as verb + noun. Enter Secure Room, Enable System, Occupancy Violation Detected, Summon Police, Identify by Fingerprint. Each is a collection of possible interactions relating to a goal; the goal of Enter Secure Room is to be inside without triggering the alarm.

4

Attach the relationships. Enter Secure Room «include»s Identify by Fingerprint, because the main scenario cannot complete without a valid scan inside 30 seconds. Summon Police «extend»s Occupancy Violation Detected and also «extend»s Occupy Secure Room at the panic-button extension point, since it is optional behaviour triggered by a condition. If a visitor entry mode is added later, it is a generalization of Enter Secure Room.

5

Recover the numbers the prose hid. The informal specification already contains hard constraints: 30 seconds after the door opens, 45 seconds to leave after enabling, and an 8-digit alarm id registered with the police at installation. These are requirements, not decoration — put them in the use case header's preconditions and success/failure conditions so they can be tested.

6

Say what the diagram still does not give you. Ordering. “Scan within 30 seconds after opening the door” is a temporal constraint that a use case diagram cannot express. That is exactly why the deck says use case design is complemented by UML interaction modelling, and why the two are not the same thing.

Traceability, Evolution and Agentic Requirements

The deck's closing argument is that requirement modelling is a small but crucial step in requirements engineering, because it is what enables automated support for traceability between requirements and other development artefacts.

Why the informal specification is not sufficient (the deck's six reasons). Informal prose makes it difficult to: identify potential conflicts and inconsistencies between requirements; determine interdependencies between individual requirements; ensure all requirements are covered; understand precisely what needs to be implemented; trace requirements to implementation and vice versa; and provide a basis for comprehensive testing. The deck adds a practical requirement of its own: you also need requirements in electronic form, for easier manipulation, searching, linking and tracing.
Requirements engineering activityWhat it producesWhere modelling helps
Eliciting requirementsAnswers to: what problems need to be solved, where is the system boundary, who are the stakeholders and what are their goalsA use case model makes the boundary and the actors explicit and argues back at the elicitation
Modelling and analysing requirementsModels that can be checked for conflict, omission and dependencyFeature diagrams expose combination rules; scenarios expose branch coverage
Communicating requirementsSomething every stakeholder can read and reason aboutAn ellipse-and-actor diagram plus a short textual scenario
Agreeing requirementsA shared, signed-off understandingExtension points and failure conditions are the places disagreement hides
Evolving requirementsChange that does not break what was already agreed«extend» adds behaviour without touching the original specification
Functional vs non-functional, and why the split is not clean

A functional requirement describes what a software system should do and relates to its functionalities. A non-functional requirement places constraints on how the system will do so — performance characteristics such as accessibility, reliability, latency or accuracy. The deck's caution: for very many systems these are not separable concerns. “Compute the optimal route for a data packet” and “compute the optimal route for a data packet in 4 µsec” can be two very different systems.

Requirements for agentic software

The deck's contemporary example is an AI-native healthcare agency: a clinical director who owns judgement, safety and approvals; an intake agent that collects symptoms and context; an evidence agent that retrieves approved clinical guidance; a care agent that drafts next steps and prepares referrals; and an operations agent that updates records, scheduling and follow-up queues. Its running goal is to triage patient enquiries and coordinate care without removing clinician accountability. Two consequences follow for requirement modelling.

Functional and non-functional, restated for agents

  • Functional: the agent shall check reported symptoms for emergency warning signs; generate content based on natural language; converse with a human.
  • Non-functional: low latency; a high-accuracy model for NLP and computer vision; a user-friendly computer–human interface.

What a use case model must emphasise

  • Bounded autonomy and observable outcomes, not a fixed sequence of interactions
  • Actor goal stated as a business outcome: “deliver an evidence-backed weekly brief”, not “run the research agent”
  • Authority: what the agent may read, write, send, purchase or change — capability separated from permission
  • Success conditions defined as observable evidence, not as an exact reasoning path
  • Failure and uncertainty modelled as normal branches: missing data, conflicting evidence, unavailable tools, low-confidence results
  • Human checkpoints showing when the agent must ask, obtain approval, hand off or stop
The deck's worked agentic use case: Assess and coordinate patient care. Goal — safe next step prepared without delay; level — user goal; primary actor — patient; preconditions — identity and consent confirmed, approved guidance and scheduling tools available; success postcondition — clinician-approved guidance sent and record updated; failure postcondition — no advice sent, clinician alerted with reason. In its main success scenario the agent prepares the patient response but does not send it until the clinician has reviewed and approved — the checkpoint is written into the scenario, not left to policy.
Which of these is a non-functional requirement for the AI health agent?
The agent shall check reported symptoms for emergency warning signs
The agent shall respond to a patient enquiry with low latency
The agent shall converse with the patient and generate content in natural language

Week 3 Recap

Success is meeting the intended purpose

Requirement modelling is the process of discovering that purpose: identify stakeholders and their needs, and document them in a form amenable to analysis, communication and implementation.

The premise

Three approaches, combined in practice

Ad hoc is informal and error prone but the usual starting point; feature analysis is capability-based and static; use case analysis is operational. A pragmatic combination of all three is often best.

Method

Actor → goal → use case

An actor is an entity that interacts with the system to achieve goals; a use case is a collection of possible interactions relating to one or more of them; the diagram is only the shorthand.

Core framework

include, extend, generalize

include points at the required child, extend points at the base and is woven in at an extension point under a condition, generalization points a hollow triangle at the parent.

Notation trap

Check yourself

Can you write the header of one use case from memory — name, goal, level, precondition, success condition, failure condition, trigger — and then give its main success scenario plus two extension scenarios?

Prepare for the lab

Laboratory 3 applies all three approaches to your project topic in sequence: an ad hoc pass for flexible definition, a feature-oriented pass to refine requirements into concrete features, then a use case pass to check the features meet user expectations.
