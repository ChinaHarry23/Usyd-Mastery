From Waterfall to Agile

The deck starts where the whole unit started: with the process the models were supposed to serve. Waterfall organises work by stage; agile organises it by iteration. Everything else in Week 8 follows from that change of axis.

The waterfall model, as the lecture describes it: development activities are divided into stages, each stage is owned by a separate team of specialists, and the work is passed from team to team using documents. At the end of all the stages you have a software product ready to ship — and each specialist team is then assigned to a new product.

Strengths the waterfall model does have

  • Clear stages and explicit handoffs
  • A document trail between specialist teams
  • Easy to plan once the requirements stop moving

Why the deck moves on

  • Rapid-fire change makes a frozen plan expensive
  • Handoffs lose information that no document recovered
  • A large feature set can raise the customer's cost rather than lower it
Say this instead of “waterfall is wrong”: waterfall optimises for a stable specification and specialist teams; agile optimises for change and for a cross-functional team. The week's argument is that the second assumption is the one that holds in a market where new technology, new players and new requirements keep arriving — which is why the lecture answers its own question with “small is beautiful”.
The Week 8 lecture lists agile practices and then says “will you use every Agile practice? Maybe not…”. What is required instead?
Every practice on the list, because Scrum mandates them
Agile values — the practices are a menu, not a checklist
Nothing: if you run iterations you are agile by definition

Agile Values and the Scrum Frame

Week 8's actual subject is models, but the deck spends its first half establishing the process those models live inside. Read every Scrum element below as a constraint on your modelling.

Definitions

Agile development is adaptive (teams and process stay flexible under rapid-fire change), iterative/incremental (working products in stages, a growing set of completed and working software) and people-oriented (team organisation and process support good people, the most important ingredient to project success).

What the week saysWeek 2–7 idea it changesWhat you actually do about it
Keep things smallWhole-system models drawn before any codeModel one feature or one iteration's slice at a time
Deliver partially-completed software frequentlyA single design that must be complete before implementationKeep only the parts of the design the next increment uses
Talk to the customer oftenRequirements captured once and frozenElaborate a small set of functional requirements per iteration
Write more code than documentationModels as the deliverableModel where the model answers a question no one can answer by reading code
Everyone on the team learns togetherHandoff between specialist teamsMake the model legible to the whole team, not only to modellers

Three roles

Product Owner owns the rate of investment, is available for the whole product development period, gets answers to requirements questions, talks with customers about priorities and keeps the Product Backlog current. Scrum Master is the rules guardian, coach, remover of impediments and referee. Team Member does the work: a normal team is 5 to 9 people in a single location.

Three lists

Product Backlog — every feature and sub-feature known to be needed, in priority order, open to new items at any time. Sprint Backlog — the tasks the team committed to for this iteration. Impediments List — the obstacles surfaced by the Daily Scrum.

Three meetings

Planning (release and Sprint) at the start. Daily Scrum every day: 15 minutes, no longer, everyone speaks, and no problem solving in the meeting. Sprint Review at the end, followed by a retrospective that is “like a post-mortem, but it isn't dead yet”.

Estimation rule

Managers and customers set priority with the Product Owner; the team estimates size and effort. And the rule that follows: even if managers and customers do not like the estimates, they are not allowed to change them.

✅ Still important in an agile project

  • Documentation — but if it is the only kind of communication in your project, it is not good.
  • A development plan — in a different format: the schedule is built from iterations, and each iteration lets the team incorporate what it learned instead of following a non-adaptive plan.
  • Real working code — more valuable than documents because it is less ambiguous; documents are easy to leave something out of and easy to misinterpret.

⚠️ The failure modes to name

  • “Development in iterations” without the rest is not agile: the deck's version is don't do Agile, be Agile.
  • A document that nobody reads. If the artefact is a PBI at all, it has to contribute value — otherwise leave it out “because you are Agile”.
  • Iteration estimates that are too pessimistic: the burndown chart reaches zero well before the end of the iteration.
A Scrum team finishes Sprint Planning and management then asks for one extra screen to be added during the Sprint. What does the Week 8 material say should happen?
It is added to the Sprint, since priorities can change at any time
It goes on the Product Backlog, not into the running Sprint
The Product Owner re-estimates the whole backlog first

Modelling Without the Ceremony

This is the pivot of the week. An agile project still models; what it refuses is the model that exists only to be a document. The test is not “is this diagram complete?” but “which question does this diagram answer, and who is going to read it?”

Working rule

Lightweight modelling: produce the smallest model that answers the question in front of the team, at the moment it is asked, and expect to change it in the next iteration. A model is kept when it earns its place — as a PBI whose value is real, as a shared understanding the code cannot express, or as an input a tool consumes. A model that nobody reads is left out.

Question the team is askingSmallest model that answers itWhat would be ceremony
Who uses this, and to what end?One user story, or one use case with its main scenarioA full requirements specification written before the iteration starts
What are we building in this increment?Product Backlog items in priority order, with the team's effort estimatesA document that restates the backlog and that nobody reads
Is the structure still sound?The two or three classes the increment touches, with their associations and multiplicitiesRe-drawing every class in the system for each Sprint
Does the ordering logic still hold?One state machine for the object whose lifecycle changedAn activity diagram for behaviour that has not changed since Week 6
Can this be deployed?One deployment diagram with the nodes the increment actually usesA deployment diagram listing hardware nobody will provision
The three agile modelling tests. (1) Purpose — name the decision this model changes; if there is none, do not draw it. (2) Audience — name who reads it; if the answer is “nobody”, the lecture's own advice is to leave it out. (3) Lifetime — say when it will be updated or deleted. A model with no answer to the third test becomes the stale artefact that agile development is accused of producing.

Worked example — trimming a model to fit one Sprint. The increment is: “Registered users can manage their notification preferences”, from the Week 8 tutorial's user-story exercise. Apply the three tests to each candidate model and keep only what survives.

1

Write the story first, at the size of an increment. “As a registered user, I want to manage my notification preferences so that I can control how and when I receive updates.” The story is a PBI: it is a customer feature, small enough for one Sprint, and its estimate comes from the team.

2

Class model — purpose test. The question is whether a preference belongs to the user or to a separate Subscription. That decision changes the database and the API, so the model earns its place: one class box with User, one with NotificationPreference, an association with multiplicity 1 to 0..*, and the invariant that a preference is unique per user and channel. Everything else in the class diagram is ceremony for this Sprint.

3

State machine — purpose test. A preference is default, customised, or muted, and only the transition from default to customised requires an event. That is three states and four transitions: small, and it settles the question “what happens when a user clears every channel?” which the story does not answer on its own.

4

Audience and lifetime tests. Both models are read by the whole team during the Sprint, and both are updated in the same change as the code. A use case document restating the user story, and a sequence diagram for the unchanged login flow, fail both tests: no reader, and no maintenance plan.

5

Say what you deliberately left out. The deployment view, the package structure of unrelated subsystems, and the activity diagram for account creation. The retrospective can ask whether a missing model caused rework; the answer decides what to keep next Sprint, which is the iterative part of iterative modelling.

Which artefact would agile modelling keep, according to the Week 8 material?
A design document written for completeness and filed for the unit's records
A class model the code generator consumes and the team updates with each change
A diagram that restates the current source code in boxes

Executable and Testable Models

Agile's preference for working software over documents (with the lecture's caveat that documents still matter) has a direct modelling consequence: a model that can be executed or checked is worth more than one that can only be read.

Definition

An executable model is precise enough that a tool can run it — state machines with triggers, guards and effects; operations with pre- and post-conditions; OCL invariants. A testable model is one whose claims are attached to checks that fail when the claim is broken. Both replace a prose promise with something that produces evidence.

Model elementExecutable readingTest that fails when it is wrong
State and transitionTrigger, guard and effect are all present, so the transition can fireFire the trigger in the source state and assert the target state
GuardA boolean over the object's attributesFire the trigger with the guard false and assert nothing happens
Association multiplicity1 and 0..* are cardinality constraintsInsert a second child where the model says one, and expect rejection
Invariant (OCL)A predicate evaluated after every operationConstruct an object that violates it and expect the check to fail
Pre- and post-conditionContract on an operationCall the operation out of contract and assert the error, not the result
Acceptance testA scenario the requirements already namedRun the scenario end to end and compare with the expected outcome
Why the lecture's requirements advice matters here. The Week 8 deck says that for some key requirements you should create acceptance tests at the same time as you write the requirements. In modelling terms that means the requirement arrives with its test, the test constrains the model, and the model stops being an opinion: a Sprint is “done” only when the model's claims and the acceptance tests agree.
The trade this section is asking you to make: express a constraint in the model only when the model is the cheaper place to settle it. A guard that stops an illegal transition is cheap in the model and expensive to rediscover in code; a validation that one line of code answers is the reverse.

The Model-Driven Toolchain

Agile modelling is not done by hand in a drawing tool and then forgotten. The model sits in a chain of automated steps, and every step in that chain is where an iteration can be kept honest.

StageWhat must be true for the next stage to workCost of skipping it
ModelThe model answers one named question and is legible to the whole teamThe generator emits code nobody asked for
RepositoryEvery element has one home namespace; two packages never share a nameDuplicated elements and silently diverging copies
ValidateGuards, invariants and multiplicities are all expressed, not impliedThe defect surfaces as a runtime failure after the Sprint review
GenerateThe transformation is repeatable and its output is not hand-edited without a markerThe next regeneration overwrites somebody's fix
TestEach acceptance criterion has a test that can fail“Done” means “the demo worked once”

Jira as the management layer. The Week 8 tutorial and lecture both point at Jira Software for running Scrum on the project: create the project, start from the Scrum template, keep the backlog and the board, and track the Sprint with a burndown chart. The model repository holds the design; Jira holds the commitments, the estimates and the evidence that the increment is finished. An epic, a story and a theme are the three levels at which that work is named, and a user story is the unit of work: an informal, general explanation of a software feature, simpler than a use case, written from the user's perspective as “As a <role>, I want to <goal> so that <benefit>”.
A team regenerates code from the model and loses a fix that a developer made by hand last Sprint. Which stage of the toolchain failed?
Validate — the model should have rejected the inconsistency
Generate — the transformation assumed output was not hand-edited
Deploy — the increment was shipped before the merge

The Model–Code Round Trip

Generation is only half of the loop. The other half is what happens when the code teaches you something the model did not know — which, in an iterative process, is most Sprints.

Forward: model → code

The model is the source. A transformation emits class skeletons, state dispatch, schema and configuration. Because the transformation is repeatable, regenerating after a model change is cheap — that cheapness is what makes modelling affordable inside a two-week Sprint.

Reverse: code → model

Implementation always discovers something: an edge case, a constraint the model missed, an operation that turned out to belong elsewhere. The reverse step is deliberate — the learning is folded back into the model in the same change, not left in the code as a private truth.

The consistency rule that makes the loop safe

A round trip is only usable when three things are true of it: the transformation is deterministic (same model, same output), the model is the single source for everything the generator owns, and every manual edit is either moved into the model or placed in a region the generator is told to leave alone. Break any of the three and the loop becomes a race between the tool and the developer.

What the round trip buys an agile team: the same two things agile already asks for. Faster feedback — a model that generates and runs can be corrected in minutes, where a document is corrected in a meeting. And one shared truth — the lecture's reason for preferring working code is that it is less ambiguous than a document; a model that is kept in step with the code inherits that property instead of competing with it.

Tutorial exercise — planning one Sprint in Jira and in the model. The Sprint goal is: “A customer can track an order from the store's order-processing screen.” Work the Week 8 tutorial's backlog and board material together with the model repository.

1

Write the story, then split it. “As a customer, I want to enter my tracking serial number and see the current shipping status so that I know when to expect the delivery.” Split into PBIs: the tracking lookup, the status display, and the error case for an unknown serial number. Each PBI gets an effort estimate from the team, and the priorities are set by the Product Owner with the customer.

2

Sort the Sprint with MoSCoW. Must: serial-number lookup and status display. Should: the unknown-serial message. Could: a delivery estimate in days. Would like: a map of the courier's progress. Only the must-haves and should-haves are forecast into the 2–4 week Sprint; the rest stay on the Product Backlog.

3

Model only the must-haves. A package view with Track Order, Order Processing and Shipping, and the dependencies the tutorial's Order Processing subsystem needs: <<access>> between Track Order and Order Processing in both directions, <<import>> from Shipping into Track Order, and the Track Order dependency on the UI Framework.

4

Add the executable part. The tracking request has states — submitted, looking up, found, not found — with a guard on “serial number is well formed”. That machine is what the acceptance test drives: a well-formed serial number reaches found or not found, and a malformed one never leaves submitted.

5

Track it, then reflect. Draw the Sprint on the board, watch the burndown each day, and hold the 15-minute Daily Scrum for obstacles only. If the burndown goes up because a PBI was under-estimated, acknowledge it early rather than padding the next estimate — and if the model was the thing that was wrong, that is a round-trip correction, not a code-only fix.

Where does a discovery made while coding belong in an agile model-driven process?
In the code only — the model was agreed at planning time
Back into the model, in the same change as the code fix
Into the retrospective notes, for the next release

Modelling a Sprint: Packages and Deployment

The Week 8 tutorial puts two diagram types in front of you. Both are organisational rather than algorithmic, which is exactly why they survive an iterative process: they are cheap to redraw and they answer questions the code cannot.

Package diagram

Package diagrams structure high-level system elements to simplify complex class diagrams by grouping classes. Packages are drawn as file folders and can be used on any UML diagram; a package is a collection of logically related UML elements and represents a namespace. Names must be unique within a system, but classes inside different packages may share a name. The atomic modules of nested packages are usually class diagrams.

Deployment diagram

A deployment diagram consists of nodes and the relationships between them. It describes the structure of the system at runtime: the configuration of hardware and how software is deployed into the network structure, giving a static view of the deployment. It is used to visualise the topology of the physical components, to understand distributed systems, and there is only one deployment diagram for a system model. The parameters it controls are performance, scalability, maintainability and portability.

ElementNotationWhat it lets you check in a Sprint review

Deployment versus component diagrams. The tutorial draws the contrast explicitly: most UML diagrams deal with the logical components of a system, whereas the deployment diagram focuses on the hardware topology — the hardware components on which software components are deployed. The two are closely related: a component diagram describes components and how they are deployed, and the deployment diagram names the machines they land on.

Week 8 Recap

Iteration replaces stage

Waterfall divides work into stages owned by specialist teams and joined by documents. Agile delivers working software in short timeboxed iterations from one cross-functional team.

Process shift

Lightweight modelling

Model the smallest thing that answers the question in front of the team. Three tests: purpose, audience, lifetime. A model nobody reads is left out.

Modelling rule

Executable and testable

Triggers, guards and effects make a model runnable; acceptance tests written with the requirements make it checkable. Both are evidence, not opinion.

Evidence

The round trip

Generate from the model, discover in the code, fold the learning back into the model in the same change. Deterministic transformation, one source, marked manual edits.

Consistency
The one-sentence answer for an exam question on agile modelling: agile modelling keeps models that earn their place by answering a question a reader actually has, makes them executable and testable so they produce evidence, and keeps them in step with the code through a round trip that runs inside the iteration — which is the only way a model survives rapid-fire change.

Check yourself

Can you say what agile changes about the unit of planning, name the three roles, three lists and three meetings, and state the three tests a model has to pass to be kept?

Prepare for the tutorial

Bring a package diagram for one subsystem and a deployment diagram for one environment. Be ready to justify every dependency arrow with the tutorial's rule: changes here can force changes there.
