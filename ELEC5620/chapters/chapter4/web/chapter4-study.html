Why Architecture Decays

Architecture is the first artefact that has to survive contact with a changing system. Week 4 opens with the failure mode it exists to prevent.

Architectural decay

The gradual corruption of a system's architecture through seemingly minor fixes and modifications. It is caused by two difficulties: difficulties in perceiving an architecture, and difficulties in enforcing architectural decisions. To avoid decay it must at least be possible to easily recognise a software architecture and the design intent behind it.

Architecture is about understanding — both the problem and the solution. The deck makes the point with a producer–consumer system: as source code it is a wall of SC_MODULE, ports, sensitivity lists and links; as a model it is three boxes and two relationships. Understanding requires abstraction ⇒ modelling.

Architecture — ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010

“[the] fundamental concepts or properties of a system in its environment embodied in its elements, relationships, and in the principles of its design and evolution”. An architecture description is “[a] work product used to express an architecture”.

Phrase in the definitionWhat it commits you to
“fundamental”Abstract: irrelevant information is hidden or removed. An architecture is defined as much by what it leaves out as by what it shows.
“elements … relationships”Encompasses both structure and behaviour — not just a box-and-line picture.
“principles of its design and evolution”Guidelines (and rules) for the detailed design that follows, including the rules that govern later change.
Architecture descriptionA work product: the document, model or diagram set that expresses the architecture to someone else.
Architectural principles

General design principles or invariants that hold across the system — the statements that let a reviewer decide whether a proposed change is admissible. The deck's examples:

  • “There shall be no single point of failure in components deemed critical.”
  • “Fault recovery in the system is effected by means of cascading levels of restart.”
  • “At the highest level of description, the lifecycle of any component in the system is defined as a progression through a series of explicit operating modes.”
  • “Mode switches of a component are always driven by the designated external controller of that component.”

Architecture cannot be enforced
Exam framing: decay is not the same as change. A system that evolves on purpose, guided by its recorded principles, is healthy. Decay is change that is locally reasonable and globally destructive — and it is diagnosable by asking a new team member to describe the architecture from the artefacts alone.
A team fixes three defects by adding direct calls between two modules that the architecture says must not know about each other. Two years later nobody can say where the boundary actually runs. What has happened?
The architecture evolved, and evolution means the design changes as requirements arrive
Architectural decay: the architecture was corrupted by a series of locally reasonable fixes
Nothing has happened, because the code still compiles and the tests still pass

Design Space, Constitution and Evolution

Architecture earns its place by what it removes. It is a high-level design decision that constrains and guides every more detailed decision made after it.

An architecture reduces the available design space

High-level design that constrains and guides more detailed design: fewer design choices, to facilitate consistency and reduce complexity. Requirements open a solution space; one architectural decision — “a single computer” versus “a distributed system” — eliminates whole families of solutions before any of them is designed in detail.

PropertyPoint-to-point architecture“Mediator” architecture
Links for \(n\) participantsEach participant may talk to each peer: up to \(n(n-1)/2\) linksEach participant talks only to the mediator: \(n\) links
What a component must knowEvery peer it communicates with, and their protocolsOnly the mediator's interface
Adding a participantTouches every existing participantRegisters one new connection
Effect on evolutionA change ripples across the graphA change is localised behind the mediator
CostSimple while \(n\) is smallThe mediator becomes a critical component and a potential bottleneck
Architecture as a constitution

A constitution is the principal law of a state or enterprise: it defines the foundational precepts according to which an institution is governed, and limits and guides the definition of its refinements (the lesser laws). The deck offers this as perhaps a more appropriate analogy than “architecture” — because a constitution is judged by what it permits and forbids downstream, not by how it looks.

Architecture and evolution. An architecture has a fundamental impact on a system's ability to support evolution. New requirements do not arrive into an empty space; they arrive into a space that the existing architecture has already pruned. Whether the next requirement is cheap or catastrophic is decided by choices made before that requirement existed.

Views and Viewpoints

If architecture is a set of decisions, a view is how one audience sees them. IEEE 1471 gives the two words precise — and different — meanings.

Viewpoint (IEEE 1471)

Identifies the set of concerns and the representation techniques used to describe the architecture to address those concerns. A viewpoint is a convention: a stakeholder's question plus the notation that answers it.

View (IEEE 1471)

A representation of a whole system from the perspective of a related set of concerns. It is the result of applying a viewpoint to a particular system. A view is what you see through a viewpoint.

One system, two viewpoints, two views. Neither view is complete on its own, and neither is wrong.

Design time versus run time

The most important split inside a viewpoint catalogue. A design-time view is the static organisation of the system specification in a design repository — classes, associations, multiplicities, «import» dependencies between packages. A run-time view is the dynamic organisation of instances created by an executing computer program (itself a specification) — a:A, b1:B, b2:B, c1:C. The two are formally coupled but quite distinct.

Question you are askedAnswer the unit expects
Is there one agreed set of viewpoints?No. There is yet no consensus on what constitutes a suitable set of viewpoints for different categories of systems, so you choose your own or adopt an existing, well-documented proposal.
Which proposals are well known?Philippe Kruchten's “4+1” model, and the Siemens model (Hofmeister, Nord and Soni).
What do the proposals agree on?Separation into views; a distinction between run-time and development (design-time) views; and treating deployment as a separate concern so that it can support reuse.
Why one diagram is never enough: the deck's agentic healthcare example asks you to describe a patient consultation handled by a Triage Agent plus Health Advice, Appointment and Emergency agents. A simple diagram showing all agents is not enough — because it answers the logical question while silently pretending to answer the process, development and deployment ones.

Key Analysis Issues and Architecture Analysis

Architecture work is usually described as four core activities. Analysis comes first, and it decides what the architecture will later be judged against.

Key analysis issueThe question it makes you answer
Requirements identification and specificationWhat needs to be built, and why?
Stakeholder viewpoint identificationWhose concerns are these, and how are those concerns conceptualised?
Domain identification and partitioningHow is the problem decomposed into areas of conceptual and technological homogeneity?

Analysis produces the criteria the architecture must satisfy. The deck expresses them as quality attributes and turns each one into an architecture question — a form that is directly usable in a design review.

How to use the table in an answer. Name the attribute, ask the architecture question, then point at the artefact that answers it — a component, a view, a control policy. “Privacy: only the triage and records agents hold a token that resolves a patient identity, and the deployment view keeps identifiable data inside the private environment” is an architectural answer. “We will encrypt everything” is not.
During analysis a team writes: “decompose the consultation domain into scheduling, clinical knowledge and notification, because each has different data, latency and change rates”. Which analysis issue is this?
Requirements identification and specification

Worked example — an architecture analysis of the agentic healthcare consultation. The scenario: a patient reports a headache and fever; a Triage Agent assesses risk, delegates to Health Advice, Appointment or Emergency agents, and a clinician reviews and records the outcome.

1

Name the domain partition first. Triage, clinical knowledge retrieval, scheduling and notification are separated because they differ in data sensitivity, latency budget and rate of change. That is a partition into areas of conceptual and technological homogeneity, and it is what makes the later component definitions defensible.

2

Turn requirements into quality attributes. Pick the ones the system would actually fail on: safety (unsafe advice), privacy (record exposure), auditability (why did the agent act?), availability (emergency path must survive an agent failure), explainability (a decision must be traceable).

3

Answer each one architecturally, not aspirationally. Safety: high-impact actions require approval plus policy. Privacy: identifiable data stays in the private environment and only de-identified context reaches an external LLM. Auditability: every tool invocation is logged with the inputs that produced it. Availability: the emergency path is a deterministic workflow, not an agent.

4

Map each answer to a view. Agents and services are logical; the guardrail and policy files are development; concurrent execution and the coordination problem are process; private versus cloud model placement is physical. A quality attribute that maps to no view has not been designed, only wished for.

5

State the trade-off. Keeping all identifiable data in the private environment protects privacy and costs capability, because the general-purpose cloud model sees less. Naming that trade-off is the difference between an analysis and an advertisement.

Models of Computation

Before a system can be decomposed, someone has to say how the parts make the whole behave. Choosing the dominant model of computation is a primary architectural decision.

Model of computation (MoC)

Explains how the behaviour of the whole system is the result of the behaviour of each of its components. A model of computation defines the rules governing how computational components execute, communicate, synchronise and exchange data. In software design it is the abstract framework that defines how components compute and communicate.

Two families dominate. Structure-dominant MoCs start from interconnected objects that hold state; behaviour-dominant MoCs start from what runs, and split again into procedural (control-flow based) and data flow. The deck's Triage Agent shows why the choice matters: the same four activities — retrieve EHR context, retrieve medical knowledge, check available appointments, decide — become a different system depending on whether everything executes sequentially, some activities execute concurrently, or components respond to events on an event bus.

MoCComponents are…Triggered by…System behaviour is…Where it breaks
Structure-dominant Interconnected objects that act as containers of information (state) and behaviour, reacting in response to stimuli; the response may be a function of state A stimulus — components as actors send and receive messages asynchronously The composite of individual component responses Reasoning about state and message ordering; individual responses still have to be specified, usually with a behaviour-dominant MoC
Procedural (control flow) Algorithm fragments or functions Completion of predecessor components The composite of (partially) ordered component executions — computation modelled as functions mapping inputs to outputs The pipeline is only as flexible as the call graph that was fixed in advance
Data flow Functional transforms Availability of input data, so the execution order is determined by data availability and fine-grained parallelism is possible The composite of individual component executions No natural home for interactivity or for control that is not data-driven

The deck's data-flow worked example: a house is built by a design house plan transform that produces three parallel downstream transforms — design plumbing, design electricals, design doors and windows — which together feed construct house. Nothing sequences the three design steps; the data does, and they can run in any order or at the same time.

MoC choice for agentic AI. Agents perceive, reason and plan, act, and coordinate, so the MoC must support asynchronous interaction, concurrency and modularity. Structure-dominant suits autonomy, concurrency and multi-agent interaction — the AI agent is the component or actor. Data flow suits chaining reasoning, perception and action functions. One warning from the deck: do not confuse an agent's own reasoning loop with the model of computation of the whole software architecture. They are at different levels.
A pipeline of perception, then reasoning, then action is redesigned so that each stage fires as soon as its input becomes available, allowing several stages to run at once. Which change has been made?
The system moved to a procedural (control-flow) model of computation
The system moved to a data-flow model of computation
The decomposition changed from three components to four

Control in Architecture

Capabilities tell you what a system can do. Control tells you how it reaches, and stays in, a state where it can do it — and Week 4 treats that as a first-class architectural concern.

Control in architecture

The set of (additional) mechanisms and actions required to bring a system into the desired operational state and to maintain it in that state in the face of various planned and unplanned disruptions. For software systems this includes:

  • system and component start-up and shut-down;
  • failure detection, reporting and recovery;
  • system administration, maintenance and provisioning;
  • (on-line) software upgrade.
The number worth remembering: in highly-dependable systems, as much as 80% of the system code is dedicated to control behaviour — yet control is often treated in an ad hoc manner, because it is not part of the primary system functionality, and is typically retrofitted into a framework optimised for the functional behaviour.

Control predicates function

Before a system can perform its primary function, it first has to reach its operational state. Control is not an add-on that runs after the functionality; it is the precondition for it.

Control is independent of function

The process by which a system reaches its operational state is often the same regardless of the specific functionality of the component. That independence is what makes control reusable — and what makes it disappear from functional test suites.

Principle 1 — separate control from function

  • Separate control components from functional components.
  • Separate control from functional interfaces.

The test is mechanical: if a change to a control policy forces an edit to a functional component's public interface, the two were not separated.

Principle 2 — centralise control (decision making)

  • If possible, focus control in one component.
  • Place control policies in the control components and control mechanisms inside the controlled components.

Policies decide; mechanisms act. Splitting them the other way round produces components that cannot be reused because they carry someone else's rules.

The deck's agentic reading. Control design covers execution control (when to act, what to prioritise, how long to pursue a task), self-monitoring with error detection and recovery, and modes of operation — reactive, proactive, deliberative. Function design covers input processing, planning, inference and decision-making, executing changes in the environment, and storing and recalling past interactions, context and learned knowledge.

A team puts the retry-and-restart rules for five services inside each service, in each service's public API. Which design principle has been violated, and what did it cost?
Centralising control, because the rules should have been split across the services
Separating control from function: policies were placed in functional components and exposed through functional interfaces
The model of computation, because retries imply asynchronous execution

The Five Classic Architectural Patterns

A pattern is the codified answer to a problem that keeps coming back. Week 4 names five, and expects you to know which context each one is valid in.

Architectural design pattern

Defines types of elements and relationships that work together in order to solve a particular problem from some perspective (viewpoint). Every pattern has three parts:

  • a recurring common situation — the context;
  • the problem that arises in the given context;
  • a reused solution to that recurring design problem.

Its importance is that it delivers abstraction, requires only minimal changes to a design, and supplies a general structure that both designers and reviewers already understand.

PatternContext & problemSolution shapeAdvantagesDisadvantages
Data-flow (pipe-and-filter) Transform streams of discrete data items; divide the work into reusable, loosely coupled components with simple interaction. Successive transformation of streams of data: data enters the system and flows through the components one at a time until it reaches its final destination. Easy to unplug and replace filters. Interactivity is limited.
Shared-data Various components need to share and manipulate data; several independent components must access the same data. Communication between data accessors through a shared data store. Three important elements: communication, data definition and a data manipulation protocol. Ensures data integrity and reliability. Scalability.
Layering Develop and evolve portions of the system independently; modular segmentation to allow independent development and evolution. A set of layers with well-defined interfaces between them, grouping modules that offer a cohesive set of services. Reduces complexity; improves modularity, reusability and maintainability. How to define the layers?
Service-oriented (SOA) A number of service providers exist and the service customer only needs to understand the use of the services; supporting interoperability of distributed components. Application functionality provided as a set of services: a collection of distributed components and service providers. Abstraction, reusability, loose coupling. Managing service metadata.
Model-View-Controller The user interface is typically the most frequently modified part of an interactive application; retrieve data from a data store and display it for the user while keeping interface modification separate from the rest of the system. Separates the modelling of the domain, the presentation and the actions based on user input into three classes. Interface changes stay local; the domain model is reusable across presentations. More indirection, and the controller can accumulate logic that belongs in the model.

MVC: the controller interprets input and informs the model and/or the view; the model holds the domain behaviour and data and answers requests about its state; the view manages the display. The deck's agentic reading of MVC is a web application framework — Spring MVC, Apache Struts — whose job is to alleviate the overhead of the activities every web application repeats.

Patterns are chosen, not collected. Each of the five optimises a different thing — substitutability of steps, data integrity, independent evolution, interoperability, or presentation churn. Name the property you need first, then the pattern; a pattern named without its context is worth no marks.

Worked example — choosing a pattern for a clinical triage intake. The requirement: patient messages arrive continuously, must be normalised, de-identified and enriched with retrieved knowledge, and the intake UI changes every sprint while the clinical rules change quarterly.

1

Write the forces before the pattern. (a) Messages are discrete items and each step is replaceable. (b) The knowledge base is shared reference data that several steps read. (c) The UI churns far faster than the domain. (d) Retrieval and inference may be remote services.

2

Match each force to a context. (a) is the data-flow context: transform streams of discrete data items, divide into reusable loosely coupled components with simple interaction. (b) is the shared-data context: several independent components need to share and manipulate data. (c) is the MVC context. (d) is the service-oriented context.

3

Choose the one that governs. The intake pipeline governs: pipe-and-filter, with normalise → de-identify → enrich → classify as filters. Its known price is stated up front — interactivity is limited, so anything needing a mid-stream clarification must leave the pipeline rather than fight it.

4

Compose, and say where the seams are. The knowledge base is a shared-data store read by the enrichment filter; retrieval is a service accessed through a published interface; the clinician's review screen is MVC over the classification result. Three patterns, three different concerns — and no pattern is claimed to solve a force it does not address.

5

State what would change the decision. If the process needed a human decision inside the stream, data-flow becomes the wrong governing pattern and the process view — not the pattern catalogue — is where that is discovered.

A team adopts layering for a new system and immediately hits the question “which layer does the audit trail belong to?”. Which statement is correct?
Layering is the wrong pattern, because every pattern must answer every design question it raises
The question is the known disadvantage of layering; the pattern still delivers reduced complexity, modularity, reusability and maintainability
The audit trail should be a layer, because cross-cutting concerns always become layers

Validation and Verification

Architecture is validated against the problem and verified against the specification. The distinction is one sentence long; the engineering is not.

Verification

“Are we building the product right?”

The software should conform to its specification. Verification is a closed-world claim: it compares the artefact against the description you wrote, and it can be automated.

Validation

“Are we building the right product?”

The software should do what the user really wants — meet the customer's expectations and requirements. Validation is an open-world claim: the specification itself is on trial.

Method or toolWhat it establishesWhere it is weak
TestingThat specific observed behaviours conform to the specification for the cases selectedOnly covers the cases chosen; says nothing about the cases not chosen
InspectionThat the artefact matches the intended design, by structured human review against the architecture and its principlesDoes not scale linearly, and reviewers see what they expect to see
SimulationBehaviour under scenarios that are expensive or unsafe to produce in the real systemThe simulation's fidelity is an assumption, not a result
Formal verification
(model checking, theorem proving, quality analysis)		Properties for all executions within the model — the strongest form of “building it right”Proves things about the model; the model may not be the system
The three key V&V issues
  • Selecting suitable methods and tools — testing, inspection, simulation or formal verification, and the tooling for each.
  • Selecting and defining test cases — given limited resources and time, what should be tested?
  • Executing test cases — how to optimise the running of tests: when, and how?
The deck's joke, which is also a question. “Theory is when one knows everything but nothing works. Practice is when everything works but nobody knows why. In our lab, theory and practice go hand in hand: nothing works and nobody knows why.” — Validation or verification? The answer: neither. It is a failure to establish either the specification or the correspondence to it.

Worked example — a V&V plan for an architecture, not for code. Target: the agentic healthcare architecture with a private model for sensitive tasks and a cloud model for general tasks.

1

Say what is being verified. Not “the system works” but “the architecture conforms to its specification”: the logical view contains no component that reads identifiable data and calls an external model; the process view shows one coordinator owning patient-facing messaging.

2

Choose the method per claim. Boundary claims — is identifiable data ever serialised into an external call? — are inspection plus static quality analysis, not testing, because the failure is an absence. Concurrency and coordination claims are simulation or model checking on the process view, because the interesting interleavings are rare. Behavioural claims about triage thresholds are testing.

3

Handle “what should be tested?” explicitly. With limited time, rank by (probability of failure) × (cost of the failure). Unsafe advice and any leak of identifiable data rank first; a slow non-urgent appointment lookup ranks last.

4

Handle “when and how?”. Run architecture-conformance checks in continuous integration so a boundary violation fails the build the day it is introduced, and run the expensive interleaving exploration nightly. The point of the timing decision is to catch drift while the design intent is still recoverable — which is exactly the defence against architectural decay.

5

Validate as well as verify. Verification can be perfect while the system is useless: ask the clinicians who review the triage output whether the escalation policy matches how they actually work. That question cannot be answered by a test suite, and it is the one that decides whether the architecture was worth building.

The architecture description specifies that no component outside the records service may hold a patient identifier. The team builds a linter that fails the build when one does. What is the linter doing?
Verifying the implementation against the specification, and enforcing an architectural decision
Validating the requirement, because it checks what the users really want
Performing formal verification by model checking

Architectural Patterns for Agentic AI

Part 2 of the deck re-runs the week's questions — computation model, control, structure — on systems with an LLM inside the components.

BDI — Belief, Desire, Intention

A widely used theoretical framework for designing intelligent agents in an agentic AI architecture. It separates three attitudes:

  • Beliefs (B) — the agent's dynamic mental model. Its knowledge about the environment, about itself and about other agents. May include facts, perceptions or inferred information, and can be incomplete or uncertain.
  • Desires (D) — the goals that drive action. The goals or states of the world the agent would like to achieve. They are motivational attitudes, and not all of them can be achieved at the same time.
  • Intentions (I) — committed plans of action. The subset of desires the agent commits to achieving, given its beliefs and constraints. Intentions guide concrete plans and actions.

Agent-oriented structural patterns

Pattern choice follows uncertainty and coordination

CoordinationLow task uncertaintyHigh task uncertainty
HighHierarchy; supervisor–workerBlackboard; peer-to-peer
LowWorkflow; single-agent tool useReAct; planner–executor

Two decisions that are not about capability

Not every service should become an agent

Ask first: does the task require contextual judgment? No → use a deterministic service or workflow. Yes → use a bounded agent with explicit tools. If the action is high-impact, add approval plus policy on top.

Agent candidates: triage, clarification, planning. Service candidates: authentication, notification.

Capabilities do not determine control

Two systems with identical functional features — patient interaction, clinical retrieval, record access, risk assessment, scheduling — can have completely different control architectures. Control is a separate design decision from capability.

Control architectureStructureConsequence
Workflow-controlledCollect → Assess → Book, with the order fixed by the workflowPredictable execution, easy to audit and to reason about; less able to adapt when the case does not fit the flow
Supervisor-controlledA supervisor coordinates triage, records and booking agentsDynamic order and an explicit coordinator; more adaptive, but the supervisor becomes the critical component and the thing to be tested
Where this leaves Week 4. The agent patterns are the classic architectural ideas in new clothing: pipe-and-filter becomes planner–executor, layering becomes cognitive layering, shared-data becomes the blackboard, and centralised control becomes the supervisor. The exam wants the mapping, not the vocabulary alone.
A team must route expense claims: fixed steps, fixed order, one approval, no judgment required. Which architecture is defensible?
A deterministic workflow or service, because the task does not require contextual judgment
A ReAct agent, because it can react to exceptions in the claim data
A supervisor–worker architecture, because approval requires coordination

Week 4 Recap

Architecture is a work product with a definition

Fundamental concepts or properties of a system in its environment, embodied in its elements, relationships and the principles of its design and evolution. Its purpose is to make the architecture and its design intent recognisable — the defence against decay.

42010

Views answer audiences, viewpoints define them

A viewpoint is a set of concerns plus representation techniques; a view is the result of applying one to a system. Kruchten's 4+1 gives logical, development, process and physical views, tied together by scenarios.

Documentation

Choose a model of computation, then design control

Structure-dominant, procedural or data flow — the choice decides how the whole behaves as a composite of the parts. Control brings the system into its operational state and keeps it there; separate it from function and centralise it.

Design decisions

Patterns are context-bound answers

Data-flow, shared-data, layering, service-oriented and MVC each solve a different recurring problem, each with a named disadvantage. The agent patterns of Part 2 are the same ideas applied to LLM-based systems.
