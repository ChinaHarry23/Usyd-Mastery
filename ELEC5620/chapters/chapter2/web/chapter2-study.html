The Project Theme: A One Person AI Company

Week 2 has two halves. The first names the system you will build all semester; the second explains why a system like that is modelled rather than merely prompted.

The project theme, in the deck's words

A one-person AI company is owned and directed by one founder. AI agents, software and specialist contractors perform repeatable work under the founder's control. It can research markets, create content, sell, support customers, analyse data and run back-office workflows, while the founder owns judgment, relationships and accountability.

Agentic AI, as the deck defines it

Highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work. They possess human-like cognitive abilities, as opposed to the specialised tasks of narrow AI. Generative AI is the layer underneath: LLMs that generate content such as text and images.

Why the course turns to modelling here

The deck's argument runs from object-oriented to agent-oriented programming, and then makes the move the unit is named after: an agent is an abstraction, and an abstraction has to be modelled. Useful interface, strong capability and a useful architecture are all design claims about that abstraction.

Ethics, safety and security belong in the model. The deck lists responsible use to prevent misinformation, handling sensitive data and personal information (preventing accidental leaks in generated text, masking sensitive information) and keeping LLM applications fair and unbiased. These are requirements and policies — which means they are model elements with traceability, not an afterthought.

The Problem: Complexity

The MBSE story in the deck has three parts — the problem, the premise, the results. This is the problem.

The enemy is complexity. Modern software systems are reaching levels of complexity comparable to those of biological systems — systems of systems. It causes problems and errors in system planning, architectural design and development: projects fail, miss their deadlines or exceed their budgets. Building a complex system is always connected to a risk of mistakes and missed requirements, because humans are intrinsically prone to errors when handling too many details.

Fred Brooks: two kinds of complexity

Essential complexity

  • Inherent to the problem
  • Cannot be eliminated by technology or technique
  • Simple solutions would not adequately solve the problem

Accidental complexity

  • Due to the implementation technology or the methods used to solve the problem
  • Ineffective planning or project management
  • A mismatching or immature implementation technology

Source given in the deck: F. Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month, Addison Wesley, 1995.

Accidental complexity can be avoidable. One of the main tasks in software engineering is to discover means to minimise accidental complexity, and raising the level of abstraction of the technology used for software development is one of the basic means for guarding against it. That sentence is the entire justification for this unit: a model is a raised level of abstraction.
Symptom in a projectWhich complexityWhat modelling changes about it
The problem is genuinely hard: many interacting concerns, no simple solutionEssentialNothing removes it. A model lets you see the essential structure instead of holding every detail in your head.
Three weeks lost to build scripts, framework versions and generated glueAccidentalTool-supported outputs remove hand-written glue; the effort moves into the model once.
Two teams disagree about an interface nobody wrote downAccidentalThe interface is an explicit model element that both teams can read and check.
A requirement is missed because it was only in a conversationAccidentalRequirements traceability links every model element back to the requirement it serves.
Nobody can say what the system actually does at run timeBothRun-time views make states and behaviour explicit, and can be executed and observed.
Exam framing: never write “modelling removes complexity”. The defensible claim is narrower and stronger: modelling attacks accidental complexity by raising the level of abstraction, and makes essential complexity legible by representing the parts that matter for a set of concerns.
A team loses a fortnight to a mismatched toolchain version and a build script nobody understands. How does the deck classify that, and what follows?
Essential complexity — the problem is simply hard

Neither — project overhead is unavoidable and outside the scope of modelling

What a Model Is

The deck answers “what is a model?” in four moves, then tightens it into the definition of an engineering model.

A model…

Is a representation of a part of the function, structure or behaviour of a system. Is a simplification of a system — information, abstraction, specification. Can answer questions in place of the actual system — inference, prediction. Is used for a purpose — planning, design, risk analysis, testing.

Abstraction is forgetting (some) details. The deck's one-line definition: a model of a thing is the thing seen through an abstraction. What makes a model good is not how much it contains but whether what it kept is the part that matters.
Why engineers build modelsQuestion the model answersWhat it must therefore contain
To understandWhat are the interesting characteristics of this existing or desired system and its environment?The structure and the relationships, at a size a person can hold in mind
To predictWhat will the interesting characteristics be, if we analyse the model?Behaviour that can be executed, simulated or checked
To communicateCan others — and can I — see the same design intent?Shared notation with agreed meaning: a language, not a sketch
To specifyWhat exactly is to be implemented?A precise enough description to serve as the blueprint
Engineering model

A selective representation of some system that captures accurately and concisely all of its essential properties of interest for a given set of concerns. Every clause is doing work: selective (it omits), accurate and concise (it is not a dump), essential (not incidental), for a set of concerns (there is no context-free model).

Software model

An engineering model — specified using a modelling language — of some software that represents: (1) design-time views: the structure and content of the software specification; and (2) run-time views: the structure and behaviour of the software in execution. The deck adds that one of the primary motives for many modelling languages is the need to represent software in execution more clearly.

Tutorial 2's summary of the same idea: abstraction helps people understand; automation helps tools act. The tutorial is explicit that a use case diagram mainly supports abstraction and communication — not direct code generation. Being honest about which role a given model plays is part of the mark.

The model can become the system

Modern MBSE development style: models can be refined continuously until the application is fully specified, so that the model becomes the system that it was modelling. Software is in a unique position to do this, because a software model and the software being modelled share the same medium — the computer, which also happens to be our most advanced and most versatile automation technology. Software therefore allows models to be evolved into implementations without fundamental discontinuities in the expertise, tools, or methods.

Viewpoints and Levels of Abstraction

The deck's building slide carries the whole idea: we do not see everything at once, and what we do see is adjusted to human understanding.

Viewpoint

A viewpoint is a perspective on the system together with the concerns it addresses and the language used to express them: the architect's view, the builder's view, the electrician's view. In modelling terms it is a convention for what a particular audience is allowed to see.

View

A view is the model of the system produced from one viewpoint. One system, many views: a class diagram, a state machine and a deployment diagram can all be faithful and still disagree in appearance, because each answers a different question.

Levels of abstraction

LevelWhat it describesWho reads itDeck note
Problem domainThe situation as it is: users, goals, rules, constraintsStakeholders and analystsThe starting point of the software engineering loop
ModelThe chosen aspects of structure, function and behaviourEveryone, each from a viewpointAbstraction using a model
High-level languageAn implementation of the model in a programming languageDevelopersStill above the machine, still full of detail
MachineWhat actually executesHardware, runtimes, toolsThe level the model is eventually deployed to
Alan J. Perlis, quoted in the deck: “A programming language is low level when its programs require attention to the irrelevant.” Notice that this is a statement about attention, not about syntax or byte code: a level is low exactly when it makes you think about things that do not matter to the problem.
Two loops, not one. Software engineering as the deck draws it runs problem domain → implement → solution domain, and then solution domain → validate → problem domain. Viewpoints sit on both edges: the problem-domain views say what is wanted, the solution-domain views say what was built, and validation is the check that they still describe the same system.
A new engineer asks for “the model” and is handed a class diagram, a state machine and a deployment diagram. Why is that not a mistake?
They are three drafts of the same picture and one of them must be authoritative
Each diagram is a view for a different set of concerns, and each is selective by design
A system may only have one model, so two of the three are redundant

Modelling Languages and UML Notation

A model you cannot check is a picture. Notation is what turns “the parts that matter” into something a tool — and an examiner — can verify.

Natural language (vibe coding)

  • Intent lives in conversational prompts
  • The agent produces code, with less control
  • Best fit: small scope, rapid learning, low consequence

Model-based software engineering

  • Intent lives in structured and versioned models
  • The agent produces code under explicit structure and behaviour constraints
  • Best fit: large systems that evolve, integrate, or carry risk

The deck's punchline about the two: both approaches use the same agents. The difference is whether the agent receives transient conversation or durable engineering intent.

Three words that carry the section

A modelling language is a language with defined syntax and semantics for expressing models — UML is the one this unit uses. A metamodel defines the language itself: which constructs exist (Class, Association, State, Transition), what properties they have and how they may be legally combined. A model is an instance of that metamodel. A stereotype is a controlled extension of the metamodel, written «like this», that brands an existing element with domain meaning.

Why the notation matters for the project. Tutorial 2 spends its modelling time on use cases, and the discipline it enforces is notation-level: an actor is a role played in relation to the system, never a person's name; the system boundary defines responsibility; and each line in the use case diagram means one actor participating in one system goal. Get the notation sloppy and the boundary becomes arguable.

Worked example — read the producer/consumer model back into prose. The C++ listing in the deck is followed by a two-box UML model. Practise turning one into the other, in both directions.

1

Start with the classes. There are two: a1 : Producer and b1 : Consumer. Written as instance : class, they say that this model is about two concrete objects, one of each type.

2

Read the attributes as the interface. Producer exposes start : sc_in<bool> and out1 : sc_outmaster<int>; Consumer exposes in1 : sc_inslave<int> and keeps sum : int. The ports state what can cross the boundary of each object, and the type parameters state what kind of value crosses.

3

Read the association as the wiring. One link joins the two objects, realised through the ends out1 and in1, with multiplicity 1 at each end. This is the claim that is hardest to see in the source code: which object is connected to which, and through what.

4

Read the stereotypes as the domain vocabulary. «sc_method», «sc_slave» and «sc_link_mp» say that these elements come from the SystemC method/slave/link idioms. A reader who knows the profile gets the mechanism; a reader who does not still gets the structure.

5

Say what the model deliberately does not say. It does not say when generate_data() runs, how the scheduler orders the two processes, or what the tenth iteration does. Those belong to a behaviour view — a state machine or a sequence diagram — not to this one. Naming the omissions is how you show the abstraction was a decision rather than an accident.

What does applying the stereotype «sc_method» to a class actually do?
It adds a new attribute to the class
It brands an existing model element with domain meaning drawn from a profile
It replaces the class name with the tool's internal name

The MBE Approach: Abstraction and Automation

Model-based engineering is an approach to system and software development in which software models play an indispensable role. The deck grounds it in two time-proven ideas.

(1) Abstraction — the realm of modelling languages

The behaviour is expressed as states and transitions, with events and actions: S1 → S3 on e3/action3. This is a claim about what the system does, in a language whose constructs were designed for that claim.

(2) Automation — the realm of tools

The same behaviour, acted on by tools: checked, transformed, generated into a switch statement, executed and observed. The model is not a document; it is an input to machinery.

MBE opportunityWhat it isWhat it still requires of you
Code generation The accidental complexity of current programming languages can be greatly reduced by the appropriate use of computer-based automation; the deck notes that advanced AI models are used for this. A model precise enough to generate from — and review. The deck's own slide shows a BUG in the generated switch statement: generation does not remove the need to check.
Exploiting formal methods Modelling language constructs can be made better behaved than programming language constructs, so formal methods become usable: state machines, Petri nets, model checking, theorem proving. Formal semantics for the modelling language. The deck is explicit that programming languages could not handle this semantic complexity.
Model execution Execute a model on a computer and observe its behaviour. Harel: “Models that are not executable are like cars without engines.” Controllability (start, stop, slow down, speed up, drive execution), observability (view execution and state in model form) and partial model execution (abstract and incomplete models still run).
Model transformations Model viewing (abstraction and refinement), domain-to-domain transformations (for example UML to a queuing network model) and model-to-code transformations. A comprehensive theory of model transformations comparable to compiler theory. The standard named in the deck is OMG's Queries, Views, and Transformations (QVT).

Platform independence — and its limit

Separation of concerns lets a model focus on the functionality and behaviour of a system, and that is what enables portability. The deck draws one application with «deploy» dependencies onto computing platforms 1 to N, and then asks the question worth remembering: does “platform independence” mean that we can ignore platform concerns when designing our application? It does not. The platform did not disappear; it moved from the code into the model, where the deployment relationship, the runtime assumptions and the resource limits are recorded explicitly instead of being implicit in a build script.

The «deploy» dependency is notation for a claim: this artefact runs on that platform. Drawing it is what makes platform assumptions reviewable.

Worked example — choosing the automation that pays. Scenario: the healthcare service app from Tutorial 2 has a state machine for “medical appointment”. The team wants to know which of the four MBE opportunities to invest in first.

1

Ask whether the model is precise enough to be acted on. A state machine with named events, guards and actions is. A whiteboard sketch of the appointment journey is not. Everything downstream depends on this answer.

2

Model execution first. Execute the appointment state machine and watch it: can a patient be booked into a slot that is already cancelled? Controllability and observability turn that question into an experiment, which is cheaper than an argument.

3

Formal methods where the cost of being wrong is high. Double-booking and permission rules are safety properties: model checking or theorem proving is justified where the policy must hold for every reachable state, not just for the states somebody thought to test.

4

Transformations for leverage, not for novelty. A UML-to-queuing-network transformation answers a capacity question the state machine cannot; model-to-code generation removes the glue. Both require the model to be the authoritative artefact — if it is not, the transformation is just a one-off export.

5

State what generation does not give you. The deck shows a bug surviving into generated code. Generation is only as correct as the model and the transformation, so review the model against the requirements — which is exactly what traceability is for.

Which MBE opportunity is described by “the ability to execute a model on a computer and observe its behaviour”, with controllability, observability and partial execution as its key capabilities?
Code generation
Model execution
Model transformations

Styles of MBSE, Traceability and Results

The deck closes the premise with a spectrum, not a verdict: how much of the system lives in the model is a design decision, and each position buys something different.

Levels of abstraction and automation rise left to right. The five positions are the deck's own labels; the quotes attached to them are its one-line summaries of each stance.

The Results: a major telecom equipment manufacturer

The deck's evidence that the spectrum is not theoretical. MBE technologies used: UML, Rational Technical Developer and the Rational Unified Process.

Code onlyNothing: understanding is reconstructed by reading codeEvery design decision is implicit; nothing is checkable before build time

Traceability: the property that makes a model reviewable

Traceability

The recorded links between model elements and the things they exist to serve — requirements, decisions, tests, code. Traceability is what lets you answer two questions mechanically: why does this element exist? and if this requirement changes, what must be revisited? The tutorial lists requirements traceability among the established capabilities of MBE today, alongside consistency checking and verification.

Established capabilities (Tutorial 2)Emerging opportunities (Tutorial 2)
Code and test generationAI-assisted modelling
Simulation and executable modelsGenerative architecture exploration
Model transformationDigital threads and digital twins
Requirements traceabilityCross-tool interoperability
Consistency checking and verificationContinuous model–runtime feedback
The shift, in the tutorial's words: models are becoming connected, adaptive and AI-supported across the full system lifecycle. Established methods remain the foundation; new tools extend how models are created, linked and updated. For an exam answer, keep the two columns apart — claiming traceability is an “emerging” idea is a mark lost.

From Vibe Coding to Engineered Delivery

The deck's closing argument is about what happens to intent over time: vibe coding optimises the next output, while model-based engineering optimises the lifecycle.

Vibe coding: intent drift

  • A rough product idea becomes a prototype or user flow in hours, not weeks
  • Requirements and assumptions disappear between sessions
  • The specification is the conversation, and the conversation is transient

Vibe coding: architecture decay

  • Agents probe an unfamiliar API, framework or integration through rapid experiments
  • Over time they make locally plausible edits
  • Those edits violate global boundaries, interfaces and data rules
Models give coding agents a durable control plane. The model constrains what to build; the agent still decides how to build it. Models capture capabilities, interfaces, data schemas, states, policies, non-functional requirements and acceptance criteria. The model is not a picture for a document archive — it is a control surface the agent can read, transform and validate against. Keep the vibe for discovery. Engineer the system for delivery.
Model repository

The versioned store the whole toolchain reads from and writes to. It is what makes “structured and versioned models” a working description rather than a slogan: because the model lives in a repository, a tool can transform it, a reviewer can diff it, an agent can read it, and a requirement change can be traced through it.

Model quality: the checklist the definition implies

An engineering model captures accurately and concisely all essential properties of interest for a given set of concerns. Read as a checklist: accurate (it does not contradict the system), concise (nothing incidental survives), essential (the omissions are deliberate), concern-scoped (you can name the viewpoint it serves), and fit for use (it supports the purpose — understanding, prediction, communication or specification — that it was built for).

Applying it to the project

Tutorial 2's modelling moveWhat it looks like in the one-person AI company
Primary user groupsThe tutorial's healthcare sample lists patients, doctors and the technical team. For your project, name the roles your agents serve or replace — Lab Note 2 requires at least three distinct AI roles performing primary stakeholder or business functions.
Actors are roles, not namesAn actor is outside the system and interacts with it to achieve a goal. “Customer” is an actor; a person's name is not.
System boundaryInside: what your service does. Outside: the people and services it depends on. Tutorial 2's boundary rule for the shoe store is the model of a good constraint: the AI may recommend and explain; it does not make the final purchase decision.
Key capabilities and core featuresEvery feature is a model element that traces back to an actor's goal. Features that trace to no goal are accidental scope.
Optional featuresKept explicitly out of the MVP. The MVP question is the one to answer out loud: can this help the user make a better decision? Keep only the features needed to test that value.

Tutorial exercise — draw a boundary you can defend. Take Tutorial 2's healthcare service and apply the same moves the tutorial applies to the smart shoe store.

1

Name the actors and their goals. Patient: describe symptoms and receive an initial recommendation. Doctor: review agent-generated reports and consult. Technical team: manage permissions and update the LLM-based agents. Each is an external role with a goal — the definition of an actor.

2

Put the repeatable work inside the boundary. Automatic diagnosis support, health records, appointment scheduling and health consultation are system goals: the agents perform them. That is the “repeatable work under the founder's control” half of the one-person-company definition.

3

Put judgment, relationships and accountability outside it. A diagnosis is confirmed by a doctor; a payment is processed by an external service. The boundary rule to write down explicitly, in the tutorial's style: the AI may recommend, explain and schedule; it does not confirm a diagnosis or move money.

4

Check the notation, not just the prose. Each line in the use case diagram is one actor participating in one system goal. If a line cannot be read that way — because it joins two goals, or names a person, or crosses the boundary — the diagram is wrong even if the sentence beside it sounds right.

5

Trace the requirements you have to satisfy. Lab Note 2 requires at least three distinct AI roles performing primary stakeholder or business functions, and at least two core features plus one optional feature implemented by each group member. Write those as requirements first, then trace use cases and features back to them — that is traceability doing real work rather than being decorative.

6

Fold in the ethics requirements early. Preventing misinformation, masking sensitive information and avoiding biased behaviour are properties of the model — constraints on what an agent may say and which data it may read. Added at the end they are patches; added here they are design.
