DEMO101 — Building a Study Hub

Week 1: Content and Structure

A study hub is a folder of small, predictable files. This chapter introduces the content pack, the five pieces that make up one chapter, and the section rules the page generator reads when it builds your pages.

The content pack

Everything the engine needs sits inside one folder. Copy the folder, rewrite the words, and you have a second course.

A content pack is the unit of authoring. The engine never reads your files from scattered locations: it opens one pack, reads its manifest, and follows the paths that manifest describes. That constraint is what makes a course portable. You can zip the pack, hand it to a classmate, and their build produces the same pages yours did.

The demo course you are reading was written this way. Its pack contains a manifest, three chapter folders, and four data files:

course.json

The manifest. It names the course, sets the storage key, and lists the chapters in order.

chapters/NN/

One folder per chapter holding the study page, the foundations page, the mind map, and an optional script.

data/

Practice material that spans the whole course: quiz entries, flashcards, and two concept graphs.

No build files

Nothing is compiled. The pack is plain text, so a diff shows exactly what changed between drafts.

Definition — content pack

A folder containing course.json, a chapters/ tree, and a data/ folder. Every page in the hub is generated from files inside that folder and nothing else.

Two consequences follow from that definition, and both are easy to feel while authoring. First, a missing file is a visible problem rather than a silent one: the generator either finds chapters/01/study.html or it does not. Second, filenames are part of the interface. Chapter folders are numbered with two digits so they sort correctly as text, and the page names inside them are fixed.

The course manifest

The manifest is the only file that knows about the course as a whole. Get it right and the rest of the pack has somewhere to belong.

The manifest is a single JSON object. Its top-level fields describe the course, and its chapters array describes each chapter in the order you want them displayed. The home card, the chapter numbering, and the progress keys all derive from this one object, so it is worth reading slowly the first time you edit it.

Keep the counts honest

The demo manifest advertises three chapters and twelve widgets. Those numbers are only useful because they match the pack: twelve widget instances are mounted across the six content pages, and the manifest says so. A metric that drifts away from the material teaches readers to distrust every other number on the card.

Writing a section

A study page is a fragment: an ordered list of sections with no page furniture around them.

You do not write <html>, <head>, <body>, a sidebar, or a script tag in a chapter page. The generator supplies all of that. Your file begins with your first section and ends with your last, which keeps the interesting part of the document — the teaching — free of boilerplate that every chapter repeats.

Four rules do the heavy lifting:

  1. The first section is the hero. It carries id="hero" and data-track="hero".
  2. Every other section carries a unique id of the form sec-something and a matching data-track value in lower case with no spaces.
  3. Every section except the hero opens with an <h2 class="section-title">. That heading text becomes the sidebar label, so keep it to two to four words.
  4. A study page holds six to nine sections. The hero counts as one.

The data-track attribute is what turns a section into a progress unit. As a reader scrolls, the engine records each track it has seen and fills the progress bar from the ratio of seen tracks to total tracks. A section without a track is still rendered, but it is invisible to progress: the reader can finish it and the counter will not move.

Naming pays compound interest. Anchors such as #sec-manifest are quoted by mind maps, concept graphs, and cross-links in other chapters. A vague anchor works today and becomes a guessing game the moment somebody else needs to link to you.

Fragment

A partial document that the generator wraps in a full page. Chapter pages are fragments; the layout, navigation, and scripts are added at build time.

Section contract

The four rules above, taken together: hero first, unique anchors, a short labelled heading, and a section count inside the allowed range.

Content classes

You do not invent layout. You pick from a small vocabulary of blocks, and the stylesheet keeps them consistent across chapters.

The engine ships a set of content classes that are already styled, already responsive, and already tested against the dark theme. Using them is faster than writing markup from scratch, and it means a reader who learns one chapter can navigate every other chapter without re-learning the visual grammar.

ClassUse it forNotes
section-titleThe heading the sidebar reads.Required outside the hero; keep it short.
section-leadOne sentence that frames the section.Place it directly after the heading.
def-boxA term and its definition.One idea per box.
theorem-boxA result stated once, cleanly.Reserve it for things worth quoting.
insight-boxA connection worth noticing.Best when it changes how a reader reads the next paragraph.

glance-grid + glance-cardA small set of parallel items.Cards hold a short heading and a sentence or two.

why-cardArguments for and against.Add pro or con for the green and red treatments.

badgeA short status or category tag.Two or three words, no punctuation.
quick-checkA single self-test question.Exactly one option is marked correct.
solution-toggle + solution-bodyA worked answer behind a button.The button names the target by id.

Why a fixed vocabulary helps

  • Readers recognise the same block types in every chapter.
  • Responsive behaviour is solved once, not per page.
  • Reviews focus on the explanation rather than the markup.

Where it costs you

  • A genuinely new layout needs a new class in the stylesheet.
  • Two blocks that look alike may need different classes.
  • Overuse flattens emphasis: if everything is a callout, nothing is.

Prose first, data second

It is tempting to start with the quiz and the concept graph because they are structured and satisfying to fill in. Resist it for one draft. Those files quote your anchors, so writing them against a moving target means rewriting them twice.

Checking your work

Most broken chapters fail in one of four familiar ways. Learn to spot them and a review takes five minutes.

The fastest review is mechanical. Confirm the hero, confirm the anchors, confirm the heading in every section, then confirm the JSON. Only after those four checks does it make sense to read the page as a reader would.

Quick check
A section renders correctly but never appears in the sidebar and never advances the progress bar. What is the most likely cause?
The heading is missing its section-title class
The section is placed after the hero instead of before it
The section is missing a unique data-track value
The chapter has no study.js file

Two of the four failure modes above are structural, and two are typographical. Structure is worth checking first because it is cheap: count the sections, scan the ids, and read the headings out loud. Typographical faults hide in long lists of markup, which is exactly where a script beats an eye.

Worked answer — a five-minute structural review. Run the checks in this order and stop at the first failure; later checks assume earlier ones passed.

  1. Hero. The first section has id="hero" and data-track="hero". If it does not, every relative anchor in the pack is suspect, because the rest of the page was numbered from a bad start.
  2. Count. Six to nine sections on a study page, five to seven on a foundations page. A tenth section usually means two sections were merged badly or one was never needed.
  3. Anchors. Every non-hero section has a unique id beginning with sec- and a track of lower-case words. Read them as a list and check that no two sound alike.
  4. Headings. Every non-hero section opens with an h2 carrying section-title, and the text is two to four words long, because that text is the whole sidebar entry.
  5. Data. Parse every JSON file. A widget block that fails to parse usually fails silently: the placeholder stays an empty box on the page, which reads like a styling bug and wastes an hour.

If all five pass, hand the page to a reader. Structure cannot tell you whether the explanation lands; it can only tell you that nothing is standing in the way of it landing.
