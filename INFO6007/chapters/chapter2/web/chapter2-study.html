The Project Lifecycle: Five Phases

Before comparing methodologies, be precise about what they all have to cover. Every methodology in this unit is a different way of travelling through the same lifecycle.

The hypothesis for the week: a methodology does not replace the lifecycle. It changes how many times you repeat each phase, how big each pass is, and how much of the plan is fixed in advance.

Definition — project lifecycle

The project lifecycle is the structured sequence of phases a project goes through from start to finish. It provides a clear road map for managing work effectively, reducing risks and ensuring success. The five phases are initiation/defining, planning, execution, monitoring & controlling and closure.

Where the knowledge areas sit. Defining sets the project up and gains approvals; planning is the most knowledge-heavy phase, with almost every knowledge area deeply involved; execution uses those plans to manage people, quality, procurement and communication; monitoring & controlling touches nearly every knowledge area because it is where actual versus planned performance is measured; closure confirms formal completion.

What a Methodology Is — and When Waterfall Fits

A methodology is a repeatable answer to “how do we deliver?”. The first answer in the deck is the oldest one: finish each phase before starting the next.

Definition — project management methodology

Project management methodologies are structured approaches or systems of practices used to plan, execute and manage projects efficiently. They provide a repeatable framework that guides teams on how to deliver work successfully from start to finish.

Why a methodology is worth having

  • Consistency and predictability across projects
  • Better team collaboration
  • Risks, time and costs are managed rather than absorbed
  • Work stays aligned with business goals

What a methodology is not

  • It is not a substitute for a sponsor, a scope or a budget
  • It is not a guarantee: the same framework succeeds and fails
  • It is not free: ceremonies, documents and gates all cost effort
Waterfall

The waterfall model is a linear, sequential project management methodology in which each phase must be completed before the next begins. It is one of the earliest and most traditional approaches, often used in engineering, construction and structured IT projects.

Advantages the deck lists

  • Simple to understand and use
  • Clear milestones and documentation
  • Easier to manage when scope is fixed and requirements are stable
  • Ideal for government, construction or compliance-heavy projects

Disadvantages the deck lists

  • Not flexible to change once development begins
  • Difficult to go back to a previous phase
  • Delayed testing uncovers late-stage problems
  • A poor fit for dynamic or evolving requirements
Example 1 Waterfall

A mid-sized manufacturing company with 500 employees replaces its paper-based HR system with a new HRMS covering employee records, payroll, leave management and performance tracking. Senior leadership mandates a strict 8-month timeline; scope and budget are clearly defined up front. The chosen vendor wants a detailed requirement specification before starting any work. The company has no prior agile experience and prefers a structured, linear delivery process.

Why waterfall is chosen here: requirements are well understood and unlikely to change; success depends on complete documentation; stakeholders want formal approvals at each phase; development is outsourced and needs clear handoffs.

Read the reasons, not the industry. “It is an IT project” does not make it agile. The waterfall signals in this example are stable requirements, contractual handoffs, documentation as a deliverable and stage-gate approvals.
A government department must replace a case-management system under a fixed-price contract. The legislation it implements is settled, the vendor needs a signed specification before mobilising, and each stage needs formal acceptance. Which approach do the stated constraints favour?
Waterfall, because the requirements are stable and the contract depends on documented, formally accepted phases
Kanban, because a board would make the queue visible
Scrum, because sprints would surface problems earlier

Agile and Scrum: Roles, Events and Artefacts

Agile is the mindset; Scrum is one framework that implements it. The exam usually asks you to name the parts precisely and then justify the choice.

Agile

Agile is a flexible, iterative approach to project management, used primarily in software development and dynamic project environments. Unlike rigid, linear models such as waterfall, it breaks the project into small, manageable increments called sprints or iterations. The deck is emphatic: it is not a management technique but a mindset.

Scrum

Scrum is an agile framework that delivers work in time-boxed iterations called sprints (typically 1 to 4 weeks), focusing on empirical process control, team collaboration and continuous feedback.

Scrum componentWhat it isWho owns it
SprintA time-boxed development cycle, usually 1–4 weeksThe Scrum team, protected by the Scrum Master
Product BacklogThe list of all desired featuresProduct Owner
Sprint BacklogThe list of tasks to be completed in the current sprintThe Scrum team
IncrementThe potentially shippable product at the end of the sprintThe Scrum team
Example 2 Scrum

A university IT department receives funding to build a mobile app that improves student engagement: event notifications, campus maps, peer messaging and feedback surveys. Users are current students and university staff. Requirements keep evolving because different departments request features. The Product Owner stresses incremental delivery so students can test and give feedback regularly. The app must go live in 4 months, with core features first.

Why Scrum fits: requirements are not fixed up front; prioritisation changes with student feedback; frequent demos and stakeholder input are needed; the team is small and cross-functional; value delivery is emphasised over documentation.

Worked answer — running one fortnight of the student engagement app. The team has chosen two-week sprints and a four-month window, so there are roughly eight sprints. Here is how a single sprint is assembled, using only the parts the lecture defines.

1

Sprint goal. “A student can log in and see their personal timetable.” One outcome, demonstrable in fourteen days — not a list of five half-finished features.

2

Product Owner orders the backlog. The owner ranks the timetable view above peer messaging because the timetable is core to engagement and messaging depends on identity and presence that do not exist yet. Prioritisation is a value decision, not a technical one.

3

Sprint planning produces the sprint backlog. The team pulls the stories it believes it can finish inside the time box: sign-in, timetable API call, timetable screen, empty and error states. Anything that will not fit stays in the product backlog.

4

Daily stand-up keeps the time box honest. Fifteen minutes a day: what moved, what is next, what is blocked. When the timetable API turns out to need an authentication change, that is a blocker for the Scrum Master to clear, not a reason to extend the sprint.

5

The increment must be potentially shippable. Working login plus timetable, tested, is an increment. Five features that are 80% done are not — the sprint either produces something a student could use, or the team reports what it did not finish.

6

Review then retrospective. In the review, students and staff try the increment; their feedback reorders the product backlog — which is how this project handles changing requirements at all. In the retrospective the team changes one thing about how it works, such as automating the test run that caught the authentication regression late.

Kanban: Flow, WIP Limits and Cycle Time

Kanban is the methodology to reach for when work arrives continuously and there is no natural end date. Its instrument is a board; its discipline is a limit.

Definition — Kanban

Kanban is a visual workflow management method that focuses on continuous delivery, limiting work in progress (WIP) and workflow transparency.

ElementWhat it isWhat it changes
Kanban boardA visual board that tracks tasks through stages: To Do → Doing → DoneMakes the queue, the work and the bottleneck visible to everyone
Cards / user storiesIndividual tasks or work itemsOne item of value per card, movable between columns
WIP limitsA restriction on the number of items allowed in a columnPrevents overload; forces the team to finish before starting
Cycle timeThe time taken for a task to go from start to finishTurns flow into a number the team can improve
Example 3 Kanban

A growing software company wants to improve its customer support ticket handling. Tickets pile up in a backlog and are often delayed because the five-person support team struggles to prioritise tasks and balance workload. The team handles bug reports, feature requests and user questions. There is no strict deadline for resolving tickets, but the company wants to reduce response times and increase customer satisfaction.

Why Kanban fits: continuous flow of work with no fixed-length iterations; incoming tickets must be handled at any time; visual management exposes bottlenecks; WIP limits stop agents being overloaded; the emphasis is continuous improvement (Kaizen).

Scrum versus Kanban

FeatureScrumKanban
StructureTime-boxed sprintsContinuous flow
RolesDefined: Product Owner, Scrum Master, teamNo specific roles required
WorkloadPlanned per sprintPulled as capacity allows
Change handlingLimited during a sprintChange is allowed at any time
Visual managementOptional boardMandatory Kanban board
Best forTeams that need structureTeams that need flexibility
Flow has arithmetic. Little's Law relates the three Kanban numbers on this page: WIP = TH × CT (work in progress = throughput × cycle time), so cycle time rises with the amount of work in progress. The Foundations page works the numbers for this support team — a WIP limit is a cycle-time decision, not a housekeeping rule.
A support team is interrupted by new tickets all day, has no release deadline and cannot see where work is piling up. Which choice matches the stated conditions, and why?
Waterfall, because the ticketing process can be planned once and then followed
Kanban, because the work arrives continuously and the problem is flow, not iteration planning
Scrum, because sprints would protect the team from interruptions

DevOps and the *Ops Family

DevOps is what happens to delivery when the development and operations teams stop handing work over a wall. It is a culture question before it is a tooling question.

Definition — DevOps

DevOps is a set of practices that integrates software development and IT operations to shorten the development lifecycle, increase deployment frequency and deliver high-quality software continuously. DevOps = Development + Operations.

The four goals in the deck

  • Faster delivery cycles through CI/CD
  • Improved collaboration between teams
  • Automated testing, deployment and monitoring
  • Infrastructure as Code (IaC) for scalability and reliability

Why it matters to a project manager

  • Batch size falls, so deployment risk per release falls
  • Feedback arrives while there is still time to act on it
  • Environment and pipeline work becomes a planned deliverable
  • Quality control shifts left into the build rather than the end
Delivery modelRelease rhythmBatch sizeFeedback latency
WaterfallDesign → Code → Test → Deploy, onceEverything the project promisedMonths: the first real feedback is the first test cycle
AgileDesign, then Code → Test repeated per increment, then DeployOne increment per iterationOne sprint, if the increment is demonstrated
DevOpsContinuous: every change flows through an automated pipelineThe smallest change that is worth shippingMinutes to hours, from monitoring in production
Example 4 DevOps

A mid-sized e-commerce company struggles with slow software releases and frequent production outages. Development and operations work in silos: developers finish coding and “throw it over the wall” to operations for deployment and maintenance. The company decides to adopt DevOps practices including CI/CD, infrastructure as code, automated testing and monitoring.

Why DevOps is chosen: a need for faster, more reliable software delivery; a desire to break down silos and improve communication; reducing manual errors and deployment risk; improving scalability and infrastructure management.

DevOps in project management. The deck's list of benefits is long — accelerated delivery and innovation, automation and efficiency, cross-functional collaboration, reliability and consistency, better monitoring and quality control, governance, security and compliance, and support for agile and continuous practices. The examinable point is that DevOps changes the plan, not just the toolchain: pipelines, environments and monitoring become deliverables with dates.

Requirement Gathering

Every methodology needs something to deliver. Requirement gathering is where the something is established — and where a project most often goes quietly wrong.

Definition — requirement gathering

Requirement gathering is the process of identifying, collecting and documenting the needs and expectations of stakeholders for a new or updated system or product.

Why it is worth the effort

  • Ensures the final product meets stakeholder needs
  • Reduces scope creep and rework
  • Forms the foundation for design, development and testing
  • Makes planning and estimation possible at all

What weak gathering produces

  • Requirements that only one vocal stakeholder believes in
  • Estimates based on the wrong scope
  • Acceptance arguments at the end, when change is expensive
  • Test cases with nothing authoritative to test against
TechniqueHow it worksBest when
InterviewsOne-to-one with stakeholdersDepth matters, or the topic is sensitive or political
Surveys / questionnairesGather wide input quicklyMany similar respondents, or geography and time are constraints
WorkshopsCollaborative group sessionsConflicting views must be reconciled and decisions taken in the room
ObservationWatch users interact with the current systemPeople describe the official process, not the real one
Document analysisReview existing system and process documentationThere is a baseline to verify, or regulations and forms must be traced
Scenario Requirement gathering

A hospital is replacing its paper-based outpatient appointment system with a digital one. Receptionists manage bookings, nurses record patient arrivals and doctors request follow-up appointments; patients will also be able to view or change appointments online. The project team has three weeks to gather requirements. Staff across the hospital's five clinics follow slightly different processes, the existing procedure manual is outdated, and some staff are too busy to attend long meetings. The system must also meet the needs of elderly patients and patients with limited English.

The question: what is the best approach to gathering the requirements for this project, and why?

Worked answer — the hospital, in three weeks. No single technique survives all five constraints, so the answer is a designed combination, sequenced so that the cheapest-to-fix disagreements surface first.

1

Start with document analysis, but treat it as a hypothesis. The outdated manual still lists every form, field and hand-off, so it gives a fast first draft of the process across all five clinics. It is a starting point to be corrected, never the requirement itself.

2

Observe the real process in each clinic. People describe the official process; observation shows the actual one — the sticky note on the monitor, the phone call that bypasses the system, the double booking at 8am. With five clinics and three weeks, observation is also cheaper than five long meetings.

3

Run one short workshop per role, not per clinic. Busy staff can spare a focused hour more easily than a half-day. The workshop that matters most reconciles where the five clinics differ, because those differences are the integration risk and the scope creep waiting to happen.

4

Reach patients with a survey, made accessible. Patients are numerous, dispersed and not in the building. A short questionnaire reaches them, but elderly patients and patients with limited English will be under-represented unless it is offered in translated and assisted formats and supplemented by a few interviews with, or alongside, carers and interpreters.

5

Validate and record. Play the requirements back to a representative group in a final session, and trace each requirement to its source (observed, stated, or mandated). What cannot be validated in three weeks is written down as an assumption with an owner — which is a risk to manage, not a gap to hide.

For the hospital scenario, which approach best fits the stated constraints?
Analyse the existing manual and write the specification from it
Combine document analysis, observation in each clinic, short role-based workshops and an accessible patient survey
Hold a full-day workshop with staff from all five clinics to agree every requirement

Project Initiation: the Charter and the Stakeholders

The defining stage is where the project is authorised. Two artefacts carry that authority: the charter, and a stakeholder map you actually use.

Purpose of initiation / defining

Clarity on what is being done and why; buy-in from stakeholders and leadership; and a baseline for planning, execution and success measurement.

Identify the business need or problem Define project goals and objectives Define the high-level scope Identify stakeholders Appoint the PM and key roles Develop the project charter Determine a high-level budget and timeline Conduct initial risk and feasibility analysis Align with organisational strategy
Definition — project charter

A project charter is a formal document that authorises the project and grants the project manager the authority to use organisational resources. Its purposes: it establishes a clear vision of the project, aligns stakeholders and secures executive support, provides a high-level road map for planning, and acts as a reference point throughout the project. The tools used to develop it are expert judgment, the business case, enterprise environmental factors and organisational process assets.

Charter elementWhat it fixesFails when…
Business need / problem statementWhy the organisation is spending money at allIt is a slogan rather than a problem with evidence
Measurable objectives and success criteriaWhat “done” and “successful” will meanObjectives cannot be measured, so nobody can disagree
High-level scope and boundariesWhat is in, and explicitly what is outExclusions are omitted, so every request looks in scope
Key stakeholders and sponsorWho authorises, who is affected, who decidesThe sponsor is named but never available
Project manager and authority levelWhat the PM may commit without escalationAuthority is implied rather than stated
High-level budget and milestonesThe envelope the plan must fit insideA date is promised with no resource behind it
High-level risks and constraintsWhat is already known to be dangerousKnown risks are kept out to make the case look strong
Approval and sign-offThe date and the signature that start the projectNobody can say when the project was authorised
Stakeholders

A stakeholder is anyone who impacts or is impacted by the project, positively or negatively — customers, sponsors, team members, suppliers, regulators and others. Identifying them early ensures buy-in and support, helps manage expectations and influence, reduces resistance and improves communication, and aligns project goals with stakeholder interests.

ActivityWhat you actually do
IdentifyBrainstorm, use expert judgment and read existing documents to list everyone affected
AnalyseDetermine each stakeholder's power, interest and influence
PrioritiseCategorise with a tool such as the Power/Interest Grid
Engage and communicateTailor the strategy to the stakeholder type — not one newsletter for everyone
Monitor engagementAdjust the plan as stakeholder needs and positions change

Tools and techniques in the defining stage

  • Expert judgment
  • Brainstorming and workshops
  • SWOT analysis
  • Stakeholder analysis
  • Meetings and interviews

Common challenges in initiation

  • Unclear objectives and scope
  • Lack of executive support
  • Poor stakeholder engagement
  • Incomplete or weak business case
  • Unrealistic timelines and budgets
  • Undefined roles and responsibilities
  • Inadequate risk awareness
You are the project manager for a new e-commerce platform. The CEO wants it live in six months, detailed requirements are undefined, IT is unsure the platform is feasible, and no charter exists. Which first step best fits the defining stage?
Facilitate a charter with the sponsor: business need, measurable objectives, high-level scope, key roles, and a high-level budget and timeline
Start a two-week sprint immediately so the team can show progress to the CEO
Wait until the marketing team documents every requirement, then begin defining the project

Selecting a Method — and Mixing Them

The deck never says one methodology is correct. It gives you four worked examples in which the choice follows from the constraints — which is exactly how the exam will ask about it.

Selection driverPredictive (waterfall)Agile (Scrum / Kanban)Hybrid
Requirement stabilitySettled and unlikely to changeEvolving, or expected to be learnedStable at the boundary, evolving inside it
Change toleranceLow: change is a formal requestHigh: change is expected inputChange inside a release, controlled across releases
Documentation and evidenceDocumentation is a deliverable in itselfWorking product valued over documentationEvidence produced for the gates, not for everything
Contract shapeFixed price, specification before mobilisingTime and materials, or capacity-basedFixed envelope with incremental statements of work
Delivery cadenceOne pass, milestones per phaseContinuous: sprints or flowIncrements released inside a stage-gated frame
Stakeholder availabilitySign-off at gates is enoughNeeds a Product Owner and available usersSponsor at gates, users in the increments
Risk postureRisk is front-loaded into analysis and designRisk is reduced by shipping small increments earlyHighest-risk work pulled into an early increment
Definition — hybrid delivery

Hybrid delivery combines a predictive frame with agile execution: the charter, the business case, the governance gates and the cost baseline are produced predictively, while the delivery of scope inside that frame runs in increments with a prioritised backlog. It is the common answer when a project must satisfy an approval and audit regime and requirements that are still moving.

Three shapes a hybrid takes

  • Discovery then deliver: a short predictive feasibility phase (the case study's “IT is unsure about feasibility”) followed by agile delivery.
  • Agile inside stage gates: sprints run continuously, but a release only passes a gate with its evidence pack.
  • Predictive outer, continuous inner: the contract and milestones are fixed; DevOps-style continuous delivery sits underneath them.

What gets you marked down

  • Naming a methodology without naming the constraint it answers
  • Claiming agile means no planning or no documentation
  • Claiming waterfall means no change control is needed
  • Forgetting that the lifecycle, the knowledge areas and the charter exist under every methodology
How to argue the recommendation. State the drivers that carry the most weight (here: undefined requirements at 0.20 and tolerance of change at 0.25), say what the scores show, then name the caveat: hybrid leads on the numbers, Scrum is close behind, and hybrid only works if the sponsor accepts a short discovery phase before the delivery increments begin.

Tutorial worked answer — the UniConnect trade-off meeting. Baseline from the Week 2 tutorial: scope = personal timetable, assessment reminders, campus map and emergency notifications; time = 12 weeks, ready before next semester; cost = maximum $180,000; quality expectation = the app must be secure, accessible and reliable. The project is outsourced to a local software company. Each change below is answered with the constraint that moved, the pressure it creates, and a response that protects the quality expectation.

1

Change A — scope increases (live chat and room booking added; deadline and budget unchanged). Two more features mean more analysis, design, development, integration and testing, so the project normally needs more time, more money, or both. Response: keep the four original features in the 12-week release and move live chat and room booking to a later release. If the sponsor insists on both at launch, the schedule or budget must be revised before the change is accepted. Quality risk: rushed testing on live chat, which may handle personal information, introduces security, privacy and reliability risk.

2

Change B — time is reduced (12 weeks to 8, about one third less; budget unchanged). Holding the full scope would need more people, which the fixed budget will not fund, so the realistic response is a smaller first release. Response: deliver the personal timetable and emergency notifications within eight weeks, then add assessment reminders and the campus map in a later release, confirming priorities with users and the sponsor. Quality risk: a compressed schedule squeezes security, accessibility, integration and reliability testing, which raises the chance of defects or an unsuccessful launch.

3

Change C — cost is reduced (by $45,000, from $180,000 to $135,000: a 25% cut; deadline unchanged). Fewer staff hours and specialist services can be funded, so keeping the whole scope would normally need more time — which the fixed deadline does not allow. Response: ask the sponsor and users to prioritise the original features: retain the personal timetable and emergency notifications for launch and defer the campus map and assessment reminders, while preserving essential security, accessibility and testing work. Quality risk: under-resourcing the outsourced team produces rushed work, technical debt and disputes about what the supplier can deliver for the reduced price.

4

The pattern behind all three answers. Scope grows, or time shrinks, or money shrinks — and because quality is the constraint the sponsor cannot see, it is the one that gets quietly absorbed unless the project manager makes the trade-off explicit. Name the constraint that moved, quantify the pressure, and offer a staged scope that keeps the deadline, the budget and the quality expectation intact.

Five phases, every time

Defining, planning, executing, monitoring & controlling, closing. A methodology changes the cadence, not the destination.

Lifecycle

Waterfall when requirements are settled

Linear phases, clear milestones, documentation as a deliverable — and expensive change once development starts.

Predictive

Scrum when priority must move

Time-boxed sprints, three roles, four events, three artefacts. It is a mindset before it is a set of meetings.

Agile

Kanban when work arrives continuously

Visualise the flow, limit WIP, measure cycle time, improve continuously. No sprints required.
