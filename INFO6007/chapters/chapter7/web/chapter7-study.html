What Risk Actually Is

The whole week turns on one distinction that exam answers get wrong again and again: a risk is not a problem. It is an uncertain event that may or may not happen — and it may help as easily as it hurts.

Definition

“A project risk is an uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, has a positive or negative effect on one or more project objectives (such as scope, schedule, cost, or quality).” The load-bearing word is uncertain: something that may or may not happen. Once it has happened it is no longer a risk.

Why IT projects need this more than most: IT projects are highly dynamic because of rapidly changing technologies, evolving user requirements, cybersecurity concerns and data privacy concerns. Every one of those is a source of uncertainty rather than a source of work, which is exactly what risk management is for.

The five main goals of risk management

Identify early

Find potential risks while there is still time and budget to do something about them.

Assess probability and impact

Judge how likely each risk is, and how much it would matter if it happened.

Develop strategies

Minimise threats and maximise opportunities — both directions, not just the bad one.

Monitor throughout

Track risks across the whole project lifecycle, not once at the start.

Enable decisions under uncertainty

Give the sponsor and the team evidence for choices they have to make anyway.

Avoid firefighting mode

Risk management is not a one-time task and not a separate process: it complements scope, schedule, cost and quality work.

The number worth remembering: the Standish Group CHAOS studies show that over 60% of IT projects fail to meet time, budget or scope objectives, and the major reason is poor risk management. That single statistic is the business case for this entire knowledge area.

The exam trap: the deck's list of threat responses has five entries, not four — escalation sits alongside avoid, mitigate, transfer and accept. Escalation applies when the risk is outside the project's scope (a company-wide cybersecurity risk, for example), so the project manager cannot respond to it and hands it to program or portfolio management. Transfer, by contrast, keeps the risk inside the project and moves the consequence to a third party through insurance, a vendor SLA or outsourcing.
A project manager discovers that the corporate network the project depends on has a known, unfixed vulnerability that only the enterprise security team can patch. What is the correct response?
Transfer — buy cyber-insurance and continue
Escalate — the risk sits outside the project's scope and belongs to a higher authority
Accept — note it in the register and take no action

The Six Processes of Risk Management

Risk management in IT runs as six processes. They are numbered for teaching, not for scheduling: identification, analysis and monitoring all continue across the project lifecycle rather than finishing once.

Stakeholders included Service NSW, Transport for NSW, NSW Police, licence holders and licence checkers. Identifying Risks

Identification determines which risks may affect the project and documents their characteristics. It is conducted early and continues throughout the lifecycle, and its whole point is to avoid being blindsided by threats or missing opportunities.

Risk breakdown structure (RBS)

A hierarchical representation of risk categories that helps the team think systematically through all possible risk sources, rather than listing whatever comes to mind first. The deck's five top-level categories are market, financial, technology, people, and structure/process.

Four identification techniques, with their real trade-offs

Brainstorming

  • A group creativity technique that generates a large number of risk ideas in a free-flowing, non-judgmental environment.
  • Good: quick, easy, encourages diverse perspectives; suits early-stage projects with high uncertainty.
  • Weak: may be dominated by louder voices; quality depends on facilitator skill.

Delphi technique

  • A structured method of obtaining consensus from a panel of experts through multiple rounds of anonymous surveys.
  • Good: avoids the influence of dominant personalities; effective for complex, uncertain projects needing expert judgement.
  • Weak: time-consuming because of the multiple rounds; relies on the availability of true experts.

Interviewing

  • Gathering risk information through structured or unstructured interviews with stakeholders, subject matter experts or experienced team members.
  • Good: provides deep insight and context; uncovers risks that are not obvious to the broader team.
  • Weak: can be subjective; risk of interviewer bias or incomplete information.

SWOT analysis

  • Assesses internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats; adaptable to both positive and negative risks.
  • Good: easy to use, covers internal and external factors, balances positive and negative risks.
  • Weak: can oversimplify complex risks; output quality depends on participants' knowledge.
Why stakeholders belong in identification: stakeholders may identify risks unknown to the project team; they improve the accuracy of probability and impact assessments; they build shared ownership of risks and responses; and they reduce resistance to change by being involved early. Effective risk communication is clear, consistent, timely, transparent and action-oriented.

Risk Appetite and Organisational Culture

How much risk a project is allowed to carry is a decision made above the project manager. Appetite and culture are the two settings that determine whether a response is even permissible.

Risk appetite

The degree of uncertainty an organisation is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives. Some organisations are risk-seeking (bold moves to innovate); others are risk-averse (stability and predictability come first).

Four factors that shape appetite

  • Industry context — tech startups often have a higher appetite than government agencies
  • Financial health — companies with strong reserves may tolerate more risk
  • Leadership mindset — risk-taking versus conservative decision-making
  • Stakeholder expectations — shareholders demanding high growth push appetite up

Three risk cultures

  • Risk-averse — avoids uncertainty, prefers compliance and detailed approvals, slow to innovate
  • Risk-neutral — balances opportunities and threats pragmatically
  • Risk-seeking — encourages experimentation, innovation and rapid scaling

Alignment is crucial: if risk appetite doesn't match the actual organisational culture, risk management fails.

PreferenceHow the stakeholder choosesWhat that means for a risk response
Risk aversePrefers to avoid uncertainty; will choose a certain but lower payoff over a risky higher payoffAcceptance is hard to justify; transfer and avoidance look attractive even at a price
Risk seekingPrefers risk; willing to take chances for a potentially higher payoffOpportunities get exploited and enhanced; a gamble may be accepted deliberately
Risk neutralIndifferent to risk; focuses only on expected monetary value (EMV)The response is chosen purely on EMV arithmetic — which is what the Foundations page does
Reading a risk utility function. The x-axis is monetary value (or project benefit); the y-axis is utility — the satisfaction or value a person or organisation derives from an outcome. A risk-averse curve flattens as money increases (each extra dollar buys less), a risk-seeking curve steepens, and a risk-neutral curve is a straight line. Two stakeholders looking at the same decision tree can therefore reach different answers without either being irrational.
A government agency and a venture-backed startup are both offered the same risky project with a positive EMV. The startup accepts it and the agency declines. What best explains the difference?
They have different risk appetites, and a risk-averse organisation does not decide on EMV alone
The agency calculated the EMV incorrectly
Government agencies have larger budgets, so they carry more risk

Worked example — ranking the NSW Digital Driver Licence register. Four risks were identified and rated qualitatively. Walk the ratings through to the priority decision.

1

R1 Peak launch demand — probability High, impact High → EXTREME. A high-likelihood, high-impact threat. The deck's instruction is to treat immediately: staged rollout, added capacity, load testing.

2

R2 Record integration errors — probability Medium, impact High → HIGH. Less likely than R1 but just as damaging, so the response is planned rather than immediate: assurance, integration testing, fallback procedures.

3

R3 Privacy or security issue — probability Low, impact Very high → HIGH. This is the case where impact carries the rating. Low probability does not license inaction when the impact is severe, and the plan's illustrative rule escalates any extreme identity, security, privacy or availability risk immediately.

4

R4 Trial feedback improves rollout — probability Medium, benefit Medium → MODERATE (opportunity). Rated on the same two axes, so it belongs in the register too; the action is to monitor and enhance rather than to treat it as noise.

5

The prioritised decision. Treat R1 immediately, plan R2 and R3 responses, and monitor and enhance the R4 opportunity. Note that three of the four ratings are driven by impact rather than probability — which is exactly why probability alone is a bad triage rule.

Quantitative Risk Analysis

Qualitative analysis ranks risks; quantitative analysis puts a number on them. It is a numerical, data-driven process that models the effect of risks on cost, schedule, scope and quality — and it is what justifies a contingency reserve.

What this step is for

Determining the probability of meeting deadlines, the expected cost overruns, and the overall risk exposure of the project. Its objectives are to measure the combined effect of individual risks on objectives, determine probabilities of success or failure, support decisions on contingency reserves, budget and schedule buffers, and prioritise the risks with the greatest quantitative impact.

Monte Carlo simulation

A probabilistic technique that uses random sampling to simulate possible outcomes. It runs thousands of iterations with varying inputs (time, cost, risk impact) and produces a range of outcomes instead of a single estimate.

  1. Define variables — identify uncertain factors such as task durations and costs, and assign probability distributions (Normal, Triangular, Beta).
  2. Run simulations — software randomly selects values within those distributions and runs the schedule or cost model thousands of times.
  3. Analyse results — read the distribution of outcomes, not the single most likely one.

Decision tree analysis

A visual and analytical tool for evaluating choices when outcomes are uncertain. It combines decision branches (choices) with chance branches (risks and probabilities) and is used to calculate Expected Monetary Value.

  1. Identify the decision point (for example, outsource versus develop in-house).
  2. List the alternatives as branches.
  3. Attach probabilities to each possible outcome.
  4. Assign payoffs or costs to the outcomes.
  5. Calculate the EMV of each branch, then select the highest.

The planned response package

For every risk worth acting on, the plan records six things. A response that is missing any of them is a wish, not a plan.

2. Design action
→
3. Assign owner
→
4. Set trigger

In the NSW licence project: strategy — avoid or mitigate threats, exploit or enhance opportunities; action — load testing, staged release, assurance and fallback; owners — the product, platform, integration and security leads; triggers — contingency, acceptance and escalation points. The full package adds the contingency or fallback and a residual-risk target.

Implementation is five moves, not one. Authorise the response; scale and load-test; resolve findings; prepare support and communications; record the residual risk. Responses then become scheduled, owned work — staged rollout, added capacity, security and integration fixes, support scripts and fallback procedures — against an explicit monitor list of action status, named owner, trigger reached, and residual exposure.

Worked example — the Week 7 case study. A mid-sized financial services company is migrating legacy systems to a cloud infrastructure, with multiple vendors, internal development teams and strict compliance requirements for sensitive customer data. Five risks were identified.

1

Classify and categorise each risk. Vendor A may delay delivery of migration tools — negative, market or structure/process (a supplier dependency). A new government regulation on customer data storage may take effect during the project — negative, market (external conditions). A 25% chance of data loss during migration — negative, technology. A senior developer is considering leaving — negative, people. Adopting AI-powered monitoring could improve uptime and cut post-migration costs — positive, technology.

2

Compute the EMV of the data-loss risk. With a 25% probability and a $200,000 impact: \(\text{EMV} = 0.25 \times \$200{,}000 = \$50{,}000\). That $50,000 is the average exposure this single risk contributes to the project's contingency reserve — not a prediction that $50,000 will be spent.

3

Recommend a response for the data-loss risk. Mitigate first: verified backups, a rehearsed restore, a dry-run migration on a copy, and a go/no-go checkpoint. Then transfer what can be transferred through the vendor contract and insurance, and state a residual-risk target.

4

Contingency or fallback for the developer leaving. People risk is reduced by knowledge transfer rather than by optimism: pair the developer on the migration runbook, document the architecture decisions, cross-train a second engineer, agree a retention or handover arrangement, and hold a named standby contractor. The trigger is the resignation, not the rumour; the fallback is a documented handover window.

5

Do not forget the fifth risk. The AI-monitoring opportunity is enhanced, not merely noted: pilot it on one service, measure the uptime change, and only then scale it. That is the difference between an opportunity on a register and an opportunity that pays.

The Risk Register

The register is the central document of risk management: every identified risk, its characteristics, and its planned response, in one place that people actually read.

Risk register

A central document that records all identified risks, their characteristics and planned responses. It acts as a living document, updated throughout the project lifecycle, and it ensures risks are visible, monitored, and assigned owners.

Capture, track, communicate

A structured way to record risks and to tell stakeholders what is being done about them.

Ensure accountability

Every risk carries a named owner. An unowned risk is an unmanaged risk.

Support decisions

It is a decision-making tool for prioritising work and planning responses, not an archive.

Monitor and Control Risks

Monitoring tracks identified risks, watches residual risks, finds new ones, and evaluates whether the risk process itself is working. It happens throughout the project — not once, and not only when something goes wrong.

1. Review register
→
2. Check triggers
→
3. Test response effectiveness
→
4. Identify new & residual
→
5. Update & communicate

What monitoring watches

  • New risks that have appeared since the last review
  • Residual risks after a response has been applied
  • Whether the response actually worked
  • Threshold breaches that trigger contingency

In the NSW licence project the evidence base was trial feedback, incident logs, capacity data, assurance findings and support demand — the register is kept relevant as conditions change.

Why it is a process and not an event

Ensuring the risk management plan remains relevant and effective is the stated purpose of this process. A register frozen at kick-off describes a project that no longer exists: risks close, new ones open, probabilities move, and responses acquire their own residual exposure.

Traditional, agile and hybrid approaches

ApproachWhen risks are handledArtefacts and practices
Traditional (waterfall)Identified, analysed and planned upfront during initiation and planningA risk register plus formal reviews
AgileAddressed iteratively throughout the project in short cycles (sprints, iterations); embedded in daily activities rather than only upfrontRetrospective risk reviews, risk-based backlog ordering, risk burn-down charts, collaborative ownership
HybridStructured traditional risk processes combined with agile practiceRegister and probability-impact matrix plus daily monitoring and adaptive responses

Agile risk practices

  • Iterative risk reviews — risks are revisited at the end of every sprint in the retrospective; continuous delivery gives early detection and reduces uncertainty.
  • Risk-based backlog prioritisation — the stories and features with higher uncertainty are delivered earlier, the “fail fast” principle. Testing a new cloud service early is the deck's example.
  • Risk burn-down charts — like a sprint burn-down, but they track risk exposure over time so you can see whether overall risk is rising or falling.
  • Collaborative ownership — risks are shared across the team rather than owned by a single “risk manager”, which encourages cross-functional visibility.

Advantages of agile and hybrid

  • Agile: faster risk detection, better stakeholder involvement through constant feedback, and adaptability to changing requirements.
  • Hybrid: balances flexibility with formal governance, which suits large IT projects with multiple stakeholders, regulators and vendors.
  • Implication: the choice is not “agile or risk management”. Agile changes the cadence of the same six processes — shorter cycles, the same register, the same owners.

Week 7 Recap

Risk is uncertain, and it cuts both ways

An uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, has a positive or negative effect on scope, schedule, cost or quality. Threats are managed with avoid, mitigate, transfer, accept, escalate; opportunities with exploit, enhance, share, accept, escalate.

Core definition

Six processes, run continuously

Plan, identify, qualitative analysis, quantitative analysis, plan and implement responses, monitor and control — with monitoring running underneath all of them for the whole lifecycle.

The process spine

Rank qualitatively, then quantify

Probability × impact and a matrix tell you where to look first; Monte Carlo and decision-tree EMV tell you what the combined exposure is worth. That number is what sizes the contingency reserve.

Analysis

The register is a living document

Risk event, category, trigger or evidence, owner, response with strategy and action, residual-risk target — updated throughout the project and reviewed on a stated cadence.
