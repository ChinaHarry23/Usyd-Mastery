Foundations: the arithmetic behind risk management

Week 7's case studies quote probabilities, cost effects, expected monetary values and a net exposure figure, and the lecture's closing questions ask you to compute one. This page supplies the notation, walks the NSW Digital Driver Licence numbers end to end, and then connects risk to the two quantitative techniques that feed it from the schedule and cost knowledge areas: the critical path through a WBS, and the earned-value indices that forecast an overrun before it happens.

The two axes of every rating

Probability is how likely the uncertain event is. Impact is how much it would matter against cost, time, scope and quality. Qualitative analysis multiplies ordinal ratings of the two; quantitative analysis multiplies a real probability by a real amount. Both end in the same sentence: this risk is worth this much attention.

Why probability alone cannot triage a register. The NSW licence project rates R3 (privacy or security issue) at low probability but very high impact, and the result is still a HIGH priority. A register sorted by probability would bury it; a register sorted by score surfaces it. The mathematics of the product is what forces both axes into the conversation.

Scores, scales and net expected exposure

Two calculations belong in every Week 7 answer: the ordinal risk score that sets priority, and the monetary expectation that sizes the reserve.

1. The risk score and its bands

Rate likelihood \(L\) and severity \(S\) on the same 1–5 scale and multiply:

\[ r_i = L_i \times S_i, \qquad r_i \in \{1,\dots,25\} \]

A workable banding is extreme at \(r \ge 15\), high at \(10 \le r < 15\), moderate at \(5 \le r < 10\), and low below that. The exact thresholds are a project decision recorded in the risk management plan — what matters is that thresholds exist, because “set thresholds: what level of risk is acceptable” is one of the stated objectives of planning risk management.

2. The probability–impact matrix, read as a grid

With a 5-point scale on both axes, the matrix is just \(r = L \times S\) drawn as a grid. Probability runs up the side, impact runs across the bottom.

\[ \begin{array}{c|ccccc} L \backslash S & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ \hline 5 & 5 & 10 & 15 & 20 & 25 \\ 4 & 4 & 8 & 12 & 16 & 20 \\ 3 & 3 & 6 & 9 & 12 & 15 \\ 2 & 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 & 10 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \end{array} \]

The bands fall out of the grid as diagonal stripes, which is why a shaded matrix and a threshold table always agree. Marking a risk moves it up or down the stripes, and response planning is the work of moving marks down-left for threats and up-right for opportunities.

3. Net expected exposure

Each risk contributes a signed expected value, \(\mathrm{EMV}_i = p_i \cdot I_i\), negative for opportunities because the benefit reduces exposure. The project's exposure is the sum:

\[ E_{\text{net}} \;=\; \sum_{i \in \text{threats}} p_i I_i \;-\; \sum_{j \in \text{opps}} p_j I_j \;=\; E_{\text{threat}} - E_{\text{opp}} \]

The contingency reserve is then set against \(E_{\text{net}}\) — either as a percentage of it or as the value at a chosen confidence level from a simulation. A negative \(E_{\text{net}}\) is not an error: it means the identified opportunities outweigh the identified threats, and the project is carrying upside.

EMV and decision trees

Decision tree analysis combines decision branches (choices you make) with chance branches (outcomes you do not control) and computes an expected monetary value for each choice.

The decision rule

A chance node's value is the probability-weighted sum of its outcomes; a decision node takes the best of its branches.

\[ \mathrm{EMV}(\text{node}) = \sum_{k} p_k \, V_k, \qquad \text{decide} = \arg\max_{\text{branches}} \mathrm{EMV} \]

Costs are carried as negative values, so “best” means the largest (least negative) expected value. Subtract the cost of a branch inside that branch, before comparing, or the comparison is meaningless.

Worked: build in-house or outsource?

1

Decision point. The project must deliver an integration component. Option A: build in-house for A$400k, with a 70% chance of succeeding and a 30% chance of a rework cost of A$600k. Option B: outsource for A$600k, with a 90% chance of succeeding at that price and a 10% chance of remediation costing another A$200k.

2

In-house chance node. \(\mathrm{EMV}_A = 0.70 \times (-400{,}000) + 0.30 \times (-400{,}000 - 600{,}000) = -280{,}000 - 300{,}000 = -\mathrm{A}\$580{,}000\).

3

Outsource chance node. \(\mathrm{EMV}_B = 0.90 \times (-600{,}000) + 0.10 \times (-600{,}000 - 200{,}000) = -540{,}000 - 80{,}000 = -\mathrm{A}\$620{,}000\).

4

Decision. Building in-house has the higher expected value (−A$580k beats −A$620k), so a risk-neutral decision maker builds in-house. The downside, however, is worse: A$1.0m versus A$0.8m. That asymmetry is the whole reason risk appetite matters — a risk-averse organisation can rationally buy the A$40k of expected value for A$200k less downside exposure.

5

What to say in an exam. State the EMV of each branch, name the higher one, and then name the qualitative caveat: EMV assumes risk neutrality and repeatable decisions, and this decision is neither.

A vendor offers a fixed A$50,000 SLA-backed integration service. The in-house alternative has an EMV of −A$45,000 and a 20% chance of a A$200,000 failure. Which statement is defensible?
In-house is always correct because its EMV is higher
Outsourcing is defensible: the A$5,000 expected saving does not compensate for the A$200,000 tail risk
Outsourcing removes the risk from the project entirely

Simulation, samples and the contingency

EMV handles one risk at a time. Monte Carlo simulation handles all of them at once, by sampling every uncertain input together and reading the distribution of outcomes.

How a Monte Carlo answer is read

Run the model \(N\) times and sort the outcomes. The value at the \(c\)-th percentile is the \(c\)% confidence estimate; the contingency is the gap between it and the deterministic baseline.

\[ D_{c} = F^{-1}(c), \qquad CR_{c} = D_{c} - D_{\text{base}}, \qquad D_{c} \approx \mu + z_c \sigma \ \ (\text{normal approximation}) \]

with \(z_{0.90}=1.28\) and \(z_{0.95}=1.65\). The normal approximation is only a sanity check on the simulated numbers — the simulation itself makes no normality assumption, which is the point of using it.

Worked: a P90 contingency in three lines

A simulation of the delivery schedule returns a mean duration of 23 days with a standard deviation of 2.2 days.

\[ D_{90} \approx 23 + 1.28 \times 2.2 = 25.8\ \text{days} \qquad CR_{90} = 25.8 - 23 = 2.8\ \text{days} \]

The reading: commit to 27 days and hold roughly four days of schedule reserve, or commit to 23 days and accept a 50% chance of being late. Both are legitimate; only the second is a promise the data does not support.

Define variables

Identify the uncertain factors — task durations, costs, risk impacts — and assign each a probability distribution: Normal, Triangular, Beta, or an empirical one drawn from history. The distribution is the model's only place for optimism to hide, so it is where the argument happens.

Run, then analyse

Software samples within each distribution and runs the schedule or cost model thousands of times. You then read a range of possible outcomes instead of a single estimate: the probability of finishing by a date, the expected overrun, and the confidence level that a given reserve buys.

What simulation changes about the conversation. A single-point estimate invites the question “will we hit it?”, which has a yes-or-no answer nobody can give. A distribution invites “what does 90% confidence cost, and is it worth buying?” — a question with a number attached, and one that a risk-averse or risk-seeking organisation will answer differently.

Worked solution — the five-risk cloud migration from the Week 7 case study. Compute the EMV of the data-loss risk, then decide what the numbers license you to claim.

1

Extract the inputs. Probability \(p = 0.25\); impact \(I = \$200{,}000\). Everything else in the scenario — vendor delay, regulation, developer attrition, AI monitoring — has no stated probability attached, and an EMV without a probability is not computable.

2

Compute. \(\mathrm{EMV} = p \times I = 0.25 \times 200{,}000 = \$50{,}000\).

3

Interpret. $50,000 is the average exposure this risk contributes across many comparable migrations. It is not a forecast that $50,000 will be spent: in the single run that happens, the cost is the full $200,000; in the runs that do not, it is zero. The expected value is the right input to a contingency calculation and the wrong input to a cash-flow forecast for one project.

4

Extend to the whole register. If all five risks had probabilities and impacts, \(E_{\text{net}} = \sum p_i I_i\) with the opportunity's contribution subtracted, and the contingency would answer to that total. That is precisely the calculation the NSW licence register demonstrates.

5

State the limitation. Risks are not independent: a vendor delay raises the odds of a rushed migration, which raises the odds of data loss. Adding EMVs treats them as independent, so a simulation is the better tool when the dependencies matter — and saying so is worth marks.

WBS, network and the critical path

Schedule risk has its own arithmetic, and it starts from the work breakdown structure: you cannot estimate, sequence or crash work that has not been decomposed. This is the Week 3 toolkit, worked here because it is the schedule half of quantitative risk analysis.

Why this belongs on a risk page

The critical path is the chain with zero float — the activities that, if any one of them slips, move the project end date. That makes it the schedule-risk map: risk responses, buffers and monitoring attention all go where the float is zero, because float is the only schedule shock absorber the network has.

2

For C, whose only successor is E: \(\mathrm{LF}_C = \mathrm{LS}_E = 9\), so \(\mathrm{LS}_C = 4\). For D, whose only successor is F: \(\mathrm{LF}_D = \mathrm{LS}_F = 13\), so \(\mathrm{LS}_D = 11\). For B: \(\mathrm{LF}_B = \mathrm{LS}_D = 11\), so \(\mathrm{LS}_B = 8\).

3

Float. \(\text{Float} = \mathrm{LS} - \mathrm{ES} = \mathrm{LF} - \mathrm{EF}\). Zero for A, C, E, F and G — which is the critical path — and 4 for both B and D.

4

Three-point estimates. Replace each single duration with \(t_e = (o + 4m + p)/6\): A 4.0, B 3.0, C 5.0, D 2.0, E 4.0, F 6.0, G 4.0 — the same durations, so the deterministic path still runs 23 days.

5

Schedule uncertainty. Sum the variances of the critical activities only — A, C, E, F and G — because the other branches have float and therefore do not drive the finish date: \(\sigma^2 = 0.44+0.25+0.69+1.00+0.44 = 2.82\), so \(\sigma = \sqrt{2.82} = 1.68\) days. A 90% confidence date is \(23 + 1.28 \times 1.68 = 25.2\) days — roughly two to three days of schedule reserve on this chain, and none of it needed on B or D while their float holds.

6

The risk reading. B and D each show four days of float, but they are drawing on the same four days: D cannot start until B finishes and F cannot start until D does, so a three-day slip on B consumes three of the four for both. This is the classic trap: float is shared, not owned. If B is a vendor-dependent activity, that is where the response — expediting, a performance bond, or a second supplier — earns its cost.

Activities B and D each show 4 days of float, and D follows B. B slips by 3 days. What happens to the project end date?
Nothing changes at all — the slip is absorbed and the float is untouched
The end date still holds, but only 1 day of shared float remains; a further slip beyond one day would delay the finish
The project end date slips by 3 days immediately

Earned value as a cost-risk signal

Earned value management converts three numbers into a forecast. In risk terms it is the monitor-and-control instrument: it tells you that a cost threat has already materialised, while there is still time to respond.

The definitions, exactly

At a status date: \(\mathrm{PV}\) is the budgeted value of the work planned by then, \(\mathrm{EV}\) the budgeted value of the work actually completed, and \(\mathrm{AC}\) what that completed work has actually cost.

\[ \mathrm{CV} = \mathrm{EV} - \mathrm{AC}, \qquad \mathrm{SV} = \mathrm{EV} - \mathrm{PV}, \qquad \mathrm{CPI} = \frac{\mathrm{EV}}{\mathrm{AC}}, \qquad \mathrm{SPI} = \frac{\mathrm{EV}}{\mathrm{PV}} \]

A negative variance means trouble; an index below 1 means trouble. A negative SV on a project with a critical path that cannot move is a schedule risk that has already converted into a threat, which is why SV and SPI belong in the risk review even though they are cost-area metrics.

Forecasting the overrun

\[ \mathrm{EAC}_{\mathrm{CPI}} = \frac{\mathrm{BAC}}{\mathrm{CPI}}, \qquad \mathrm{EAC}_{\text{atypical}} = \mathrm{AC} + (\mathrm{BAC} - \mathrm{EV}), \qquad \mathrm{ETC} = \mathrm{EAC} - \mathrm{AC}, \qquad \mathrm{VAC} = \mathrm{BAC} - \mathrm{EAC} \]

The choice between the two EAC forms is a risk judgement. Dividing by CPI assumes the current cost performance continues — the pessimistic case, and the right one when the cause is systemic. Adding the remaining budget assumes the deviation was a one-off — defensible only when you can name the one-off.

A project reports \(\mathrm{CPI} = 0.80\) and \(\mathrm{SPI} = 0.95\) at the halfway point. Which risk reading is most defensible?
The schedule is the problem; the cost index is close enough to 1 to ignore
Both threats have materialised; the cost one is severe, and a halfway-point CPI this low justifies the systemic EAC

Worked solution — choosing a contingency and defending it. A project has a cost baseline of A$2.0m. Simulation gives a mean completion cost of A$2.1m with a standard deviation of A$150k. Risk appetite is documented as “risk-averse: commit at 90% confidence or better”.

1

Translate appetite into a percentile. “90% confidence or better” means the \(D_{90}\) figure, not the mean. Appetite is not decoration: it selects which point of the distribution becomes the commitment.

2

Compute the point estimate. \(D_{90} = 2{,}100{,}000 + 1.28 \times 150{,}000 = 2{,}100{,}000 + 192{,}000 = \mathrm{A}\$2{,}292{,}000\).

3

Size the reserve. Against the baseline, \(CR_{90} = 2{,}292{,}000 - 2{,}000{,}000 = \mathrm{A}\$292{,}000\). Against the mean it is A$192,000 — so the two components are worth separating: A$100,000 closes the gap between baseline and mean (the expected drift), and A$192,000 buys the confidence the appetite demands.

4

Name what the reserve is not. Contingency answers identified risks and sits under the project manager's control. Unidentified risks are answered by the management reserve, which is controlled above the project manager. Quoting one figure for both is the error this step exists to prevent.

5

Sanity-check the response. If a A$292,000 reserve is unaffordable, the answer is not to lower the confidence target silently — it is to change the risks. Re-plan the highest-EMV threats, then re-run the simulation and read the new \(D_{90}\).
