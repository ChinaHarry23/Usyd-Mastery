Definitions that must be exact

Cost questions are marked on wording as much as on arithmetic. Each definition below is written so that the consequence is included, because the consequence is what the marker is looking for.

Cost

A resource sacrificed or foregone to achieve a specific objective, or something given up in exchange. Costs are usually measured in monetary units such as dollars.

Project cost management

The processes involved in planning, estimating, budgeting, financing, funding, managing and controlling costs so that the project can be completed within the approved budget.

Cost baseline

The approved, time-phased budget for the project, built by summing estimated costs by work package and scheduling them over time. It includes direct costs, indirect costs and contingency reserves, and it is the benchmark against which actual performance is measured. Because it is time-phased, the planned spend to any date is the Planned Value.

Contingency reserve vs management reserve

A contingency reserve allows for future situations that may be partially planned for and is included inside the project cost baseline. A management reserve allows for future situations that are unpredictable and sits outside the cost baseline, inside the total project budget.

Planned Value, Earned Value, Actual Cost

PV — the budgeted cost of work planned to be done by a certain date. EV — the budgeted cost of work actually completed by a certain date; the value of the physical work done. AC — the real expenditure for the work performed, including direct and indirect costs.

WBS, cost roll-up and the critical path

Cost management sits on top of Week 3's work. The WBS gives you the units to estimate, and the critical path tells you which durations actually drive the schedule — and therefore which delays drive cost.

1. The critical path is the longest path, not the shortest

For each path of dependent activities, add the durations. The path with the longest total duration is the critical path; activities on it normally have zero float, so they cannot be delayed without delaying the project.

The lecture's online booking website has two paths:

\[ \text{Requirements} \to \text{Interface design} \to \text{Development} \to \text{Testing and launch} = 13\ \text{days} \] \[ \text{Requirements} \to \text{Hosting setup} \to \text{Content preparation} \to \text{Testing and launch} = 12\ \text{days} \]

The first path is critical. The second has \(13 - 12 = 1\) day of float, so it could slip by one day without moving the launch date.

2. The tutorial's activity-on-node chain

Requirements (2 weeks) → Design (3) → Development (6) → Testing (4) → Deployment (2). With a single chain every activity is on the critical path, so the project duration is the sum:

\[ D = 2 + 3 + 6 + 4 + 2 = 17\ \text{weeks} \]

If development takes 8 weeks instead of 6, the duration becomes:

\[ D' = 2 + 3 + 8 + 4 + 2 = 19\ \text{weeks} \]

The critical path is unchanged — with one chain there is nothing else it could be — and the project finishes two weeks later.

Why this belongs on a cost page. A two-week slip on the critical path is not only a schedule event. If six people work on that path at 40 hours a week and the plan's rate is $60/hour, the extra direct cost is \(2 \times 6 \times 40 \times \$60 = \$28{,}800\) before any re-planning or penalty. Duration and cost are the same decision seen from two sides.
A network has two paths: path A totals 17 weeks and path B totals 14 weeks. Development on path B slips by 2 weeks. What happens to the project duration?

Estimating formulas

Three relationships cover almost every estimating question: effort times rate, a weighted three-point average, and a benchmark rate applied to a count.

1. Bottom-up: effort × rate, then roll up

Each work package cost is the effort multiplied by the rate in the cost management plan, and the estimate is their sum:

\[ C = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( e_i \times r_i \right) \]

The lecture's mobile banking app example: \(120 \times \$60 = \$7{,}200\) for login, \(300 \times \$60 = \$18{,}000\) for payments, \(200 \times \$50 = \$10{,}000\) for testing and \(\$12{,}000\) for a year of cloud hosting.

\[ C = 7{,}200 + 18{,}000 + 10{,}000 + 12{,}000 = \$47{,}200 \] \[ \text{Contingency} = 0.10 \times 47{,}200 = \$4{,}720 \qquad \text{Baseline} = \$51{,}920 \]

2. Three-point (PERT) estimating

Weight the most likely value four times, and give the extremes one weight each:

\[ E = \frac{O + 4M + P}{6} \]

For the lecture's cloud infrastructure setup with \(O = \$8{,}000\), \(M = \$12{,}000\) and \(P = \$20{,}000\):

\[ E = \frac{8{,}000 + 4(12{,}000) + 20{,}000}{6} = \frac{76{,}000}{6} = \$12{,}666.67 \approx \$12{,}667 \]

A standard extension (beyond the deck's example, but useful for showing the spread) is the activity standard deviation \(\sigma = (P - O)/6\), which here is \((20{,}000 - 8{,}000)/6 = \$2{,}000\). The point estimate is $12,667 with roughly $2,000 of one-sigma uncertainty around it — which is the honest way to quote a PERT result.

3. Parametric and benchmarking relations

A parametric estimate applies a rate to a measurable driver, and benchmarking supplies that rate from history or industry averages:

\[ C = \text{driver} \times \text{rate per unit} \]

The lecture's benchmarks: \(\$150\)–\(\$250\) per user migrated to cloud services, a cost per function point or story point, and \(\$40{,}000\)–\(\$60{,}000\) for a two-week agile sprint.

Applied to the digital licence project's rollout, which the deck sizes at 18,500+ trial users:

\[ 18{,}500 \times \$150 = \$2{,}775{,}000 \qquad 18{,}500 \times \$250 = \$4{,}625{,}000 \]

That range is not an estimate to put in a baseline. It is a sanity check: an estimate outside \(\$2.8\text{m}\)–\(\$4.6\text{m}\) for that migration needs a written explanation.

The budget stack

Three lines of arithmetic turn estimates into an authorised budget. Getting the boundary between contingency and management reserve right is the single most examined detail of the week.

1. From estimates to total budget

\[ \text{Cost baseline} = \text{cost estimates} + \text{contingency reserve} \] \[ \text{Total project budget} = \text{cost baseline} + \text{management reserve} \]

Reserves are normally expressed as a percentage of the estimate base, so \(\text{contingency} = p_c \times \text{estimates}\) and \(\text{management reserve} = p_m \times \text{estimates}\).

2. Worked on the student portal case study

The lecture's case: a six-month online student portal, planned cost of $180,000 by month, with a 10% contingency reserve and a 5% management reserve.

\[ \text{Contingency} = 0.10 \times 180{,}000 = \$18{,}000 \qquad \text{Cost baseline} = \$198{,}000 \] \[ \text{Management reserve} = 0.05 \times 180{,}000 = \$9{,}000 \qquad \text{Total budget} = \$207{,}000 \]

Note the exam convention. When a question gives you a “total budget” and then a monthly plan that adds up to that same number, the monthly plan is being used as the estimate base — so BAC for the earned value arithmetic is $180,000, and the reserves are stacked on top of it. State your reading in the answer; examiners mark the reasoning as well as the number.

3. Time-phasing creates Planned Value

Spreading the baseline across the schedule is what turns a total into a PV curve. Cumulative planned cost to the end of month \(k\) is:

\[ PV_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \text{planned cost in month } i \]

For the student portal: \(PV_1 = \$20{,}000\), \(PV_2 = \$55{,}000\), \(PV_3 = \$85{,}000\), \(PV_4 = \$120{,}000\), \(PV_5 = \$150{,}000\), \(PV_6 = \$180{,}000\).

Worked example — the full student portal case. Six-month portal, $180,000 planned, contingency 10%, management reserve 5%. At the end of Month 3 the course registration module is fully done and exam scheduling is 50% complete; EV is $80,000 and AC is $85,000. PV is the sum of the first three months' planned costs. Calculate CV, SV, CPI, SPI and EAC (assuming CPI continues), then interpret.

1

Establish the three inputs. \(PV = 20{,}000 + 35{,}000 + 30{,}000 = \$85{,}000\). \(EV = \$80{,}000\) (given). \(AC = \$85{,}000\) (given). \(BAC = \$180{,}000\).

2

Variances. \(CV = EV - AC = 80{,}000 - 85{,}000 = -\$5{,}000\): over budget by $5,000. \(SV = EV - PV = 80{,}000 - 85{,}000 = -\$5{,}000\): behind schedule by $5,000 worth of work.

3

Indexes. \(CPI = EV/AC = 80{,}000/85{,}000 = 0.9412\): about 94 cents of value per dollar spent. \(SPI = EV/PV = 80{,}000/85{,}000 = 0.9412\): progressing at about 94% of the planned rate.

4

Forecast. With current cost performance continuing, \(EAC = BAC/CPI = 180{,}000/0.9412 = \$191{,}250\). So \(VAC = BAC - EAC = -\$11{,}250\), an overrun of about $11,250, and \(ETC = EAC - AC = 191{,}250 - 85{,}000 = \$106{,}250\).

5

Interpret both dimensions together. The project is marginally over budget and marginally behind schedule, and the two are consistent with each other: the same under-delivery that shows up in SV is showing up as unrecovered cost in CV.

6

Check the reserves. Spend to date is $85,000 of a $198,000 baseline including contingency — about 43%. The 10% contingency ($18,000) is still largely intact, and the forecast overrun of $11,250 is inside it, so no management reserve release is needed yet. If CPI falls further, that changes.

7

State the alternative reading. If the examiner intends BAC to include contingency, then \(EAC = 198{,}000/0.9412 = \$210{,}375\). The interpretation does not change; only the size of the overrun does. Show the assumption you used and you will not lose the mark either way.

Earned value, fully worked

Every earned value question is the same four steps: identify PV, EV and AC; subtract for variances; divide for indexes; then forecast with a stated assumption. Here is the whole chain on the lecture's figures.

1. The formulas

\[ EV = \%\,\text{complete} \times BAC \qquad PV = \%\,\text{planned} \times BAC \] \[ CV = EV - AC \qquad SV = EV - PV \] \[ CPI = \frac{EV}{AC} \qquad SPI = \frac{EV}{PV} \] \[ EAC = \frac{BAC}{CPI} \qquad EAC = AC + (BAC - EV) \qquad EAC = AC + \frac{BAC - EV}{CPI \times SPI} \] \[ ETC = EAC - AC \qquad VAC = BAC - EAC \]

2. Worked: an IT project with BAC $100,000 over six months

At the end of month 2 the team has completed 30% of the work and spent $40,000, while the plan says 35% should be complete.

\[ BAC = \$100{,}000 \qquad AC = \$40{,}000 \] \[ EV = 0.30 \times 100{,}000 = \$30{,}000 \qquad PV = 0.35 \times 100{,}000 = \$35{,}000 \] \[ CV = 30{,}000 - 40{,}000 = -\$10{,}000 \quad \text{(over budget by } \$10{,}000\text{)} \] \[ SV = 30{,}000 - 35{,}000 = -\$5{,}000 \quad \text{(behind by } \$5{,}000 \text{ of work)} \] \[ CPI = \frac{30{,}000}{40{,}000} = 0.75 \qquad SPI = \frac{30{,}000}{35{,}000} = 0.857 \] \[ EAC = \frac{100{,}000}{0.75} = \$133{,}333 \qquad VAC = 100{,}000 - 133{,}333 = -\$33{,}333 \]

Reading: for every $1 spent the project earns only $0.75 of value, and it is progressing at 85.7% of the planned rate. If nothing changes, it finishes about $33,333 over budget.

3. Choosing the EAC formula is the exam question

The exam answer template

Earned value questions are long-answer questions in disguise: the arithmetic is small, and the marks are in the sequence and the interpretation. Run this order every time.

Tutorial self-assessment

The Week 4 tutorial is a scope-and-schedule session that every part of can be re-read through a cost lens: unrequested features become unrequested cost, the WBS becomes the estimating unit, and the critical path becomes the cost driver.

Part A — scope creep risks

Three risks of accepting new features at a fixed budget: delivery delays as the extra features extend development, budget overruns as unplanned work consumes resources and testing, and reduced quality as the same team absorbs more work in the same time.

Part A — two strategies

A formal change control process so every new request is evaluated for its impact on time, cost and resources, and a clear scope statement with validation so inclusions and exclusions are agreed early and signed off at milestones.

Part B — the WBS

Three levels for the HR system: Requirements (gathering, validation), Design (architecture, UI/UX), Development (front-end, back-end, integration), Testing (unit, system, UAT), Deployment (training and documentation, production deployment).

Part C — dependencies

Requirements (2) → Design (3) → Development (6) → Testing (4) → Deployment (2) = 17 weeks. With development at 8 weeks the duration becomes 19 weeks and the critical path is unchanged.

Worked answer — the cost of the tutorial's scope-creep request. The client asks for a mobile app, payroll analytics and multilingual support with no change to budget or timeline. Answer the question the tutorial actually asks, but in the language of Week 4.

1

Quantify before you negotiate. The three features are three new work packages. At the rates in the cost management plan, an illustrative costing is: mobile app 200 h × $60 = $12,000; payroll analytics 150 h × $60 = $9,000; multilingual support 120 h × $60 = $7,200; integration and regression testing 150 h × $50 = $7,500. Subtotal $35,700.

2

Attach the risk cost, not just the build cost. Integration risk is now identified, so contingency is legitimate: 10% of $35,700 = $3,570, for $39,270. The management reserve is not available for this: the request is known, not unpredictable.

3

Trace it to the schedule. The work is additive and lands on development and testing, which are on the critical path. Two extra weeks of a six-person team at 40 hours and $60/hour is \(2 \times 6 \times 40 \times \$60 = \$28{,}800\) of direct labour before the new features are built at all — which is why accepting scope at a fixed deadline costs more than the feature list suggests.

4

Apply the two strategies. Send the request through formal change control (impact on time, cost, resources, and a decision), and validate scope at the next milestone against a scope statement that named these features as exclusions.

5

Offer the three honest options. Sponsor funds the change and the baseline is re-approved; the team delivers the same value by descoping lower-priority items; or the features wait for a later release. Absorbing them silently converts identified contingency into unidentified scope, which is precisely how the overruns in the lecture's case studies began.
