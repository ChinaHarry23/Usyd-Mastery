What Project Cost Management Is

Week 4 is the money half of the triple constraint. Scope and schedule have already been planned; now every work package has to be priced, summed into an authorised baseline, and watched against reality.

Definition from the lecture

Project Cost Management includes the processes involved in planning, estimating, budgeting, financing, funding, managing, and controlling costs so that the project can be completed within the approved budget. In short: it is the process of estimating, budgeting and controlling costs, covering resources, labour, infrastructure, tools, vendors and risk reserves.

The claim that frames the whole week: a project that is on time but over budget is considered a failure by stakeholders. Schedule performance alone never tells you whether the project is healthy.
ProcessWhat it does (lecture wording)Main output
Planning cost managementDetermining the policies, procedures and documentations used for planning, executing and controlling project costCost management plan
Estimating costsDeveloping an approximation or estimate of the costs of the resources needed to complete the projectCost estimates per activity and work package
Determining the budgetAllocating the overall cost estimate to individual work items to establish a baseline for measuring performanceCost baseline and total project budget
Controlling costsControlling changes to the project budgetVariance reports, forecasts, approved changes

Why IT cost management is harder than it looks

What makes IT volatile

  • Rapidly changing technology → volatile pricing on hardware, cloud and tooling
  • Hidden costs — integration, security and compliance work that nobody scoped
  • Vendor lock-in and licence renewals — recurring costs that outlive the project

What poor cost management costs you

  • Project cancellation
  • Loss of stakeholder trust
  • Missed opportunities and sunk costs that can never be recovered

Types of Cost and the Two Reserves

A cost is a resource sacrificed or foregone to achieve a specific objective, or something given up in exchange. Costs are normally measured in monetary units — dollars — but not every cost is measurable, and not every cost belongs in the plan.

Cost typeLecture definitionIT example
TangibleCosts an organisation can easily measure in dollarsDeveloper hours, cloud hosting, licences, vendor fees
IntangibleCosts that are difficult to measure in monetary termsLoss of user goodwill after a bad release, team burnout, reputation damage
DirectCosts directly related to producing the products and services of the projectHours booked to a work package, a test environment bought for this project
IndirectCosts not directly related to the products or services but indirectly related to performing the projectOverhead, administration, office space, shared tooling
SunkMoney that has already been spent in the past when deciding what projects to invest inThe abandoned prototype you already paid for — not included in the project management plan
Reserve

A reserve is the amount of money included in a cost estimate to mitigate cost risk by allowing for future situations that are difficult to predict. The lecture splits reserves into exactly two kinds — and the split matters, because only one of them sits inside the cost baseline.

Contingency reserve

Allows for future situations that may be partially planned for. It is included in the project cost baseline, so the project manager can normally draw on it without a sponsor-level change.

Inside the baseline

Management reserve

Allows for future situations that are unpredictable. It sits outside the cost baseline but inside the total project budget, and releasing it is a management decision.

Outside the baseline

Why the distinction is examined: if you treat the management reserve as spendable project money, your cost baseline is wrong and your earned-value variances will look healthier than they are. Ask instead: was this risk identified and sized in advance? If yes, it is contingency; if no, it is management reserve.
A project is hit by a global hardware price rise that no one identified during planning. Which pot of money is designed for that situation?
The contingency reserve, because it is inside the cost baseline
The management reserve, because the situation is unpredictable
The sunk cost pool, because the money was already spent

Planning Cost Management: the Cost Management Plan

Planning cost management defines how project costs will be estimated, budgeted, managed, monitored and controlled. It is the framework for every cost decision taken later in the lifecycle — and it is a document, not a discussion.

Question the plan answersWeak answerAnswer that survives an audit
How are costs estimated?“The team estimates them”Bottom-up by work package, with three-point estimating for uncertain integration work
What is the unit of account?UnstatedOne currency, one rate card, one contingency percentage
What is the baseline?“The budget”Approved, time-phased budget = cost estimates + contingency reserve
How is performance measured?Cash spent so farPV, EV and AC together, with CPI and SPI thresholds
How does the baseline change?Verbally, when neededCost impact analysis plus formal approval; authorised changes only
Tie back to Week 2: the cost management plan is a subsidiary plan of the project management plan. It does not replace the charter's budget authorisation; it explains how that authorisation will be spent, measured and protected.

Estimating Costs

Estimating costs is the process of developing an approximation of the monetary resources needed to complete project activities. Its purpose is to predict costs as accurately as possible so stakeholders can allocate resources and approve budgets.

Why estimating is hard in IT

  • Uncertain requirements → costs are harder to lock in
  • Rapid technology change → hardware and software costs fluctuate
  • Labour-intensive work → developer hours are routinely underestimated
  • Stakeholder decisions, such as adding a feature, change cost directly

The five techniques the lecture gives you

  • Analogous — historical data from similar projects; quick but less accurate
  • Parametric — statistical or quantitative models
  • Bottom-up — estimate each work package, then roll up
  • Three-point (PERT) — accounts for uncertainty
  • Expert judgement — domain experts or consultants
TechniqueHow it worksAccuracyBest used when
AnalogousReuse the actual cost of a similar past project, adjusted for sizeLow — quick but less accurateEarly, in a business case or a bid, when detail does not exist yet
ParametricApply a statistical or quantitative model, e.g. cost per user migrated or cost per story pointMedium to high, if the model fitsRepeated, comparable work with historical data behind the model
Bottom-upEstimate every work package or task, then roll the estimates up to a totalHighest — most detailed, so most accurateOnce the WBS from Week 3 is stable and owners are known
Three-point (PERT)Weight optimistic, most likely and pessimistic values to allow for uncertaintyImproves the estimate's realismWork with real uncertainty: integration, security assurance, data migration
Expert judgementRely on domain experts or consultantsDepends entirely on the expertSpecialist work with no usable history, and to sanity-check the other four

What affects accuracy

  • Project phase — early estimates are rougher than late ones
  • Quality of requirements — clear versus vague
  • Estimation technique chosen
  • Availability of historical data
  • Team expertise

Common mistakes

  • Underestimating integration, testing and maintenance
  • Ignoring hidden costs: training, security compliance, vendor support
  • Over-reliance on optimistic timelines
  • Forgetting currency fluctuations in global projects
Benchmarking: using historical project data or industry averages to guide cost estimation. The lecture's examples: average cost per user migrated to cloud services ($150–$250 per user), cost per function point or story point in software development, and average sprint cost for an agile team ($40K–$60K for two weeks). Benefits: it anchors estimates in realistic ranges, highlights outliers that need review, and builds a reusable organisational cost knowledge base.

Worked example — costing the tutorial's scope creep. The Week 4 tutorial scenario: a team is building an HR management system, and halfway through the client asks for three new features — a mobile app, payroll analytics and multilingual support — with no change to budget or timeline. Price the request and decide what to do with it.

1

Estimate bottom-up, at work-package level. The three features become three new work packages, each with build hours and test hours. Rates come from the project's own cost management plan; the lecture's sample rates are $60/hr for development and $50/hr for testing.

2

Build the estimate. Mobile app: 200 hrs × $60 = $12,000. Payroll analytics: 150 hrs × $60 = $9,000. Multilingual support: 120 hrs × $60 = $7,200. Integration and regression testing for all three: 150 hrs × $50 = $7,500. Subtotal = $35,700.

3

Add contingency only for identified risk. The integration risk is identified in the risk register, so a contingency allowance is legitimate: 10% of $35,700 = $3,570, giving $39,270. There is no case for a management reserve: the request is known, not unpredictable.

4

Say where the money would come from. Either the sponsor releases additional funds and the baseline is re-approved, or the same value is delivered by descoping work of lower priority. What must not happen is silent absorption — that is how a project spends its contingency on unidentified scope and then has nothing left for the risks it did identify.

5

Name the consequence of doing nothing. The tutorial's three risks follow directly: delivery delays as the features extend development, budget overrun as unplanned work consumes resources and testing, and reduced quality because the same team absorbs more work in the same time.

Determining the Budget: the Cost Baseline

Determining the budget aggregates the estimated costs of individual activities or work packages to establish an authorised cost baseline. The result is the total project budget that will be used to monitor and control costs.

The budget stack

Cost estimates
+ contingency reserves
= cost baseline (the approved budget without management reserves)
+ management reserves
= total project budget

The lecture states it as Budget = Cost Estimates + Contingency Reserves + Management Reserves, and separately as Total Project Budget = Cost Baseline + Management Reserves. Both say the same thing: only contingency sits inside the baseline.

Budget componentWhat belongs in itInside the cost baseline?
Direct costsSalaries, hardware, software, vendor feesYes
Indirect costsOverhead, administration, office spaceYes
Contingency reservesBuffer for identified risks that were partially planned forYes
Management reservesBuffer for unknown, unpredictable risksNo — added on top to give the total project budget
Cost baseline

The approved, time-phased budget for the project. It is developed by summing estimated costs by work package and scheduling them over time, it includes direct costs, indirect costs and contingency reserves, and it is the benchmark against which actual performance is measured. Because it is time-phased, the planned spend by any date has a name: Planned Value (PV).

What the budget gives the project

  • A spending plan and funding authorisation
  • The time-phased cost baseline used to track PV
  • Defined reserves for risks and unknowns
  • One set of financial expectations communicated to stakeholders

What it does not give you

  • Any evidence that the work is actually progressing — that needs EV
  • Permission to spend the management reserve
  • A fixed number: authorised changes revise it under change control

Worked answer — reading a public cost position. The lecture's NSW Digital Driver Licence slide reports: October 2019, $15.2m spent from a $16.3m budget, remaining $1.1m, budget used about 93%. What can and cannot be concluded?

1

Verify the percentage. $15.2m ÷ $16.3m = 0.9325, so “about 93%” of the approved budget has been consumed. Remaining = $16.3m − $15.2m = $1.1m. The arithmetic in the slide is right.

2

State what that number is. 93% is a spend figure: it comes from recording actual costs and comparing them with the baseline. It says nothing about how much of the scope is complete.

3

Name the missing measurement. Earned Value. If EV is $15.2m the project is perfectly on budget and on plan; if EV is $12m, the project has bought only $12m of value for $15.2m of spend and the remaining $1.1m will not finish the job.

4

Ask the two follow-up questions a sponsor should ask. How much work remains (estimate to complete), and which reserves are still intact — contingency, management reserve, or neither? Spending 93% of a baseline with 40% of the scope left is a different project from spending it with 90% left.

5

Convert the finding into an action. Compare spend with completed work, assess capacity and support impacts, then approve corrective action only if the baseline genuinely needs to change — the lecture's five-step control loop, which is exactly what the section on controlling costs describes.

Controlling Costs and Earned Value Management

Controlling costs monitors the status of the project to update project costs and to manage changes to the cost baseline. Its purpose is to keep spending inside the approved budget, detect variances early, and take corrective action before an overrun escalates.

Earned Value Management (EVM) is a methodology that integrates scope, schedule and cost to objectively measure project performance and progress. It answers three questions: where are we now in terms of performance to date, where are we going (forecast), and will we finish on budget and schedule?

Benefits of EVM

  • Early warning signs of overruns
  • Objective, data-driven reporting instead of opinion
  • Scope, cost and schedule in one holistic view

The five-step control loop

  1. Record actual costs
  2. Compare with the baseline
  3. Analyse variance and forecast
  4. Approve corrective action
  5. Update and communicate
MeasureLecture definitionQuestion it answers
Planned Value (PV)The budgeted cost of work planned to be done by a certain date; the total cost estimate planned to be spent on an activity during a given periodWhat should we have bought by now?
Earned Value (EV)The budgeted cost of work actually completed by a certain date; the estimate of the value of the physical work actually completedWhat have we actually bought?
Actual Cost (AC)The real expenditure for the work performed; the total direct and indirect costs incurred in accomplishing work during a given periodWhat did it cost us?
Read the three curves, not the bank balance. PV is the plan, EV is progress, AC is the invoice. Any two of them give you a variance; all three give you a forecast. A project can be perfectly on schedule (EV = PV) and still be an emergency (AC far above EV) — which is exactly the situation the lecture's thought nugget describes.
Exam framing: EVM only works if the scope baseline is stable enough to measure “percent complete” against it. That is why Week 3's WBS and schedule baseline come first, and why agile projects struggle to apply EVM unchanged.
At the end of month 3 a project reports EV of $80,000, PV of $85,000 and AC of $85,000. Which statement is correct?
The project is exactly on budget because EV and AC are different measures
The project is behind schedule and over budget, because EV is below both PV and AC
The project is ahead of schedule because AC equals PV

Variance Analysis and Performance Indexes

Variances are subtracted; indexes are divided. Variances tell you the size of the problem in dollars, indexes tell you the efficiency of the project as a ratio you can compare across projects.

Cost Variance (CV)

The amount of budget deficit or surplus at a given point in time — the difference between Earned Value and Actual Cost.

CV = EV − AC

  • Positive → under budget
  • Negative → over budget

Schedule Variance (SV)

A measure of schedule performance — the difference between Earned Value and Planned Value.

SV = EV − PV

  • Positive → ahead of schedule
  • Negative → behind schedule

Cost Performance Index (CPI)

A measure of the cost efficiency of budgeted resources, expressed as the ratio of Earned Value to Actual Cost.

CPI = EV ÷ AC

  • CPI > 1 → cost efficient
  • CPI < 1 → cost overrun

Schedule Performance Index (SPI)

A measure of schedule efficiency, expressed as the ratio of Earned Value to Planned Value.

SPI = EV ÷ PV

  • SPI > 1 → ahead of schedule
  • SPI < 1 → behind schedule

Worked example from the lecture: student management system, end of Month 4

Worked example — the lecture's Thought Nugget. A software company approved a $300,000 budget for an appointment-management platform. At the halfway point: 50% of the work is complete as scheduled; the cost baseline says the completed work was expected to cost $150,000; the project has actually spent $260,000; the remaining activities are currently estimated to require another $120,000. The project manager reports: “The project is progressing well because we are still on schedule.”

1

Extract the three EVM values. BAC = $300,000. Because half the work is complete and that matches the plan, PV = 50% × $300,000 = $150,000 and EV = $150,000. AC = $260,000.

2

Variances. SV = EV − PV = $150,000 − $150,000 = $0, so the schedule claim is true. CV = EV − AC = $150,000 − $260,000 = −$110,000: the project is $110,000 over budget on work that is only half done.

3

Indexes. SPI = EV ÷ PV = 1.00, exactly on schedule. CPI = EV ÷ AC = $150,000 ÷ $260,000 = 0.58: each dollar spent has bought about 58 cents of value.

4

Forecast two ways. If the current cost performance continues, EAC = BAC ÷ CPI = $300,000 ÷ 0.58 = $520,000 — a 73% overrun. If instead you trust the team's bottom-up estimate for the remaining work, EAC = AC + ETC = $260,000 + $120,000 = $380,000, still 27% over the approved budget.

5

Answer the question that was asked. The project is not healthy. It is on schedule and out of control on cost, and the reason is knowable: the first half of the work cost far more than the baseline allowed — typically underestimated integration, testing or rework. The PM's report is true about one dimension and misleading about the project.

6

Recommend corrective action. Reforecast publicly, agree the revised estimate with the sponsor, look for scope to trade rather than only asking for more money, and tighten the change control that allowed the overrun to accumulate unchecked.

Forecasting: BAC, EAC and ETC

Variances describe the past. Forecasting answers the question stakeholders actually care about: given how we are performing now, what will this project cost in the end?

Budget at Completion (BAC)

The total planned budget for the entire project — the sum of all planned costs from the cost baseline. Think of it as “how much we originally expected to spend when the project finishes”.

Estimate at Completion (EAC)

The expected total cost of the project based on current performance trends. It can be recalculated during the project whenever variances occur — and it should be, because an EAC that is only revised at the end is a post-mortem, not a forecast.

If you assume…UseWhat it means
Current cost performance will continueEAC = BAC ÷ CPICPI = 1 → EAC equals the original budget; CPI < 1 → EAC higher; CPI > 1 → EAC lower
The earlier cost variance was unusual and the remaining work follows the original planEAC = AC + (BAC − EV)The remaining work is worth exactly what the baseline said it would cost
Both cost and schedule performance will affect the remaining workEAC = AC + [(BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI)]The remaining work is priced at today's combined efficiency
The exam skill is choosing the formula, not remembering it. The lecture's instruction is explicit: select the EAC formula based on what you expect to happen during the remainder of the project — then say why you chose it. A formula with no stated assumption earns half the marks.

EAC worked example from the lecture

Previous scenario: PV = $150,000, EV = $130,000, AC = $120,000, and now BAC = $520,000. CPI = EV ÷ AC = $130,000 ÷ $120,000 = 1.0833. Assume the current cost performance continues.

StepWorkingResult
EAC$520,000 ÷ 1.0833$480,000
Variance at completion (BAC − EAC)$520,000 − $480,000$40,000 below budget
InterpretationThe project is forecast to cost $480,000 at completion, $40,000 below the original budget, on the assumption that the current level of cost efficiency continues.
ETC and ETC-based forecasting. The estimate to complete is the money still needed: with the CPI assumption, ETC = EAC − AC = $480,000 − $120,000 = $360,000. When a team gives you a fresh bottom-up estimate for the remaining work, EAC = AC + that estimate — which is why the Thought Nugget earlier could be forecast as $380,000 while the CPI method said $520,000.

Traditional vs Agile Cost Management

Traditional delivery fixes the budget early and measures against it. Agile delivery fixes time and cost per iteration and lets scope move. Both need cost discipline — they just apply it at different points.

AspectTraditional (waterfall / plan-driven)Agile (iterative / adaptive)
BudgetingFixed upfront, detailed cost baselineIncremental, funded per sprint or release
EstimationComprehensive planning, WBS-basedRolling-wave, evolving estimates
Scope stabilityScope fixed early, baseline stableScope flexible, backlog reprioritised
EVM applicationWorks well: PV, EV and AC tracked reliablyDifficult: scope and metrics change frequently
Measurement of valueMonetary units — dollars, hours, costStory points, features, business value
Stakeholder reportingFinancial variance reports: CV, SV, CPI, SPIVelocity charts, burn-up and burn-down, business value
Risk handlingReserves set upfront: contingency and managementFlexibility built into scope and incremental funding

Agile budgeting in practice: the mobile banking app

Team

1 product owner, 1 scrum master, 6 developers. Average team cost: $60,000 per two-week sprint.

Funding decision

Management approves 10 sprints upfront = $600,000. Time and cost are fixed; scope is flexible.

Halfway review

End of Sprint 5: login, payments and notifications delivered. Remaining budget $300,000.

Decision

Add biometric login by adjusting priorities within the same budget — the team trades scope, not cost.

Why EVM struggles in agile projects

  • Changing scope makes Planned Value unstable
  • Story points and features are not directly measurable in dollars
  • Frequent reprioritisation reduces the usefulness of variance analysis
  • Sponsors expect cost forecasts in monetary terms, not velocity metrics

Adaptations that work

  • Track Earned Business Value (EBV) as well as cost efficiency
  • Map story points to cost using sprint burn rates
  • Recalculate EAC at the end of each iteration with rolling forecasts
Best practices for agile cost management: fund increments (sprints or releases) rather than the whole project upfront; use burn-up or burn-down charts and velocity as proxies for value delivery; combine agile tracking with lightweight EVM reporting for executives; keep contingency reserves even in agile projects; and communicate clearly that agile fixes time and cost while scope remains flexible.
Why is Planned Value unstable in an agile project?
Because velocity changes from sprint to sprint
Because the backlog is reprioritised and scope changes, so the baseline PV is calculated from keeps moving
Because story points are not measured in dollars

When Cost Management Fails: Challenges and Case Evidence

Week 4's case study is a project that mostly worked — and four projects that did not. The failures are more instructive, because each one maps onto a mistake you can now name.

Challenges in IT cost management

  • Scope creep and requirement volatility — new features, security upgrades and compliance changes add cost that was never in the budget
  • Hidden and recurring costs — licences, cloud subscriptions, SaaS fees and vendor support, with consumption-based cloud costs unpredictable unless monitored
  • Measurement challenges — tracking actual progress against costs spent is genuinely difficult
  • Vendor and outsourcing risks — delays, hidden costs in SLAs, currency fluctuations for offshore teams

Best practices

  • Start with a strong cost management plan: how costs will be planned, estimated, budgeted and controlled
  • Use a detailed, realistic WBS and assign costs at activity or work-package level
  • Build in contingency and management reserves, and benchmark against industry averages
  • Monitor costs continuously, not just at milestones: track PV against EV and AC and spot variances early

Week 4 Recap

Four processes, four outputs

Plan cost management → estimating costs → determining the budget → controlling costs. The outputs are the plan, the estimates, the baseline and the change decisions.

Process spine

Two reserves, one boundary

Contingency covers partially planned risk and sits inside the baseline. Management reserve covers unpredictable risk and sits outside it but inside the total budget.

Reserves

PV, EV, AC

Plan, progress, invoice. Everything else in EVM is arithmetic on these three numbers: CV = EV − AC, SV = EV − PV, CPI = EV ÷ AC, SPI = EV ÷ PV.

EVM core

Forecast with a stated assumption

EAC = BAC ÷ CPI, or AC + (BAC − EV), or AC + [(BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI)]. Choose by what you expect of the remaining work, then say so.

Forecast

Check yourself

Can you define the cost baseline, compute CV, SV, CPI, SPI and EAC from PV, EV and AC, and explain why agile projects find PV unstable?
