What Quality Means in an IT Project

Week 5 opens with a definition and then immediately complicates it: quality is not one property you can inspect at the end, it is a judgement made against requirements, expectations and purpose — and different stakeholders are judging different things.

The definition the deck quotes

PMBOK: quality is “the degree to which a set of inherent characteristics fulfill requirements”. The deck's plain-language version: quality is the degree to which a product or service meets requirements, expectations, and is fit for its intended purpose.

PerspectiveThe question it asksThe deck's exampleWhat it can miss
Conformance to requirementsDid the project deliver what was documented in the scope and specifications?A payroll system must calculate salaries and taxes correctlyA system can conform exactly and still be unusable
Fitness for useDoes the system actually serve the user effectively?A payroll system that “works” but is slow or hard to use lacks qualitySays nothing about whether the documented requirements were met
Customer satisfactionHow does the end-user or client experience the result?Even bug-free software is “poor quality” if it fails real business needsLate and subjective; it arrives after the money is spent

Six dimensions of quality in IT projects

Functionality & reliability

  • Does it do what it is supposed to?
  • Does it work consistently without failure?

Performance & usability

  • Is it fast and efficient?
  • Is it intuitive for end users?

Maintainability & security

  • Can it be updated and improved easily?
  • Are data and systems protected?
Both halves, always. Project quality management ensures that a project's deliverables meet stakeholder expectations, requirements and agreed standards, while also ensuring the project's processes are efficient and effective. Product quality and process quality are the two halves of the definition, and the exam question that separates assurance from control is really asking which half you are working on.

Four principles the whole week rests on

Customer focus

End-user satisfaction is the ultimate quality measure. Everything else — test coverage, defect counts, standards compliance — is a proxy for it.

Prevention over inspection

It is cheaper to prevent defects than to fix them later. This principle becomes arithmetic in the cost of quality.

Continuous improvement

Lessons learned and feedback loops: Agile retrospectives, Kaizen, DevOps. The process is expected to get better, not just to be followed.

Fact-based decision making

Metrics, dashboards and data analysis rather than opinion. Every claim about quality should be traceable to a measurement.

A system passes 100% of its functional test cases and has zero open defects. Users still describe it as poor quality. Which perspective explains their judgement?
Conformance to requirements
Fitness for use, and customer satisfaction
Cost of quality

Process 1 — Planning Quality Management

Planning quality is where the week's leverage sits. Every later problem in the deck's workshop case traces back to something that was not defined here.

What the process produces

Planning quality management is the process of identifying the quality requirements and standards for the project and its deliverables, how compliance will be demonstrated and measured, and the activities, resources and metrics needed to ensure quality is built in from the start.

Four objectives

  • Define what “quality” means for this project
  • Identify the standards, policies and metrics to use
  • Determine the processes, tools and techniques to achieve it
  • Clarify roles and responsibilities: who checks what, when and how

Five key activities

  • Identifying quality standards
  • Defining quality metrics
  • Planning quality assurance activities
  • Planning quality control activities
  • Documenting all of it in the Quality Management Plan
The failure this process prevents. If no measurable acceptance criterion is written for a non-functional requirement — concurrency, response time under load, accessibility — then no amount of testing later will catch it, because there is nothing for a test to pass or fail. The workshop case at the end of this page is exactly that failure.

Worked example — writing one clause of a Quality Management Plan. Requirement: “students must be able to enrol online.” Turn it into plan content.

1

Define quality for this project. Enrolment counts as quality only if it is correct (the right student ends up in the right unit), timely (the transaction completes while the student waits), and verifiable (the student gets evidence their submission was accepted). Those three words are the project's definition, and they are narrower than “quality” in general.

2

Name the standards. Privacy legislation for student data, the university's accessibility standard for the interface, and the security standard applied to authentication. Each one is external to the team and therefore not negotiable.

3

Define the metrics and thresholds. 95th-percentile response time ≤ 2 s at 5,000 concurrent users on the production-sized data set; 0 enrolment records lost or duplicated in a load test; 100% of critical functional test cases passing; 0 open high or critical security findings.

4

Plan assurance and control separately. Assurance: a design review of the enrolment state machine, a peer review of the concurrency code, an audit that accessibility checks are running in the pipeline. Control: load testing, end-to-end enrolment regression, a UAT sample of real students, and a release gate that refuses to open while any critical defect is open.

5

Assign owners. Product owner for the acceptance criteria, QA lead for the assurance activities, test lead for the control activities, and the project manager for the plan and its review cadence. If a clause has no name next to it, it will not happen.

Which item belongs in the Quality Management Plan rather than in the testing phase?
The threshold that makes a performance requirement pass or fail
The list of defects found in the last test cycle
The decision to retest a module after a fix

Process 2 — Managing Quality (Assurance)

Managing quality translates the plan into executable quality activity. It is process-focused and preventive: the question is not “is this deliverable correct?” but “is the way we are working capable of producing correct deliverables?”

Definition

Managing quality is the process of translating the Quality Management Plan into executable quality activities. It ensures the project processes are being followed, focuses on preventing defects rather than detecting them, and in IT projects means embedding quality into development, testing and delivery workflows.

ActivityWhat it isDeck examplesWhat it changes
Process auditsA systematic review of project processes to check whether teams are following the defined quality standards, policies and proceduresAuditing whether developers use secure coding practices (OWASP); checking that Agile teams write acceptance criteria on user stories; verifying CI/CD pipelines log test resultsThe process, before it produces a defective deliverable
Quality assurance reviewsStructured evaluations of deliverables and processes, run throughout the project: peer reviews, design walkthroughs, retrospectivesPeer review of code for maintainability; an architecture review of a microservices design for scalability; a sprint retrospectiveShared understanding, and early detection of issues
Continuous process improvementOngoing refinement of processes for efficiency, effectiveness and adaptability, guided by PDCA or Six Sigma DMAICImproving build automation to cut manual deployment errors; using bug turnaround time to optimise QA workflow; moving from manual to automated regressionTechnical debt, bottlenecks and the learning culture
Risk and defect preventionIdentifying process weaknesses that could create defects and addressing them before they occurStatic analysis (SonarQube) to catch vulnerabilities before release; threat modelling sessions; reviewing third-party API dependenciesThe probability that a defect exists at all
Audits look at the process, not the product. An audit that concludes “the code is fine” has answered the wrong question. The audit question is whether the defined practice is actually being followed — for example, whether every user story really carries acceptance criteria, or whether the pipeline really does log test results. The deliverable is inspected by control; the process is inspected by assurance.

Agile-based quality management

  • Shared responsibility for quality across the team
  • Customer-centric quality, judged each increment
  • Continuous testing inside the iteration
  • Frequent feedback rather than a release-gate surprise
  • An adaptive plan that changes as the product does

Focus: delivering quality incrementally.

DevOps-based quality management

  • Automation everywhere it removes human error
  • Continuous integration and continuous delivery
  • Monitoring and observability in production
  • Infrastructure as Code, so environments are reproducible

Focus: continuous quality from end to end.

An assurance audit finds that three of the last ten deployments skipped the automated regression suite. What has the audit actually found?
Three defective releases
A process non-conformity: the defined practice was not followed
A planned continuous improvement

Process 3 — Controlling Quality

Controlling quality is the reactive half. It works on outputs — products, services, results — and its output is a decision about whether a deliverable may be accepted.

Definition and stance

Controlling quality (QC) is the process of monitoring and verifying that deliverables meet the defined quality standards and requirements. It is more reactive: it detects defects in deliverables. It focuses on outputs, not processes, and its purpose is to ensure that only approved deliverables are accepted.

Verification and validation are not a sequence of synonyms. Verification is “did we build it right?” — conformance to the specification. Validation is “did we build the right thing?” — fitness for the user's need. A deliverable can pass every verification test and still fail validation, and that gap is where most of the cost of external failure lives.

Tools and Techniques in the Quality Management Plan

Seven classic tools, one cost technique. The deck presents each with its purpose; exams usually ask you to choose the right one for a described situation, so learn the purpose rather than the picture.

The 80/20 rule, as the deck states it. Roughly 80% of problems are caused by 20% of causes — so improvement effort should be aimed at the problems causing the majority of defects. The Pareto chart is the tool that makes that claim visible; the discipline is to fix the tallest bar rather than the most recently reported defect.

How to read the control chart

Performance is plotted over time with a centre line and upper and lower control limits, and it detects variation or trends outside acceptable thresholds. A point outside the limits is a signal, not a coincidence; a run of points on one side of the centre line is a trend even when every individual point is inside the limits.

What the tools are for, collectively

The seven tools answer three questions in order. Where is the problem? Pareto, histogram, checksheet. Why is it happening? Cause and effect, scatter, flowchart. Is the process stable now? Control chart. Cost-benefit analysis sits above all of them and decides whether the fix is worth funding.

Worked example — choosing tools for a described symptom. Symptom: the build pipeline fails intermittently, and nobody can say whether a failure means a real defect or a flaky environment.

1

Start with the checksheet. Before theorising, collect data: which job failed, which test, which agent, which environment, at what time. A structured tally costs nothing and it is the input every other tool needs.

2

Use a Pareto chart on the tally. If three test classes account for most failures, the vital few is identified and the investigation has a target. Chasing all forty flaky tests at once is how a quarter disappears.

3

Draw the flowchart of the pipeline. Map build → test → deploy including the waits, the caches and the retries. Intermittent failures usually live in a step nobody drew: a shared cache, a rate-limited external API, a test that assumes ordering.

4

Run a cause-and-effect session. Branches: machine (agent capacity, environment drift), method (test isolation, retry policy), measurement (which metric declared failure), people (who changed what, when). Record candidate causes, then test the cheap ones first.

5

Confirm with a scatter diagram, then monitor with a control chart. Plot failure rate against a suspected driver such as concurrent jobs per agent. If the correlation holds, fix it and keep a control chart on the failure rate afterwards — the chart is the evidence that the process has actually stabilised, rather than merely improved for a week.

A team wants to know whether a recent process change has made defect detection stable, or whether the apparent improvement is normal variation. Which tool answers that question?
Histogram
Pareto chart
Control chart

Standards, Frameworks and Maturity

Standards are what a project adopts when it wants its definition of quality to be external, auditable and comparable. The deck names four families and the level structure of one of them.

StandardWhat it coversWhy an IT project adopts it
ISO 9001Quality management systems: a general-purpose standard for implementing a QMS in any industry, including ITFocuses the organisation on continuous improvement, customer satisfaction and documented processes
ISO/IEC 27001Information security management, treating security as a core part of IT qualityEnsures confidentiality, integrity and availability of data; often required for financial services, healthcare and government IT projects
CMMIA framework for assessing and improving software development processes, in five maturity levelsGives an organisation a staged path from ad hoc work to optimising processes, and a comparable maturity rating
ITILIT service management: quality defined as ensuring services align with business needs and provide valueEmphasis on service level agreements and continuous service improvement across the operational life of the service
ITIL's definition is worth remembering verbatim. Quality is “ensuring services are aligned with business needs and provide value”, with emphasis on service level agreements and continuous service improvement. It is the operational counterpart of ISO 9001's project-and-organisation view: one is about building the thing well, the other about running it well.
Standards are not quality. Certifying against ISO 9001 or reaching a CMMI level describes the capability of the process. The deliverable still has to satisfy its acceptance criteria, and a certified organisation can still ship a defect. Say “the standard gives us a defined process and an audit trail; the acceptance criteria tell us whether this release is fit to ship” — that sentence distinguishes the two.
A hospital IT project must demonstrate that patient data is protected and that access is controlled. Which standard is the natural fit?
ISO 9001

Cost of Quality and the Trade-offs

The deck defines cost of quality, splits it in two, and then lists the trade-offs that quality competes with. This is the section that converts the week's principles into numbers a project manager can defend.

Definition

Cost of quality (CoQ) is the total cost incurred to ensure a product or service meets quality requirements — the cost of preventing, detecting and correcting defects. It divides into cost of conformance (prevention + appraisal) and cost of non-conformance (internal + external failure).

AspectCost of conformanceCost of non-conformance
DefinitionThe cost of ensuring that a product or service meets quality requirementsThe cost incurred when the product fails to meet quality standards
FocusPrevent defects proactivelyReactively fix defects
ComponentsPrevention + appraisalInternal failure + external failure
IT exampleAutomated tests, code reviews, trainingBug fixes, downtime, customer complaints
GoalMinimise future costs and improve reliabilityMinimise the impact of failures
Trade-offWhat the deck says happensThe quality decision it forces
Cost vs qualityHigher quality usually requires more investment in prevention, testing and training; cutting costs increases defects and reworkFund prevention deliberately, and treat the failure costs as the bill for not doing so
Time vs qualityTight schedules force reduced testing or shortcuts; extending the timeline allows thorough QA but raises costDecide explicitly which tests are non-negotiable, before the schedule pressure arrives
Scope vs qualityAdding more features dilutes focus on the quality of existing functionalityTriage scope rather than testing when time is short — the smaller product can still be a good one
Prevention vs qualityInvesting in prevention through training and standards reduces defects laterTreat prevention spend as an investment with a return, not as overhead

The mix matters more than the total. The same $200k of quality spend can be 80% conformance and 20% failure, or the reverse, and the second project is one bad release away from losing the client. Two rules of thumb from the deck's principle set:

  • Prevention is the cheapest category per defect. A defect stopped by a code review never reaches a test suite, a rework cycle or a customer.
  • External failure is the only category that damages the client relationship. Internal failure costs money; external failure costs money and trust, and the trust is harder to re-buy.

So the management question is not “is our cost of quality too high?” but “is too much of it being spent after the defect already exists?”

A project manager reports that cost of quality has fallen by 25%. What is the first question a reviewer should ask?
How much was spent on the QA team?
Which categories fell — prevention and appraisal, or internal and external failure?
Which quality standard has the project adopted?

Evolving Quality Practices

The deck closes the tools section by arguing that checklists, inspections and late-stage testing are no longer sufficient on their own, and that modern practice emphasises speed, automation and continuous improvement. Three movements do the work.

Lean

Eliminate waste: redundant processes, delays in defect resolution, excessive documentation. In IT, most waste is waiting — for a review, for an environment, for a decision — and Lean attacks the wait rather than the work.

Six Sigma

Reduce variation and defects with data: DMAIC — Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control. It is the statistical half, and it is what turns “the build is flaky” into a measured defect rate with a control limit.

PracticeWhat it changesDeck examples
Lean Six Sigma in ITStreamlines development by reducing handoffs; applies root-cause analysis and statistical methods to improve reliabilityFewer handoffs between teams; defect root-cause analysis on recurring incidents
AI in qualityShifts assurance from reactive defect detection to proactive defect prevention by predicting where defects will occurAI-driven code analysis; predictive analytics for defect probability; bots for exploratory and regression testing
Automation and DevOpsMoves testing into the pipeline and makes quality gates automatic rather than discretionaryUnit, integration, regression and performance tests in CI/CD; builds rejected automatically when defects exceed thresholds; Infrastructure as Code preventing environment defects
Automation's real contribution is removing discretion. A quality gate that a human can waive under deadline pressure will be waived. A gate that fails the build when the defect count exceeds a threshold cannot be argued with at 5pm on a Friday — and that is precisely the deck's claim that continuous quality gates reduce human error and give consistent quality across environments.
Which change is the clearest example of the shift from reactive detection to proactive prevention?
Adding a fourth round of manual regression testing before release
Running static analysis in the pipeline so insecure patterns are flagged before merge
Reducing the time to log a production incident

Deming's 14 points and Juran's 10 steps — what they are for

Deming: the philosophy

Fourteen points that make a quality plan process-focused, preventive and continuous. The first four carry most of the weight for this unit: create constancy of purpose; adopt quality as everyone's responsibility; cease dependence on inspection and build quality into the process; and stop awarding business on price alone, in favour of long-term supplier relationships.

The remaining points are about people and system: improve constantly, train on the job, institute leadership, drive out fear so problems are reported, break down barriers between departments, drop slogans and numerical targets, remove barriers to pride of workmanship, and put everyone to work on the transformation.

Juran: the management sequence

Ten steps that make quality improvement actionable and management-led: build awareness of the need; set measurable goals; organise to reach them; provide resources; train personnel; take action; report progress with metrics; give recognition; communicate results; and maintain momentum by integrating the improvement into standard process.

Where Deming supplies the philosophy, Juran supplies the sequence a project manager can actually schedule — which is why the HRM case study asks for prevention measures expressed in Juran's steps.

How to use both in an answer. Deming answers “what should the organisation believe about quality?”; Juran answers “what do we do on Monday?”. An answer that cites Deming's third point to justify moving spend from appraisal to prevention, then uses Juran's first five steps to sequence the change, is both principled and actionable.

Tutorial, Workshop and Case Study

Three exercises from the Week 5 material, worked to the standard the questions imply. The Foundations page carries the definitions and the arithmetic behind them.

Q1 Discussion

The online enrolment system. A university built a new enrolment system and followed its approved quality plan: all functional test cases passed, code reviews and security checks were run, no critical defects remained, the response-time target was met in testing, and the client approved the launch. When enrolment opened, thousands of students used the system simultaneously; it became extremely slow, some users saw error messages, and several students could not tell whether their enrolment had been submitted.

Questions: if the system passed every planned test, was its quality managed successfully? Where did the main problem occur — planning, managing or controlling quality? What criteria, testing activities or stakeholder input might have prevented it?

Worked answer — the enrolment system.

1

Was quality managed successfully? No. Not because the tests were wrong, but because the quality plan certified a system against criteria that did not describe the conditions of use. Passing every planned test is evidence about the plan, not about the product. The deck's customer-satisfaction perspective settles it: quality is ultimately judged by the end-user experience, and thousands of users experienced failure.

2

Where did the problem occur? Mainly in planning quality, with a real failure in controlling quality as the consequence. Planning never defined a measurable acceptance criterion for concurrency or peak load — the response-time target was met "during testing", on a test rig that did not reproduce enrolment day. Control then did what control can do: it compared actual performance with the thresholds that existed, and those thresholds did not include the case that mattered.

3

What was missed in assurance? A process audit would have asked whether non-functional requirements were being reviewed at all; a peer review of the load profile would have questioned how many concurrent users the test represented. Assurance is where a missing criterion is usually catchable while it is still cheap to fix.

4

The criteria that should have existed. 95th-percentile response time ≤ 2 s at 5,000 concurrent users on the production-sized data set; 0 enrolment records lost or duplicated under load; an explicit confirmation state so a student always knows whether the submission succeeded; accessibility and privacy criteria measured rather than asserted.

5

The testing that should have existed. Performance and load testing at enrolment-day scale, not a smoke test; a soak test to expose leaks that only appear over hours; end-to-end system testing on production-sized data; and UAT with real students on real devices, which is the only activity likely to surface the ambiguity about whether a submission was accepted.

6

Stakeholder input that was missing. The students themselves, and the enrolment support staff who take the calls when something goes wrong. The deck's customer-focus principle is not decoration: the people who bear the cost of a quality failure should be in the room when the acceptance criteria are written.

Q2 Case study

TechSolutions HRM system. A cloud HRM system with Employee Management, Payroll, Attendance and Performance Tracking modules, under a tight six-month deadline. At the first major release at the end of month 4 the client reports three defects: payroll miscalculates overtime for certain employees; attendance occasionally fails to sync with biometric devices; the performance module crashes when generating reports for more than 500 employees. The QA team ran unit and integration testing, but system and user acceptance testing were limited by time. The project manager is concerned about high defect costs, delayed delivery and client dissatisfaction.

Questions: which test types were missed and how would they have helped? Use cause-and-effect analysis on the payroll defect. Categorise the defects as cost of conformance or non-conformance. Propose prevention using Juran's ten steps, and say how Deming's points apply.

Worked answer — the HRM case. The arithmetic and the classification in full are on the Foundations page; this is the case reasoning.

1

Missed tests. System testing would have caught the report crash, because the failure needs the whole integrated system plus a realistic data volume. User acceptance testing would have caught the overtime miscalculation, because overtime rules are a business rule that only payroll staff can confirm. Performance and integration testing against real devices would have caught the biometric sync failure, which appears under concurrent device traffic rather than in a scripted integration test.

2

Cause and effect on the payroll defect. Method: overtime rules were never documented or approved, so the developer inferred them. People: no payroll domain expert in the review. Machine: no automated regression suite covering payroll calculations. Measurement: no acceptance criterion for overtime, so nothing could fail. The measurement branch is the root cause, and it is a planning failure rather than a coding one — which is why the fix is a criterion, not a patch.

3

Classification. All three client-reported defects are external failure: the client found them after release, so the cost includes rework, retesting, reputational damage and possible delay. The unit and integration testing that did run is appraisal, and it remains conformance spending even though it missed these defects. Before release, any defect those tests did catch would have been internal failure. The cost of conformance here is the testing and review budget; the cost of non-conformance is the whole correction effort after month 4.

4

Prevention via Juran's steps. Build awareness (quantify the defect cost and show it to the sponsor); set goals (zero critical defects at release, with written exit criteria); organise (a named quality owner per module); provide resources (an automated payroll regression suite and a performance test environment); train (payroll rules, secure coding, test design); take action (document and approve the overtime rules); report progress (a defect trend dashboard, reviewed weekly); recognise (credit the testers who find defects early); communicate results (share the root-cause analysis); maintain momentum (fold the regression suite into the standard pipeline so the improvement survives staff changes).

5

Deming's points applied. Point 3, cease dependence on inspection: build overtime validation and load behaviour into the design rather than hoping testing catches them. Point 8, drive out fear: the team limited system and UAT testing and nobody escalated it, which is a reporting problem as much as a scheduling one. Point 9, break down barriers: the payroll business experts and the development team were working in different silos. Point 5, improve constantly: the root-cause findings should change the process for the next release, not just fix this one.

6

Top management commitment. Every prevention step above costs money and time before it produces a visible benefit, so it survives only with explicit sponsorship: the schedule must protect system and UAT testing, the budget must fund the regression suite, and the sponsor must accept the release gate when it says no. Without that, the project manager's plan is a document rather than a constraint.

In the HRM case, the QA team did run unit and integration testing. Why does that not make the project's quality management a success?
Unit and integration testing are not real quality control activities
The defects lived at the system, business-rule and load levels, which the tests that ran could not reach
Because no process audit was run during the project

Week 5 Recap

Quality is conformance, fitness and satisfaction

The degree to which inherent characteristics fulfil requirements — judged against the specification, against the user's task, and by the client's experience.

Core definition

Plan, manage, control

Planning defines requirements, standards and metrics; managing quality improves the process; controlling quality decides whether a deliverable is acceptable.

Three processes

Assurance is process, control is product

Audits, reviews and retrospectives change how work is done. Testing, inspection and acceptance decisions judge what the work produced.

The distinction to memorise

Conformance versus non-conformance

Prevention and appraisal are what you choose to spend; internal and external failure are what you spend when you did not.
