INFO6007 — Week 5 supplement

Foundations: the definitions and the arithmetic behind quality

Week 5 is mostly conceptual, but three parts of it are quantitative and are where marks are usually lost: the cost of quality, the translation of a quality requirement into a measurable acceptance criterion, and the earned-value arithmetic that shows what a defect is doing to the project. This page pins the definitions down exactly, then works the numbers.

Definitions verified against the Week 5 deck EVM arithmetic follows the Week 5 tutorial answers Critical-path arithmetic links back to Week 3

Definitions that must be exact

The Week 5 deck opens by quoting PMBOK. Learn the quoted form, then the deck's own gloss, then the consequence — that is the shape of a full-mark answer.

Quality (PMBOK, as quoted in the deck)

“The degree to which a set of inherent characteristics fulfill requirements.” The deck's plain-language version: quality is the degree to which a product or service meets requirements, expectations, and is fit for its intended purpose.

Project Quality Management

The set of processes that ensure a project's deliverables meet stakeholder expectations, requirements and agreed standards, while also ensuring the project's processes are efficient and effective. Both halves are examinable: product quality and process quality.

The three processes

Planning quality management — identify the quality requirements and standards, and how compliance will be demonstrated and measured. Managing quality (quality assurance) — translate the plan into executable assurance activity; process-focused and preventive. Controlling quality (quality control) — monitor and verify that deliverables meet the defined standards; output-focused and reactive.

Verification vs validation

Verification asks “did we build it right?” — the deliverable meets its specification. Validation asks “did we build the right thing?” — the deliverable meets the user's need. The deck's NSW driver licence example states both explicitly: verification checks technical specifications, validation confirms the licence works for drivers, police and service staff.

Cost of quality

The total cost incurred to ensure a product or service meets quality requirements — in the deck's words, “the cost of preventing, detecting, and correcting defects”. It splits into cost of conformance (prevention + appraisal) and cost of non-conformance (internal + external failure).

Cost of quality: the arithmetic

The deck asserts that prevention is cheaper than correction. The cost-of-quality breakdown is how you make that assertion quantitative in an exam answer.

The decomposition

Total cost of quality is the sum of four categories, grouped into two halves:

\[ \mathrm{CoQ} \;=\; \underbrace{(C_p + C_a)}_{\text{cost of conformance}} \;+\; \underbrace{(C_{if} + C_{ef})}_{\text{cost of non-conformance}} \]

\(C_p\) prevention — training, standards, code review, threat modelling, test automation.
\(C_a\) appraisal — testing, inspection, audits, quality-control tooling.
\(C_{if}\) internal failure — rework, retesting and defect triage found before release.
\(C_{ef}\) external failure — incidents, hotfixes, downtime, complaints, compensation found after release.

Why the failure terms dominate

Let a defect fixed during prevention cost \(1\) unit, during appraisal cost \(k_a\), during internal failure \(k_{if}\) and after release \(k_{ef}\). The standard escalation is:

\[ 1 \;<\; k_a \;\ll\; k_{if} \;\ll\; k_{ef} \]

The deck's version of this is the principle prevention over inspection: “it's cheaper to prevent defects than fix them later”. Deming's third point makes the same claim in management language — cease dependence on inspection to achieve quality, and build quality into the process instead.

CategoryHalfIT examples from the deckBehaviour over the project
Prevention \(C_p\)ConformanceAutomated tests, code reviews, training, standardsSpent early, on purpose, before defects exist
Appraisal \(C_a\)ConformanceUnit, integration, system, UAT, performance testingSpent throughout; proportional to what you test

External failure \(C_{ef}\)Non-conformanceDowntime, customer complaints, emergency releasesRises when defects escape the release gate
The trap in the definition. CoQ is not “the cost of the QA team”. It is the total of all four categories, and the interesting management question is the mix: a project can spend the same total on quality and still fail because \(C_{ef}\) is doing the work that \(C_p\) should have done.
A release ships with a defect that miscalculates overtime pay. Payroll staff work a weekend to correct 1,200 payslips manually, and the client raises a formal complaint. How are these costs classified?
Appraisal cost, because the payslips had to be checked
Internal failure cost, because the defect was eventually fixed in-house
External failure cost — part of the cost of non-conformance

Worked arithmetic — rebalancing the quality spend. A 6-month IT project budgets its quality activities as \(C_p = \$30{,}000\), \(C_a = \$50{,}000\), \(C_{if} = \$70{,}000\), \(C_{ef} = \$50{,}000\).

1

Total and split.

\[ \mathrm{CoC} = 30{,}000 + 50{,}000 = \$80{,}000 \] \[ \mathrm{CoNC} = 70{,}000 + 50{,}000 = \$120{,}000 \] \[ \mathrm{CoQ} = 80{,}000 + 120{,}000 = \$200{,}000 \]

So 40% of all quality spend is conformance and 60% is failure — a project paying for defects rather than for prevention.

2

Propose a shift, not a cut. Move \(\$20{,}000\) from reactive work into prevention: more review, more automated regression, earlier defect detection. Assume a conservative conversion factor of \(1.75\) — every dollar spent preventing defects removes \(\$1.75\) of failure cost, because failure work is dearer than prevention work.

\[ C_p' = 30{,}000 + 20{,}000 = \$50{,}000 \] \[ \mathrm{CoNC}' = 120{,}000 - (20{,}000 \times 1.75) = 120{,}000 - 35{,}000 = \$85{,}000 \]
3

New total. Appraisal is unchanged at \(\$50{,}000\):

\[ \mathrm{CoQ}' = (50{,}000 + 50{,}000) + 85{,}000 = \$185{,}000 \]

A \(\$15{,}000\) reduction in total cost of quality, achieved by spending more on prevention. This is the deck's prevention-over-inspection principle expressed as arithmetic.

4

State the assumption. The result depends entirely on the conversion factor. If prevention only removes \(\$0.80\) of failure cost per dollar spent, \(\mathrm{CoQ}' = 100{,}000 + 104{,}000 = \$204{,}000\) and the shift destroys value. The honest exam answer names the assumption and the break-even point: here, prevention must remove more than \(\$1.00\) of failure cost per dollar to be worth doing on cost grounds alone — and less than that on risk grounds, because external failures also cost reputation and client trust.

Metrics and acceptance criteria

The deck's planning step says to define quality metrics; its control step says to measure against thresholds. Between them sits the step students skip: turning a requirement into something a test can pass or fail.

From requirement to acceptance criterion

A requirement becomes usable for quality control only when it is expressed as a measurable assertion over a stated condition:

\[ \text{criterion}_i \;=\; \bigl(\, m_i \;\bowtie\; T_i \;\mid\; \text{load } L,\ \text{data set } S \,\bigr), \qquad \bowtie \;\in\; \{\le, \ge, =\} \]

The condition clause is the part that carries the marks. “Response time \(\le 2\) s” is not testable; “response time \(\le 2\) s for the 95th percentile at 5,000 concurrent users on the production-sized enrolment data set” is.

Definition of done, written properly

A release gate is a conjunction of criteria, not a feeling. In the notation above, release requires \(\bigwedge_i (m_i \bowtie T_i)\) — every criterion satisfied at once, under the stated conditions. That conjunction is what the deck's driver licence example means by “statewide release proceeds only when agreed criteria are met and critical defects are closed”.

The metric that was missing

The workshop scenario on the study page failed with a full green test report because the load and concurrency clause \(L\) was never written into any criterion. Nothing was tested wrongly; something testable was never defined. That is a planning defect, and it is why the failure surfaced as an external one.

Which of these is a usable acceptance criterion for quality control?
The system should be responsive and easy to use
95th-percentile response time ≤ 2 s at 5,000 concurrent users on the production data set
Performance testing will be carried out before release

Quality work on the critical path

Week 3's arithmetic is what stops quality from being the thing that gets cut when the schedule slips. This is a small schedule for a quality-critical release, in the WBS-and-critical-path style of Week 3.

Forward pass, backward pass, float

With early start \(ES\), early finish \(EF\), late start \(LS\), late finish \(LF\) and duration \(d\):

\[ ES_j = \max_{i \in P(j)} EF_i, \qquad EF_j = ES_j + d_j \] \[ LF_i = \min_{j \in S(i)} LS_j, \qquad LS_i = LF_i - d_i, \qquad F_i = LS_i - ES_i \]

Tasks with \(F = 0\) form the critical path. Here that path is A → B → D → E → F → G, giving a project duration of \(2+6+4+4+3+2 = 21\) days. Task C has \(F = 1\) day.

Why this matters for quality. Only task C carries float. If the security review (D) runs late or the UAT sample finds defects, there is no spare time anywhere on the path to absorb it — the only levers left are scope and quality. That is the schedule-level version of the trade-off the deck describes between time and quality: “tight schedules may force reduced testing or shortcuts, risking quality”.
The security review (D) is expected to take 4 days but takes 6. The project still has a fixed go-live date. What does the critical-path arithmetic tell the project manager?
Task C's float absorbs the slip, so the go-live date is safe
Two days are added to the project end date unless testing or UAT is compressed, and compressing them is a quality decision
Adding testers to task E restores the original date without any trade-off

An earned-value check on a quality problem

Week 4's EVM arithmetic answers “how are we doing?” in money and time. Run it on a project whose problem is quality, and the numbers tell you which of the two it actually is. This is the Week 5 tutorial's Part A, worked in full.

Scenario (Week 5 tutorial, Part A). \(\mathrm{BAC} = \$200{,}000\), project duration 8 months. At the end of month 4: planned completion 50%, so \(\mathrm{PV} = \$100{,}000\); actual completion 40%, so \(\mathrm{EV} = \$80{,}000\); and \(\mathrm{AC} = \$110{,}000\).

The formulas, all five

\[ \mathrm{CV} = \mathrm{EV} - \mathrm{AC}, \qquad \mathrm{SV} = \mathrm{EV} - \mathrm{PV} \] \[ \mathrm{CPI} = \frac{\mathrm{EV}}{\mathrm{AC}}, \qquad \mathrm{SPI} = \frac{\mathrm{EV}}{\mathrm{PV}} \] \[ \mathrm{EAC} = \frac{\mathrm{BAC}}{\mathrm{CPI}}, \qquad \mathrm{VAC} = \mathrm{BAC} - \mathrm{EAC} \]

\(\mathrm{EAC} = \mathrm{BAC}/\mathrm{CPI}\) is the “current CPI persists” forecast the tutorial asks for. It is the right forecast when the cause of the variance is systemic — which, in a quality problem, it usually is.

Worked answer — tutorial Part A.

1

Cost and schedule variance.

\[ \mathrm{CV} = 80{,}000 - 110{,}000 = -\$30{,}000 \quad(\text{over budget}) \] \[ \mathrm{SV} = 80{,}000 - 100{,}000 = -\$20{,}000 \quad(\text{behind schedule}) \]
2

Performance indices.

\[ \mathrm{CPI} = \frac{80{,}000}{110{,}000} = 0.727\ldots \approx 0.73 \quad(\text{cost-inefficient}) \] \[ \mathrm{SPI} = \frac{80{,}000}{100{,}000} = 0.80 \quad(\text{progress slower than plan}) \]
3

Forecast. With the current CPI persisting:

\[ \mathrm{EAC} = \frac{200{,}000}{0.727\ldots} \approx \$275{,}000 \] \[ \mathrm{VAC} = 200{,}000 - 275{,}000 = -\$75{,}000 \]
4

Interpretation, as the tutorial answers it. Behind schedule and over budget; if the trend continues, expect an overrun of about \(\$75\)k. The quality reading of the same numbers: \(\mathrm{SPI} = 0.80\) means testing and review are being squeezed to hold the date, and \(\mathrm{CPI} = 0.73\) means the money is going into rework rather than into planned work. In cost-of-quality language, \(C_{if}\) is inflating, and the risk is that it converts into \(C_{ef}\) after release.

5

The Part B variant, for comparison. With \(\mathrm{BAC} = \$500{,}000\) and a 10% contingency reserve (\(\$50{,}000\)), at month 6 of 12: \(\mathrm{PV} = \$250{,}000\), \(\mathrm{EV} = \$220{,}000\), \(\mathrm{AC} = \$270{,}000\).

\[ \mathrm{CPI} = \frac{220{,}000}{270{,}000} \approx 0.815, \qquad \mathrm{SPI} = \frac{220{,}000}{250{,}000} = 0.88 \]

Over budget and behind schedule again, but the overrun so far is \(\mathrm{CV} = -\$50{,}000\) — the entire contingency reserve. The tutorial's suggested root causes are the quality-relevant ones: underestimated integration and testing, change requests, resource churn, vendor delays. Its corrective actions are equally quality-relevant: scope triage, senior staff onto the critical path, enforced change control.

Read \(\mathrm{SPI}\) first

Behind schedule is the leading indicator of a quality problem, because the first thing a compressed schedule loses is review and test time. Behind schedule and over budget together is the signature of rework.

Then ask what the money bought

The same \(\mathrm{CPI}\) can mean two different things: more effort than planned for the same scope, or the same effort spent twice. The defect log and the cost-of-quality mix distinguish them.

Then compare with the reserve

An overrun inside the contingency reserve is a managed risk being consumed. An overrun past it is a baseline change, and a quality failure that reaches the client is what makes the reserve insufficient.

A project reports \(\mathrm{CPI} = 0.95\) and \(\mathrm{SPI} = 0.72\). Which reading is most defensible?
The project is in serious financial trouble
Cost is roughly on plan but the schedule has slipped, so the live risk is that quality is cut to recover time
The project is ahead of schedule and under budget
The original budget is \(\mathrm{BAC} = \$120{,}000\). At the half-way point \(\mathrm{EV} = \$50{,}000\) and \(\mathrm{AC} = \$62{,}000\). What does the remaining work now have to achieve?
A CPI of about 0.81 for the rest of the project
A CPI of about 1.21 for the rest of the project
A CPI of 1.00, because the remaining work is unchanged

Tutorial work, self-marked

Two tasks from the Week 5 tutorial and workshop, answered to the standard the marking rubric implies: name the mechanism, name the evidence, name the trade-off.

Worked answer — the TechSolutions HRM case, classified. Full context is on the study page; this is the arithmetic and classification the case asks for.

1

Classify each reported defect. The overtime miscalculation, the biometric sync failure and the 500-employee report crash were all found by the client after the Month 4 release. Every one of them is therefore external failure \(C_{ef}\): the dearest category. The unit and integration testing that did run is appraisal \(C_a\) and stays conformance spending regardless of how many defects it missed.

2

Name the missing tests and what each would have caught. System testing — the whole integrated HRM against requirements — would have caught the report crash, because the crash appears only with the full data set. User acceptance testing — real users on real data — would have caught the overtime rule, because the rule is a business rule that only payroll staff can confirm. Performance/load testing would have caught the biometric sync failure under concurrent device traffic. The deck's list — unit, integration, system, UAT, “and many more” — is exactly the taxonomy to reason with here.

3

Cause-and-effect (Ishikawa) for the payroll defect. Four branches, each with a plausible cause from the case. People: no payroll domain expert on the review panel. Method: overtime rules documented nowhere, so the developer inferred them. Machine: no automated regression suite over payroll calculations. Measurement: no acceptance criteria for overtime, so nothing could fail. The last branch is the root cause, and it is a planning failure, not a coding one.

4

Prevention for the next release, in Juran's ten steps. Build awareness (the defect cost is now visible and quantified), set goals (zero critical defects at release, defined exit criteria), organise (a named quality owner per module), provide resources (an automated payroll regression suite), train (payroll rules for the team), take action (rule documentation and approval), report progress (defect trend on a dashboard), recognise, communicate results, and maintain momentum by folding the new regression suite into the standard pipeline. Steps 1–5 address this project; steps 6–10 make the change survive it.

5

The one-sentence answer. The project did not fail at testing; it failed at planning, because no measurable acceptance criterion existed for overtime calculation or for concurrency, and quality control can only detect what a criterion defines.
