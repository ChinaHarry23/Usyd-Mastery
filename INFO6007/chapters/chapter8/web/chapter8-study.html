Procurement Management and Its Three Processes

Every IT project buys something: licences, cloud capacity, hardware, a security assessment, contract developers. Procurement management is the knowledge area that decides what to buy, from whom, on what terms, and then holds the seller to those terms.

The lecture's one-line framing: procurement management is about deciding what to buy, how to buy it, who to buy it from, and how to ensure the contract is successfully completed.

Definition

Project Procurement Management is the process of acquiring goods, services, or results from outside the project team to meet project needs. The moment the work moves outside the team's boundary, a contract — and therefore procurement management — is involved.

Why it matters

  • Most IT projects rely on external parties for software, hardware, cloud services, consultants or support.
  • Effective procurement delivers the right resources on time, within budget and at the expected quality.

What it costs to get wrong

  • Poor procurement leads to delays, cost overruns and project failure.
  • The damage is structural: a badly chosen contract type transfers risk the wrong way and cannot be fixed by managing harder.
ProcessWhat it does (lecture wording)Output you can point at
1. Plan Procurement Management Documents project procurement decisions, specifies the approach and identifies potential sellers. Procurement Management Plan, make-or-buy decisions, SOW, RFP/RFQ
2. Conduct Procurements Obtains seller responses, evaluates them and awards the contract — planning turned into action in the market. Eligible bids, evaluation scores, preferred vendor, signed contract
3. Control Procurements Manages the vendor relationship, monitors contract performance and makes changes so both parties meet their obligations. Acceptance records, approved changes, verified invoices, closed contract
Exam framing: never answer “procurement is buying things” and stop. The markable version names the three processes, the artefact each one produces, and the fact that the risk of the work moves to the seller only to the extent the contract says it does.

Plan Procurement Management

Planning answers four questions before any vendor is contacted, then justifies the biggest of the answers — whether to buy at all — with a make-or-buy analysis.

Definition

Plan Procurement Management is the process of documenting project procurement decisions, specifying the approach, and identifying potential sellers.

What do we need to buy?

Scope the buy: a software component, a service, a result, or nothing at all.

How will we buy it?

RFP for complex services, RFQ for standard items; which contract type; what evaluation criteria.

When will we buy it?

Timing against the schedule, so the vendor's lead time does not become the project's delay.

Who will manage it?

The contract owner, the approvals path and the governance around changes.

Purpose of planning procurement: acquire goods, services or results in a structured, efficient and cost-effective way; align procurement activities with project scope, schedule, budget and risks; and provide a roadmap for managing vendor relationships.

Make-or-Buy Analysis

Definition

Make-or-Buy Analysis is the process of deciding whether a product, service or result should be produced internally (make) or purchased/outsourced externally (buy). It helps the project manager choose the most cost-effective, time-efficient and risk-appropriate option.

Cost

Build cost versus purchase price, including the cost of managing the contract.

Time

Can the internal team deliver in the window the schedule allows?

Quality

Which route gives the standard the project needs, and how is it verified?

Expertise and skills

Does the capability exist in-house today, or would it have to be hired and trained?

Control and confidentiality

How much control over data, IP and priorities are you willing to hand over?

Strategic importance

Is this capability core to the organisation, or a commodity it should never own?

What may be bought — the NSW example

Need: independent security testing and launch support.
Approach: RFP for complex services, RFQ for standard items.
Timing: before assurance and the statewide rollout.

Who manages it

The project manager, supported by procurement and the responsible workstream lead.
Governance: evaluation criteria, approvals and a named contract owner.

The decision the NSW team actually made: licensing integration stayed internal (it is core, continuous and confidential), while independent security testing was bought (it is specialist, periodic and needs external credibility). Read a make-or-buy answer as a sentence about control and strategic importance, not only about price.
A bank is building a mobile app with AI-driven spending insights. The capability does not exist in-house and is central to the product's differentiation. Which factor most complicates a straightforward “buy” recommendation?
Time — a vendor can usually start sooner than a new internal hire
Strategic importance and control — the capability is core, so outsourcing it creates dependency
Quality — vendors never meet the quality standard of an internal team

Procurement Documents: SOW, RFP and RFQ

Three documents do most of the work in this knowledge area: the SOW says what must be delivered, the RFP asks the market how it would be delivered, and the RFQ asks the market what it would cost.

Statement of Work (SOW)

A detailed description of the products, services or results to be delivered under a contract. It sets the scope, deliverables, timelines and standards so that both buyer and seller understand exactly what is expected — the “what and how” document for procurement.

Gives vendors clarity on requirements Forms the basis for proposals (RFP responses) Reduces misunderstandings and scope creep Acts as a legal reference in disputes

Request for Proposal (RFP)

A formal document issued by a buyer to invite potential sellers to submit proposals on how they would meet the project's requirements. It carries enough detail for vendors to propose solutions, costs, schedules and approaches.

  • Communicate requirements clearly to potential vendors
  • Encourage competition and obtain the best value
  • Evaluate proposals on technical, financial and qualitative factors
  • Ensure transparency and fairness in vendor selection

Request for Quotation (RFQ)

A formal document issued by a buyer to solicit price quotations for well-defined goods or services. Unlike an RFP, an RFQ is primarily focused on price, delivery terms and payment conditions when requirements are clear and standardised.

  • Obtain competitive pricing for specific items or services
  • Identify the most cost-effective supplier that still meets technical and quality requirements
  • Establish a baseline for vendor comparison when scope and specifications are already fixed

RFQ fine print from the lecture: detailed specifications (model numbers, sizes, quantities), price-centric evaluation (costs, discounts, payment terms), standardised and commoditised items that are easily compared, and a short decision cycle. Use it for commodities such as office supplies and raw materials, standardised IT hardware or software licences, and situations where the requirements are clear and need no innovation.

Contract Types and the Risk They Transfer

The contract type is the single most consequential procurement decision after the make-or-buy call, because it fixes how payment is structured, how risk is shared and how performance is measured.

What a contract type defines

How payment is structured (fixed price, reimbursed cost plus fee, or hourly rates plus materials); how risk is shared between buyer and seller; and how performance is measured.

AspectFixed-price contractsCost-reimbursable contractsTime and material contracts
DefinitionPrice is agreed in advance, regardless of actual costsBuyer reimburses the seller for actual costs, plus a fee or profitHybrid of fixed and cost-reimbursable: the buyer pays per hour/day plus materials
Risk on buyerLowHighMedium
Risk on sellerHighLowMedium
AdvantagesPredictable cost for the buyer; risk sits with the sellerFlexibility; good for R&D or uncertain projectsSimple to administer; flexible scope
DisadvantagesSeller may cut corners if costs rise; change requests are expensiveHigher cost risk for the buyer; less incentive for the seller to control costsRisk of cost overruns if not monitored
Best use caseClear scope, predictable deliverablesR&D, uncertain requirementsStaff augmentation, flexible scope
Risk transfer is not risk removal. A fixed-price contract does not make an uncertain scope certain; it moves the cost consequence to the seller, who will price that risk into the bid, or cut corners to protect the margin. That trade-off is the whole content of a contract-type answer.

Worked example — choosing contract types for the TechSolutions ERP project. TechSolutions Pty Ltd is a mid-sized Sydney manufacturer implementing a cloud-based ERP system. It outsources ERP software customisation, cloud deployment, data migration and cybersecurity audits, and procures servers, software licences and network infrastructure. Three vendors are selected: an offshore ERP development vendor, an onshore cloud hosting provider and a nearshore cybersecurity auditing firm.

1

Cloud hosting provider — fixed-price (FP-EPA if multi-year). Hosting is a well-defined, commoditised service with measurable service levels (an RFQ profile). The scope is predictable, so a fixed price transfers cost risk to the seller and gives TechSolutions a defensible operating budget. If the contract runs for several years, the EPA variant protects both parties from market movement.

2

ERP customisation — fixed-price with incentives, or T&M only with a tight ceiling. A fixed price is attractive because customization is the classic source of scope creep, but the requirements are not fully known at award. FFP with strict acceptance criteria suits the well-specified modules; a T&M arrangement is defensible for the exploratory interface work — with the cost-overrun risk explicitly monitored, because T&M puts medium risk on both sides.

3

Cybersecurity audit — fixed-price with an incentive, or cost plus fixed fee. The audit is periodic, specialist and defined by a standard, which suits a fixed price. Where the audit's depth depends on what it finds, CPFF keeps the seller willing to report what it discovers instead of minimising effort to protect a fixed margin — the reason cost-reimbursable suits uncertain requirements.

4

The sentence that earns the mark. “Choose the contract type by asking how well the scope is understood and who can best absorb the cost risk: fixed-price where the deliverables are predictable, cost-reimbursable where the work is genuinely uncertain, and T&M where the need is flexible staffing — then write the acceptance criteria and payment milestones so that verification, not the invoice date, releases payment.”

A vendor accepts a firm fixed-price contract for a module whose requirements are still changing. What is the most likely consequence?
The buyer's cost risk rises, because the seller will pass every change through
The seller either prices the uncertainty in advance or protects margin by cutting corners and charging dearly for change requests
Nothing changes, because a fixed price makes the scope fixed too

Selecting a Vendor: Criteria, Methods and Scoring

Vendor evaluation exists so that the organisation selects the right supplier on overall value, quality and risk management — not just on the lowest price.

Cost and pricing

Total cost of ownership, transparency of pricing, flexibility in payment terms.

Quality of goods and services

Compliance with specifications and standards.

Technical capability

Availability of skilled personnel and technical know-how.

Customer service and support

Responsiveness to queries, complaints and service issues.

MethodWhat it isBest for
Weighted scoring model Criteria are weighted and each vendor is scored against them; the weighted totals are compared. Complex procurements where multiple factors matter, not just price
Checklist A pass/fail screen against minimum standards. When compliance to minimum standards (certifications, licences) is critical
Vendor scorecard Ongoing KPI reporting on a supplier already engaged. Long-term supplier relationships
Vendor scorecard, in the lecture's detail

Purpose: ongoing performance monitoring of suppliers already engaged. Focus: KPIs such as delivery timeliness, quality, cost, customer service and compliance. Nature: retrospective and continuous — used after awarding the contract to evaluate how well the supplier is performing. Format: typically a dashboard or report with scores, ratings or traffic lights. Example: a monthly supplier performance review showing on-time delivery at 92% (green) and a defect rate of 4% (yellow).

The Menti question from the lecture. USyd is tendering for a new catering supplier for campus events. The main requirement is compliance with food safety certifications, valid insurance, and health and hygiene regulations; price and menu variety are secondary. Which evaluation method fits? The checklist — the decisive criteria are minimum compliance standards, not a trade-off between weighted factors. If price and variety had been the main levers, a weighted scoring model would have been the better instrument.

Step 1 — weight the criteria

Agree the weights before any proposal is opened: for the NSW security testing buy, capability, security, delivery and price.

Step 2 — score each vendor

Score every vendor against the same scale, with the evaluator's comments recorded as evidence.

Step 3 — check mandatory gates

A weighted score never rescues a vendor that fails a mandatory requirement: compliance is a gate, not a criterion.

Step 4 — decide on value

The NSW selection result: the chosen supplier meets mandatory security and delivery requirements and offers the best overall value.

Worked example — weighted scoring of two vendors for independent security and performance testing. Weights follow the NSW evaluation row: capability 35%, security 30%, delivery 25%, price 10%. Scores are out of 10.

1

Set the arithmetic up. Weighted total = Σ (weight × score), with the weights summing to 1. Both vendors are scored on the same four criteria, so the comparison is like for like.

2

Vendor A (specialist, expensive). Capability 9, security 9, delivery 7, price 5:
0.35×9 + 0.30×9 + 0.25×7 + 0.10×5 = 3.15 + 2.70 + 1.75 + 0.50 = 8.10.

3

Vendor B (generalist, cheap). Capability 7, security 6, delivery 9, price 9:
0.35×7 + 0.30×6 + 0.25×9 + 0.10×9 = 2.45 + 1.80 + 2.25 + 0.90 = 7.40.

4

Read the result, then check the gates. Vendor A wins on weighted value (8.10 versus 7.40) even though it is the more expensive bid, because price carries only 10% of the weight. Before awarding, confirm that both vendors pass the mandatory security and delivery requirements — a failed gate disqualifies a vendor regardless of score.

5

Say what would change the answer. Reweighting towards price (capability 30%, security 25%, delivery 20%, price 25%) moves the totals to 0.30×9 + 0.25×9 + 0.20×7 + 0.25×5 = 7.60 for A and 0.30×7 + 0.25×6 + 0.20×9 + 0.25×9 = 7.65 for B — the ranking flips, and by five hundredths of a point. That is exactly why the evaluation criteria are published in the RFP in advance.

Conduct Procurements: From Documents to a Signed Contract

Conduct Procurements is where planning meets the market. The process is short in definition and long in discipline: everyone must be treated the same way, and the basis of the decision must be recorded.

Definition

Conduct Procurements is the process of obtaining seller responses, evaluating them, and awarding the contract. It transforms procurement planning into action by interacting with the market to acquire the needed goods or services.

Procurement stageNSW case applicationDecision evidenceOutput
Issue RFP Provide a SOW for independent security and performance testing Proposed method Comparable bids
Receive proposals Hold a bidder briefing and issue the same clarifications to all vendors Compliance check Eligible bids
Evaluate vendors Apply weighted scoring to capability, security, delivery and price Scores and evaluator comments Preferred vendor
Negotiate and award Agree milestones, acceptance criteria, price and reporting Final offer and terms Signed contract
Selection result (NSW): the chosen supplier meets mandatory security and delivery requirements and offers the best overall value. Both halves matter: passing the mandatory gates is necessary, and the weighted score decides between the vendors that pass.

Why fairness is a process requirement

Identical information to every bidder, identical deadlines, and criteria published in the RFP protect the evaluation from challenge. A clarification sent to one vendor and not the others invalidates the comparison it was meant to improve.

What negotiation actually settles

Milestones, acceptance criteria, price and reporting frequency — the lecture's four items. Note that acceptance criteria are negotiated before the work starts, because they are what Control Procurements will later verify against.

During an RFP, one bidder emails a question about the security requirements. What should the buyer do?
Answer the bidder privately, since only that vendor asked
Issue the clarification to all vendors so every bid is prepared on the same information
Decline to answer, because questions during an RFP are not permitted

Control Procurements: Managing the Contract

Awarding the contract is the middle of the story, not the end. Control Procurements runs until the last invoice is verified and the contract is closed.

Definition

Control Procurements is the process of managing relationships with vendors, monitoring contract performance, and making changes as needed to ensure that both the buyer and the seller meet their contractual obligations.

Objectives (lecture)

  • Ensure the vendor delivers per contract scope, schedule, cost and quality
  • Identify and address issues: delays, cost overruns, non-compliance
  • Manage contract changes and disputes fairly
  • Maintain proper communication and documentation

What gets monitored

  • Delivery status
  • Service levels (SLAs)
  • Quality findings
  • Approved changes

Payments and contract closure follow formal acceptance of deliverables — the gate is verification, not the invoice date.

Eight tips from the deck, in exam-ready order: monitor performance regularly · keep communication transparent · manage change formally · verify before payment · build strong relationships · manage risks proactively · ensure compliance · document everything.
The two failure modes this process prevents. Paying before verifying converts a delivery problem into a financial loss with no leverage left; changing scope informally leaves no record when the dispute arrives. Both are process failures, not vendor failures.
A vendor delivers a milestone late but the work meets the acceptance criteria and the SLA penalty clause is in the contract. What is the correct control action?
Reject the deliverable to punish the delay
Test acceptance, record the delay, and apply the agreed SLA or liquidated-damages consequence formally
Waive the penalty to protect the relationship and note it verbally

Risk management in procurement

Definition

Procurement risk management is the process of identifying, assessing and mitigating risks that arise when acquiring goods, services or works from external vendors. It ensures that vendor relationships, contracts and deliverables do not jeopardise project success.

CategoryTypical procurement risks
CostPrice fluctuations, hidden costs, penalties, cost overruns
ScheduleDelays in delivery, long lead times, logistics and transportation issues
QualityProducts or services that do not meet specifications; a vendor cutting corners
Compliance and legalBreach of contract terms, IP disputes
OperationalMiscommunication between vendor and buyer, poorly written contracts

Identify

Brainstorm potential risks with the project and procurement teams; review vendor history, market conditions and past lessons learned.

Assess

Analyse risks by likelihood and impact, and use a risk matrix to prioritise them as high, medium or low.

Respond and monitor

Track vendor KPIs, audit and inspect against the contract, and keep contingency plans and escalation procedures ready.

Where procurement risk meets the schedule: every week of float on the Gantt chart is a week of schedule risk the project can absorb. When vendor selection slips in weeks 2–4, the float is not in the critical path to absorb it — the contract award milestone moves and every downstream date moves with it. Outsourcing IT Projects

Outsourcing is the strategic cousin of procurement: not “which vendor supplies this item” but “which parts of the project should another organisation run altogether”.

Definition

Project outsourcing is the practice of hiring an external organisation or vendor to perform specific project activities, tasks or deliverables instead of executing them in-house. It is often used to leverage specialised expertise, reduce costs, or accelerate timelines.

Why organisations outsource

  • Cost efficiency: reduce labour, infrastructure and operational costs
  • Access to expertise: skills not available internally, such as cloud architecture or AI development
  • Focus on core activities: vendors handle support or technical work
  • Risk sharing: vendors may assume delivery, technology or compliance risk

What it costs you

  • Communication issues across geographies and time zones
  • Quality control and adherence to standards
  • Confidentiality and data security concerns
  • Vendor dependency and lack of internal skill development
  • Hidden costs in coordination or contract management
Type of outsourcingWhat is handed overLecture example
Complete project outsourcingThe vendor manages the entire project from initiation to closureOutsourcing the development of a mobile app from design to deployment
Partial project outsourcingOnly specific components or tasksSoftware developed in-house, testing or UI/UX design outsourced
Business process outsourcing (BPO)Non-core business processes related to the projectCustomer support, payroll processing, IT helpdesk services
IT / technical outsourcingSpecialised vendors for software development, cloud infrastructure or cybersecurityCloud migration, AI model development

Benefits of outsourcing IT projects

  • Cost reduction and access to global talent
  • Faster project completion through experienced vendors
  • Reduced burden on internal teams
  • Flexibility in resource management
  • Access to skills and technologies unavailable in the company
  • Reduction of both fixed and recurrent costs
  • Allows the client organisation to focus on its core business

Governance of outsourcing

The framework of policies, processes, roles and oversight that ensures outsourced activities align with organisational objectives, deliver value and manage risks effectively — accountability, performance monitoring and compliance in vendor relationships.

  • Strategic alignment: outsourced services align with business goals and project objectives
  • Contract management: oversight of contract adherence, including deliverables, milestones, penalties and incentives
  • Roles and responsibilities: who manages the vendor (Vendor Manager, Project Manager), plus vendor responsibilities, deliverables and escalation points
Integrating procurement and outsourcing. Alignment with project objectives; risk reduction (fewer schedule delays, cost overruns and quality issues); efficient use of combined internal and external resources; better vendor management through clear accountability and compliance; and better strategic decisions about what to build in-house versus buy. The five best practices: start early (include procurement and outsourcing planning in initiation), align contracts with project milestones (payments and deliverables match the schedule), use integrated tools (track internal and external tasks in one system), keep communicating, and mitigate proactively (identify dependencies, keep alternate vendors and contingency inventory).

Worked case answer — TechSolutions Pty Ltd. Mid-sized Sydney manufacturer, cloud-based ERP, internal IT team without ERP customisation or cloud deployment expertise.

1

What is insourced, what is outsourced, what is procured. Insourced: project management, system integration and change management. Outsourced: ERP software customisation, cloud deployment, data migration, cybersecurity audits. Procured: servers for hybrid cloud backup, software licences, network infrastructure.

2

The sourcing model behind each vendor. ERP development vendor — offshore (distant country, cost savings, time-zone risk). Cloud hosting provider — onshore (same country, latency and data-residency reasons). Cybersecurity auditing firm — nearshore (nearby country/time zone, credible independence at lower cost). Together the three are a multisource arrangement, which is itself a mitigation: no single geography or vendor failure stops the project.

3

Why procurement planning matters here. Vendor selection occupies weeks 2–4 and the contract is awarded in week 5, before any deployment work can start. If the RFP, evaluation criteria and contract types are not settled in that window, the eleven-week deployment phase starts late and the whole twenty-week schedule moves.

4

Risks per vendor and the matching mitigation. Offshore ERP vendor: time-zone communication and quality risk → strict acceptance criteria in the SOW, milestone-based payments and liquidated damages for late delivery. Onshore hosting provider: concentration and SLA risk → service-level KPIs, a secondary provider in the contingency plan, and data-residency clauses. Nearshore audit firm: access to sensitive systems → confidentiality terms, scoped access and audit rights.

5

How the project manager controls the procurements. Review deliverables against the contract, check milestones and SLAs, test acceptance criteria before releasing payment, manage every change formally, verify the invoice, then close the contract. If the offshore vendor slips, multisourcing (a second development vendor, or moving a work package to the onshore partner) is the pre-agreed response — which only works if the contingency was planned before the contract was signed.

Decision analysis
