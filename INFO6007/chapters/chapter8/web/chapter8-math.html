INFO6007 — Week 8 supplement

Foundations: the arithmetic behind buying well

Week 8 is a decision-making week, and most of its decisions can be made quantitative: whether to build or buy, which vendor scores highest, what a contract will actually cost, which dates have no slack, and how much contingency a procurement risk deserves. This page pins down the definitions first, then works the arithmetic through with the lecture's own case material — the NSW Digital Driver Licence and the TechSolutions ERP project.

Definitions that must be exact

Marks in this unit are lost on definitions more often than on ideas. Each of these is one sentence, with the consequence included.

Project procurement management

The process of acquiring goods, services or results from outside the project team to meet project needs — deciding what to buy, how to buy it, who to buy it from, and how to ensure the contract is successfully completed.

Plan / Conduct / Control Procurements

Plan: document procurement decisions, specify the approach and identify potential sellers. Conduct: obtain seller responses, evaluate them and award the contract. Control: manage vendor relationships, monitor contract performance and make changes so both parties meet their obligations.

Make-or-buy analysis

Deciding whether a product, service or result should be produced internally (make) or purchased or outsourced externally (buy), on the basis of cost, time, quality, expertise, control and confidentiality, and strategic importance.

Statement of Work (SOW)

A detailed description of the products, services or results to be delivered under a contract. It sets scope, deliverables, timelines and standards so both buyer and seller understand exactly what is expected, and it acts as the legal reference in a dispute.

Request for Proposal (RFP) vs Request for Quotation (RFQ)

An RFP invites potential sellers to submit proposals on how they would meet the project's requirements; proposals are evaluated on quality, approach, capability and price. An RFQ solicits price quotations for well-defined goods or services where scope and specifications are already fixed; it is evaluated mainly on cost and delivery.

Procurement schedule

A timeline of the key activities, milestones and deadlines for managing procurement activities, aligned with the overall project schedule so that vendor lead times do not become project delays. Elements: planning timeline, document preparation, vendor selection, contract award milestone, delivery and performance periods, monitoring and controlling, and procurement closure.

Break-even, scoring and contract arithmetic

Four pieces of arithmetic cover almost every quantitative procurement question: whether to build or buy, how to score vendors, what a solution really costs, and what an incentive contract will pay.

1. Make-or-buy break-even

The internal option carries a fixed cost and a variable cost; the market option is priced per unit:

\[ C_{\text{make}}(Q) = F + vQ, \qquad C_{\text{buy}}(Q) = pQ \]

Setting them equal gives the break-even quantity, which exists only when the market price exceeds the internal variable cost:

\[ Q^{*} = \frac{F}{p - v}, \qquad p > v \]

Below \(Q^{*}\) buying is cheaper; above it, making is. When \(p \le v\) the internal option never breaks even on cost, and the decision must be argued on control, confidentiality, capability or strategic importance instead.

Worked example (illustrative numbers). A team is deciding whether to build an automated regression-testing capability or buy test runs from a specialist vendor. Internal build: \(F = \$180{,}000\) of tooling, licences and setup, then \(v = \$40\) per test run to operate. The vendor charges \(p = \$100\) per run. Then \(Q^{*} = 180{,}000/(100-40) = 3{,}000\) runs, and the cost at break-even is \(3{,}000 \times \$100 = \$300{,}000\). At 600 runs a year the project breaks even on the build after 5 years — which may be longer than the project itself, and is exactly the kind of answer that must be paired with the non-cost factors from the lecture.

2. Weighted vendor scoring

Agree weights that sum to one, score every vendor on the same scale, then take the weighted total:

\[ S_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j\, s_{ij}, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j = 1 \]

If price is scored as a criterion rather than used as a gate, normalise it so the cheapest compliant bid scores full marks:

\[ s_{\text{price},i} = 10 \times \frac{P_{\min}}{P_i} \]

3. Total cost of ownership

The lecture's first evaluation criterion is total cost of ownership, not sticker price:

\[ \text{TCO} = C_{\text{acq}} + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{C_{\text{run},t}}{(1+r)^{t}} + C_{\text{exit}} \]

Acquisition cost, running cost over the life of the deal (discounted at rate \(r\) if you want to be precise; set \(r = 0\) for a simple sum), and the cost of exiting the arrangement — migration, data extraction, retraining. A cheaper licence with a higher support annuity and a punitive exit is frequently the more expensive purchase.

Reading the race: Vendor X TCO \(= 120 + 75 + 15 = \$210\text{k}\); Vendor Y TCO \(= 80 + 135 + 40 = \$255\text{k}\). The lower bid loses on ownership cost by $45k — the arithmetic that turns “cost and pricing: total cost of ownership” from a phrase into a decision.

4. Incentive and reimbursable contract pricing

In a fixed-price incentive fee contract the seller's fee moves with the actual cost, sharing the variance by the agreed ratio:

\[ \text{fee}_{\text{final}} = f_t + \alpha_s\,(C_t - C_a), \qquad \text{price} = C_a + \text{fee}_{\text{final}} \]

The price cannot exceed the ceiling \(P_c\). The point of total assumption is the actual cost at which the ceiling is reached and the seller begins to absorb every additional dollar:

\[ \text{PTA} = \frac{P_c - (C_t + f_t)}{\alpha_b} + C_t, \qquad \alpha_b = 1 - \alpha_s \]

For a plain cost-plus-fixed-fee contract the buyer's expected cost under an uncertain overrun is an expectation rather than a formula:

\[ \mathbb{E}[C_{\text{CPFF}}] = C_{\text{base}} + f + q\,O \]

where \(q\) is the probability of the overrun and \(O\) its size. Buy the fixed price only when \(P < \mathbb{E}[C_{\text{CPFF}}]\) — remembering that the fixed price already contains the seller's own risk premium, which is why the two are comparable at all.

Worked solution — the make-or-buy recommendation, written as an answer.

1

State the model. Internal: \(C_{\text{make}}(Q) = 180{,}000 + 40Q\). Vendor: \(C_{\text{buy}}(Q) = 100Q\).

2

Find the break-even. \(Q^{*} = \dfrac{F}{p-v} = \dfrac{180{,}000}{100-40} = 3{,}000\) runs. At that volume both options cost \(\$300{,}000\).

3

Compare with the actual demand. The project forecasts 600 runs a year, so buying costs \(\$60{,}000\) a year while the build costs \(\$180{,}000\) up front plus \(\$24{,}000\) a year — roughly five years to recover the investment. If the project life is two years, buy. If the capability is needed permanently across a portfolio, the volume will pass 3,000 and make becomes cheaper.

4

Add the factors the arithmetic ignores. Independent security testing is periodic, specialist and benefits from external credibility, so confidentiality and control are not compromised by buying it. A capability that touches customer data continuously, or that is strategically differentiating, would push the decision the other way even at a higher cost.

5

Close the way the lecture does. “Compare the options in terms of cost, risk and benefits, then document and justify the decision in the Procurement Management Plan.” The number is evidence; the justification is the answer.

Worked solution — pricing an incentive contract and choosing between contract types. Target cost \(C_t = \$500{,}000\), target fee \(f_t = \$50{,}000\), seller's share \(\alpha_s = 30\%\), ceiling \(P_c = \$580{,}000\).

1

Target price. \(C_t + f_t = \$550{,}000\). That is what the buyer expects to pay if the seller hits the target.

2

An overrun of $20k. \(\text{fee}_{\text{final}} = 50{,}000 + 0.30(500{,}000 - 520{,}000) = 50{,}000 - 6{,}000 = \$44{,}000\). Price \(= 520{,}000 + 44{,}000 = \$564{,}000\). The buyer pays $14k more than target while the seller's fee falls by $6k: the 70/30 split in action.

3

The point of total assumption. \(\text{PTA} = \dfrac{580{,}000 - 550{,}000}{0.70} + 500{,}000 = 42{,}857 + 500{,}000 = \$542{,}857\). Check it: at that cost the fee is \(50{,}000 + 0.30(-42{,}857) = \$37{,}143\) and the price is \(542{,}857 + 37{,}143 = \$580{,}000\), exactly the ceiling. Beyond $542,857 of actual cost the seller absorbs 100% of every further dollar.

4

Fixed price versus cost plus fixed fee. Suppose the base cost is $500,000, the CPFF fee $50,000, and there is a 40% chance of a $100,000 overrun that a CPFF would have to reimburse. Then \(\mathbb{E}[C_{\text{CPFF}}] = 500{,}000 + 50{,}000 + 0.40(100{,}000) = \$590{,}000\). A firm fixed price of $570,000 is therefore the better buy — the seller is charging a $70,000 premium over target to carry the risk, and the buyer's expected cost is still $20,000 lower.

5

Say the caveat. The expectation assumes the overrun probability is real and the fixed-price bidder can actually absorb the variance. If the seller is under-capitalised, the fixed price transfers a risk the seller cannot carry, and the buyer inherits a failed delivery instead of a cost overrun.

5. Contract progress: planned, earned and actual

Vendor performance in three curves

The cost-management toolkit applies unchanged to a contract: planned value \(PV\) is the value the contract schedules for the period, earned value \(EV\) is the value of work formally accepted, and actual cost \(AC\) is what has been invoiced.

\[ SV = EV - PV, \qquad CV = EV - AC, \qquad SPI = \frac{EV}{PV}, \qquad CPI = \frac{EV}{AC} \]

In a procurement setting the interpretation is contractual rather than internal. \(EV\) can only be claimed for accepted deliverables, which is why “verify before payment” is what keeps \(EV\) honest — and why a negative \(SV\) at a milestone is a trigger to apply the schedule consequence rather than to keep paying.

Internal build costs $180,000 plus $40 per unit; the vendor charges $100 per unit. Forecast demand is 2,000 units. Which option is cheaper, and by how much?
The internal build, because the fixed cost is spread over 2,000 units
Buying, by $60,000, because 2,000 units is below the break-even of 3,000
The two options cost the same once the fixed cost is amortised

The procurement schedule on the critical path

A procurement schedule is a network like any other. The dates the vendors control need to be checked against the dates the project cannot move.

Forward pass, backward pass, float

Give every activity \(i\) a duration \(d_i\) in weeks. The earliest an activity can run is set by its predecessors:

\[ ES_i = \max_{j \in \text{pred}(i)} EF_j, \qquad EF_i = ES_i + d_i \]

The latest it can run without moving the project finish \(T = \max_i EF_i\) is set by its successors:

\[ LF_i = \min_{k \in \text{succ}(i)} LS_k, \qquad LS_i = LF_i - d_i \]

Total float is the slack that makes schedule risk manageable:

\[ TF_i = LS_i - ES_i = LF_i - EF_i \]

The critical path is the chain of activities with \(TF = 0\). A delay anywhere on it delays the project by the same amount; a delay off it consumes float first.

Weeks are counted from zero, so activity D spans weeks 6–16 of the calendar schedule and finishes at the end of week 16; E occupies weeks 17–18 and F week 19–20, matching the project timeline in the case study: award in week 5, deployment weeks 6–16, testing and acceptance weeks 17–18, closure in week 20.

Why the parallel branches matter. Hardware procurement, the cybersecurity audit and data migration can all slip by five to eight weeks without moving the project finish, because they must only be complete before testing starts. The ERP deployment cannot slip at all. That asymmetry is what a procurement schedule is for: it tells you which vendor conversation is urgent and which one can absorb a delay.

Worked solution — which dates move when a vendor slips. The offshore ERP vendor advises that deployment will take 14 weeks instead of 11.

1

Locate the activity. D is on the critical path (\(TF = 0\)), so a three-week slip moves the project finish one for one: \(T\) becomes 23 weeks and closure lands in week 23 instead of week 20.

2

Recompute the float on the branches. With E now starting at 19, \(LF\) for P1, P2 and P3 becomes 19: P1 float \(= 19 - 4 - 5 = 10\) weeks, P2 \(= 11\), P3 \(= 8\). The branches gain slack; the project loses three weeks. Float moving around like this is why it is recomputed after every change.

3

Can the branches pay for a recovery? No: they are not predecessors of D, so shortening the audit cannot pull deployment forward. Recovery has to come from D itself (more vendor resources, resequencing customisation), from overlapping testing with the last deployment increments at the team's own risk, or from accepting the slip.

4

What the contract should already say. This is the scenario liquidated damages exist for: a schedule risk priced in advance. The control response is to record the delay against the milestone, apply the agreed consequence formally, and re-baseline the schedule through change control — not to negotiate a verbal understanding.

Risk exposure and the contingency reserve

Procurement risk becomes manageable when likelihood and impact are multiplied instead of argued about. The lecture's risk matrix is the qualitative version of the same calculation.

Expected loss per risk, and the reserve

For each identified risk \(r\) with probability \(p_r\) and monetary impact \(I_r\):

\[ RE_r = p_r \times I_r, \qquad \text{contingency} \ge \sum_{r} RE_r \]

A response is worth its cost when it removes more expected loss than it costs:

\[ \Delta RE = RE_{\text{before}} - RE_{\text{after}} > \text{cost of the response} \]

Is the mitigation worth it? Suppose a second development vendor costs $30k and cuts the probability of a late ERP delivery from 0.40 to 0.15. Exposure falls from \(0.40 \times 200{,}000 = \$80\text{k}\) to \(0.15 \times 200{,}000 = \$30\text{k}\), a \(\Delta RE\) of $50k for a $30k response — worth doing on the numbers, and it also buys negotiation leverage. Compare that with the IP dispute: cutting a 0.10 probability further is expensive, so the rational spend is contract terms, not insurance.
Risk A has probability 0.5 and impact $40k; risk B has probability 0.1 and impact $300k. Which statement is correct?
A has the higher exposure because it is five times more likely
B has the higher expected loss, $30k against $20k, even though it is less likely
Neither can be ranked, because likelihood and impact are different units
