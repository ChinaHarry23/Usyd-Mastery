The Six Processes of Resource Management

Project resource management is the process of identifying, acquiring and managing the resources — both human and physical — required for the successful delivery of a project. Week 6 splits that work into six processes.

Definition

Project resource management is the process of identifying, acquiring and managing the resources (human and physical) required for successful project delivery.

Human resources

  • Developers, testers, analysts
  • Project managers
  • DevOps, data engineers

Physical / technical resources

  • Servers and cloud infrastructure
  • Software licences and tools
  • Hardware and test devices

Why it matters more in IT

  • IT projects need specialised roles — cloud engineers, data scientists, cybersecurity experts
  • Many resources are shared across multiple projects
  • Licences, cloud credits and hardware must be planned in advance

The cost of poor planning

  • Resource conflicts between projects
  • Delays when a specialist is not free
  • Increased cost from expedited hiring, overtime or unused licences
Exam framing: name the process, then say what it produces. “Acquiring resources” is worth little on its own; “acquiring resources produces the project team assignments and the resource calendars” is a markable answer.

People: Leadership, Motivation and the Human Resource

Human resources are the people involved in a project, and in IT projects they are the most critical asset because success depends on specialised skills, creativity and collaboration. Unlike machines or budgets, human performance is variable.

Human resource management in projects

Organising, managing and leading the project team so that the project has the right people with the right skills, and that they are motivated, managed and retained throughout the project lifecycle.

Key aspect of managing peopleWhat it covers
Leadership and motivationInfluencing and guiding people towards the project objectives; sustaining effort across a long delivery
CommunicationWho is told what, when and through which channel — the failure mode behind most “resourcing” complaints
Conflict resolutionHandling disagreements about priorities, design and technical approach before they damage the team
Performance managementSetting expectations, observing progress and giving feedback
Team developmentBuilding skills, trust and shared ways of working

Leadership

Influencing and guiding people towards achieving project objectives: inspiring, motivating, enabling collaboration. Crucial in IT because teams are cross-functional (developers, testers, analysts, architects), technology changes rapidly, and global or distributed teams need coordination.

Management

Planning, organising and controlling resources: schedules, budgets, assignments, reports. The two are not alternatives — a project needs both, and a good leader adapts style to the project environment, the team's maturity and the organisational culture.

Motivation: intrinsic and extrinsic

AspectIntrinsic motivationExtrinsic motivation
DefinitionComes from within the individual: personal satisfaction, curiosity or passionComes from external factors: rewards, recognition, or avoidance of punishment
CharacteristicsEnjoyment of the work itself; desire to learn or master a skill; feeling of accomplishmentDriven by tangible or social incentives; performance linked to external rewards
Source of driveInternal (self-driven)External (reward / punishment)

The Resource Breakdown Structure and Resource Estimating

A resource breakdown structure (RBS) is a hierarchical decomposition of project resources by category and type. It complements the work breakdown structure: the WBS decomposes tasks, the RBS decomposes the resources those tasks consume.

Resource breakdown structure

A hierarchical decomposition of project resources by category and type, used to plan, track and manage people, equipment, materials and software. The deck's ideal shape has three levels:

WBS versus RBS: the WBS answers “what work must be done?” and the RBS answers “what does that work consume?”. Both are hierarchies, and the RBS is deliberately organised by resource type, not by task, so that a single specialist appearing in five activities shows up once.

Estimating activity resources

Estimating activity resources means determining the types and quantities of people, equipment, materials and software needed to complete each activity. It matters because accurate resource estimates drive the cost baseline, the schedule and the risk register.

Purpose

  • Determine how many resources each activity needs
  • Plan budgets and schedules accurately
  • Identify resource constraints or gaps early
  • Support workload distribution and resource levelling

Four resource types in IT

  • Human — developers, testers, designers, DevOps, PMs
  • Equipment / hardware — servers, laptops, devices, networking
  • Software / tools — IDEs, PM software, licences, cloud platforms
  • Materials — documentation templates, test scripts, manuals

App developmentBottom-upMobile developers + UX + test devicesFeature complexity
System integrationExpert judgementAPI engineers + secure test environmentLegacy interfaces
Security & privacy assuranceExpert judgementCyber, privacy + assurance toolsAssurance findings
Trials & rollout readinessAnalogous + parametricService teams + cloud capacityUser demand

Questions that expose a bad estimate: How difficult are these specific activities? Is anything in scope unique enough to change the resource profile? Are resources available full-time or part-time? Can one resource handle several tasks at once? Are there conflicts with other projects? What hardware, software or licences are required, and are there availability or capacity constraints?
A project must be estimated in an afternoon for a business case, using the actual resource figures from a similar mobile app delivered last year. Which technique is being used, and what is its weakness?
Analogous estimating — fast, but the least accurate because it assumes the projects are genuinely comparable
Bottom-up estimating — accurate but it ignores historical data
Three-point (PERT) estimating — it removes uncertainty entirely

The RACI Matrix

A RACI matrix is a responsibility assignment tool that clarifies roles and responsibilities for project tasks or deliverables. It is especially useful in complex IT projects with multiple stakeholders, because it prevents confusion, overlaps and gaps in accountability.

The four letters

  • R — Responsible: the person(s) who do the work or complete the task
  • A — Accountable: the person who is ultimately answerable; approves the work and ensures the task is completed
  • C — Consulted: people whose input is sought before the task is done — two-way communication
  • I — Informed: people who need to be updated on progress or decisions — one-way communication

The two rules that catch people out

  • Exactly one A per task. Shared accountability is the same as no accountability.
  • C and I are not the same. Consulted is a two-way conversation before the decision; informed is a one-way notification afterwards.
  • R can be shared, and one person can be R on one task and A on another.
How to build one in practice: list the deliverables down the rows and the roles across the columns, then fill in A first (one per row), then R, then ask of each remaining role “does this person's input change the outcome before the work is done (C) or do they only need to know (I)?”. An empty cell is a decision, not an oversight — it means the role has no involvement.
In a RACI matrix, a row shows two roles marked A. What does this tell you?
It is correct: two accountable people means twice the commitment
It is a defect: accountability for a task must sit with exactly one person
It means the task needs two consulted stakeholders instead of one

Resource Loading, Histograms and Levelling

Three ideas fit together: loading measures how much work each resource carries over time, the histogram draws that measurement, and levelling resolves the over-allocation the histogram reveals.

Resource loading

Assigning specific resources to project activities over time and measuring their workload, so that over-allocated or underutilised resources can be identified. Units are hours, days or FTE (full-time equivalent) per time period, and the deck gives the relationship:

Resource load = ∑ allocated hours / available hours

What loading is for

  • Determine how much work each resource is assigned at a given time
  • Identify over-allocated or underutilised resources
  • Improve schedule accuracy and productivity
  • Support decisions about adjustments or hiring

The resource histogram

A bar chart of how much of each resource is allocated over time, used to visualise resource usage, workload distribution and potential over-allocation. X-axis: time periods (days, weeks, months, sprints). Y-axis: resource usage (hours, FTEs or number of resources). Bars: the allocation in each period. Common for human resources, but the same chart tracks equipment, licences or budget.

Resource levelling

Resolving resource over-allocation by adjusting the project schedule or redistributing resources. The goal is that no resource is overburdened while the project timeline is maintained as far as possible. It prevents burnout, avoids conflicts and delays caused by shortages, optimises utilisation and produces a schedule that can actually be delivered.

TechniqueWhat you doWhat it costs
Delay tasksMove an activity later, using its floatFloat is consumed; the end date survives only while float remains
Split tasksBreak an activity into two smaller windows around the peakExtra handover and set-up effort; worse for knowledge work
Reassign tasksGive the work to a resource with spare capacityRamp-up time, and the new person may be less productive
Adjust work hoursChange patterns, add part-time or overtime hoursOvertime cost and burnout risk; the deck's caution about sustainability
Why the critical path decides the answer: levelling only works on activities that have float. An over-allocated critical activity cannot be delayed without moving the project end date, so the response there is to reassign, split, adjust hours, or accept a schedule change — not to delay.

Worked example — levelling the QA load. Two testers are available. Module testing (T1), which sits on the network's floating path, needs 2 testers for weeks 6–7. The regression suite (T2) needs 2 testers for two weeks, can start in week 7 at the earliest, and has two weeks of float. UAT support (T3) needs 1 tester in week 8.

1

Load the plan. At earliest start, T1 occupies weeks 6–7 and T2 occupies weeks 7–8, with T3 in week 8. Weekly QA demand is 2, 4, 3, 0, 0 — the peak is 4 testers in week 7 against a capacity of 2.

2

Diagnose. Week 7 is over-allocated by 2 testers, so utilisation there is 4 ÷ 2 = 200%, while average utilisation across the five weeks is only 9 ÷ 10 = 90%. Neither T1 nor T2 can be done by half a person, so the peak has to move, not shrink.

3

Choose the movable activity. T2's only successor is the integration test, which has no float of its own, so T2 can absorb up to two weeks of delay. T3's float is also two weeks but moving it would not help, and T1 is a predecessor of T3 as well as of the integration test. T2 is the candidate.

4

Level by delaying. Start T2 in week 9 instead of week 7. Demand becomes 2, 2, 1, 2, 2 across weeks 6–10 — a profile whose peak is exactly 100% of capacity. T2 has consumed its two weeks of float, so the release date is unchanged and T2 now sits exactly on its late finish: there is no float left for the next surprise.

5

State the trade-off. Delaying is the cheapest technique here only because the float existed. The alternative — splitting T2 into a week in period 9 and a week in period 10 — also flattens the profile, but pays for it in test-environment set-up twice and in the risk of an inconsistent regression baseline.

A resource histogram shows a peak of 3 testers in one week against a team of 2, on an activity that has 4 weeks of float. What is the most defensible first response?

Approve overtime for the peak week and keep the schedule unchanged
Recruit a third tester before the work starts

Acquiring Resources

Acquisition is the process of obtaining the human, physical and material resources needed to complete project activities, so that the right resources are available at the right time and delays are prevented.

MethodWhat it meansAdvantage
Internal acquisitionUse existing employees or in-house resourcesFamiliar with company culture; faster onboarding
External acquisitionHire new employees, contractors or consultantsBrings specific skills, expertise or additional capacity
ProcurementAcquire equipment, software or services through vendorsConverts a capital problem into a contract with defined deliverables
Multi-source acquisitionCombine internal resources, contractors and vendorsFlexibility: critical skills stay in-house while peaks are bought in
What acquisition produces: physical resource assignments, project team assignments and resource calendars. The calendar is the artefact that later makes levelling possible, because it records when each resource is actually available — leave, public holidays, part-time patterns and commitments to other projects.
The acquisition risk in IT: the scarcest roles are the specialised ones (cloud, security, data), and those people are typically shared across several projects at once. Negotiating for a named person months in advance is resource management; discovering in week 6 that they were never actually booked is a schedule risk that has already materialised.

Developing the Team

Developing the project team is the process of improving competencies, team interaction and the overall team environment in order to enhance project performance. It builds skills, motivation, collaboration and trust.

Purpose

  • Improve skills and competencies
  • Foster collaboration and communication
  • Increase motivation and engagement
  • Enhance project performance and quality
  • Build trust and a positive team culture

Key aspects

  • Training, mentoring and workshops for technical and soft skills
  • Activities that strengthen trust, communication and cooperation
  • Recognising achievement, giving feedback, aligning tasks with interests
  • Addressing interpersonal conflict promptly
  • Ensuring everyone understands their responsibilities and reporting lines
The project manager across all five stages

Acts as facilitator, coach and mediator; monitors team dynamics, motivation and performance; and uses tools such as the RACI matrix, resource loading and skill development to support the team's growth. The deck is explicit that the model is highly relevant to IT projects, where cross-functional teams, agile delivery and virtual collaboration are the norm.

Reward and recognition systems

Reward and recognition systems are structured approaches to acknowledging and incentivising contributions, achievements and behaviour. They motivate team members, increase productivity and improve morale — and they reinforce the behaviours the project needs while retaining scarce skills.

TypeExamplesBest used for
MonetaryBonuses, allowances, paid trainingDirect financial incentive for a defined performance or achievement
Non-monetaryPublic recognition, visibility with sponsors, flexible hoursRecognising contribution without direct financial compensation
Career-basedStretch assignments, certification, a path to a senior roleEnhancing growth and future prospects — the motivator Herzberg predicts will work
Team-basedShared celebration of a release, team-level awardEncouraging collaboration and shared accountability
The deck's NSW example: the five-step team-development focus is clarify roles → build safety and belonging → coach for mastery → enable collaboration → recognise achievement, monitoring skills and competence, team cohesion, engagement and team satisfaction. Note that it combines training and workshops with responsibility, feedback and recognition — motivators, not just hygiene.
A newly formed distributed team is polite, waits for instructions and has not yet agreed how code reviews will work. Which stage is it in, and what should the project manager do?
Storming — mediate the conflict and let the team settle it
Forming — clarify purpose, roles and the reporting structure
Performing — step back and let the team self-organise

Managing the Team: Conflict and Dysfunction

Managing the project team means tracking performance, providing feedback, resolving issues and coordinating changes. Conflict is a normal part of that, not evidence that the team is broken.

Conflict management

The process of identifying, addressing and resolving disagreements between team members to maintain productivity and collaboration. Conflicts are natural in IT projects because of differing opinions, priorities and technical approaches; effective handling protects team cohesion, morale and project success.

ModeAssertivenessCooperativenessWhen to use
CompetingHighLowCritical issues needing a quick decision, with limited time — assert your view at the expense of others (win–lose)
CollaboratingHighHighComplex issues that need input from all parties, to find a win–win solution
CompromisingModerateModerateTime-constrained situations or temporary solutions — middle ground, a partial win–win
AvoidingLowLowMinor issues, or when emotions are running high and the conversation should wait
AccommodatingLowHighTo preserve the relationship, or when the issue matters more to the other person

The dysfunctional team

A dysfunctional team fails to work effectively together, producing poor performance, low morale and project risk. The deck lists five common causes, and they are worth memorising in order because each one enables the next:

Absence of trust
→
Fear of conflict
→
Lack of commitment
→
Avoidance of accountability
→
Inattention to results
Team management strategies from the deck: define roles, responsibilities and accountability using tools such as RACI matrices; keep communication open, transparent and regular; apply a leadership style that fits the situation (transformational, servant or situational); identify and address conflicts early; and acknowledge contributions to sustain motivation and morale.
The management loop: what is managed is performance, communication, motivation, conflict and team development. Observe progress, workload and behaviour; then lead (adapting style to context and team maturity), motivate (mastery, meaning, recognition) and resolve (conflict, blockers, role gaps). The deck's illustrative rule is to address conflict, overload or low motivation early, and only then adjust assignments, support or leadership style.
A senior developer and the security officer disagree about whether an encryption library is acceptable. The release decision is due tomorrow and both hold firm. Which mode does the deck's table suggest, and why?
Collaborating — it always produces the best technical decision
Compromising — a middle ground is appropriate for a time-constrained decision, provided the residual risk is recorded
Avoiding — postpone the decision until the team has more information

Controlling Resources: Availability, Productivity and Cost

Controlling resources is monitoring resource utilisation, ensuring efficient use, resolving resource conflicts and making adjustments so that the right resources are available at the right time — preventing both over-allocation and underutilisation.

1. Record resource use
→
2. Compare with plan
→
3. Analyse variance
→
4. Correct and reallocate
→
5. Update and communicate

The control decision

Keep, reallocate, replace or release resources. The decision is made by comparing planned and actual use of devices, test environments, cloud capacity and rollout equipment — and it turns usage data into performance information, change requests and plan updates.

What is monitored

  • Availability — is the resource accessible and ready when needed?
  • Utilisation — how much of the available capacity is actually used?
  • Shortages — where demand exceeds supply, and for how long
  • Cost and condition — what it costs to keep, and whether it still works
ConceptDeck definitionWhat it looks like when it goes wrong
ProductivityHow efficiently resources are used to achieve project objectives; high productivity means maximising output with minimal wasted effort or resourcesThe DevOps engineer is slow on complex deployments, so the same work consumes more of the schedule and of the specialist's goodwill
AvailabilityThe extent to which resources are accessible and ready for project tasks when needed; linked to resource planning, scheduling and allocationA developer on leave removes capacity that the plan had already spent
CostThe financial expenditure associated with acquiring, using and maintaining resourcesCloud server usage exceeds the planned budget while the delivered scope stays the same
Scenario analysis — two ways to make the same assignment decision. A cost-based assignment chooses the cheaper resource: lower rate, in-house, or an existing licence. A schedule-based assignment chooses the faster or more available resource even at a higher rate, because the critical path is what the project is actually short of. The two answers diverge exactly when a task has float: on the critical path, buy schedule; off the critical path, buy cost.

Worked example — reading the resource cost picture. At the end of week 6 of a 12-week, $100,000 project: planned value PV = $52,000, earned value EV = $44,000, actual cost AC = $58,000.

1

Cost variance. CV = EV − AC = 44,000 − 58,000 = −$14,000. The project has spent $14,000 more than the work it has actually earned.

2

Schedule variance. SV = EV − PV = 44,000 − 52,000 = −$8,000. Work worth $8,000 that should have been finished by now has not been finished.

3

Indices. CPI = EV ÷ AC = 44,000 ÷ 58,000 = 0.759: 76 cents of value per dollar spent. SPI = EV ÷ PV = 44,000 ÷ 52,000 = 0.846: 85% of the planned progress.

4

Forecast. EAC = BAC ÷ CPI = 100,000 ÷ 0.759 ≈ $131,818, so VAC = 100,000 − 131,818 = −$31,818. ETC = EAC − AC ≈ $73,818 still to spend. At this rate the project finishes roughly 32% over budget.

5

Resource reading. The cost side is worse than the schedule side (0.76 against 0.85), which points at the resource drivers the case study names: cloud capacity running above plan and a specialist whose productivity has dropped. Recovering this needs a recovery index of TCPI = (BAC − EV) ÷ (BAC − AC) = 56,000 ÷ 42,000 = 1.33 — every remaining dollar must deliver $1.33 of value.

A task on the critical path can be done by an in-house developer at $90/hour or by a contractor at $140/hour who can start immediately and finish two weeks earlier. Which assignment does a schedule-based decision choose, and when is that the right call?
The in-house developer, because cost control dominates
The contractor, because the task is on the critical path and the two weeks cannot be recovered elsewhere
Neither — defer the choice until the next steering committee

Resource Management in Agile Environments

In agile, resource management is less about fixed long-term allocation and more about matching team capacity to sprint goals. Teams plan from available time, skills and workload rather than assuming full availability.

How agile changes the practice

  • Teams are cross-functional and self-managing, so the question is whether the team collectively has the capabilities to deliver value this sprint
  • Resource management becomes a continuous activity, reviewed as priorities and capacity change
  • Capacity planning shows how much work can realistically be completed in a sprint
  • Workload is monitored to avoid overallocation, bottlenecks and burnout

Key steps

  1. Define the project scope
  2. Identify necessary resources
  3. Estimate resource requirements
  4. Allocate and schedule resources
  5. Monitor resource utilisation
Best practiceWhat it means in a sprint cadence
Clear communication and collaborationOpen dialogue so everyone understands their role in the resource plan
Continuous monitoring and reassessmentReview and update the plan every sprint as requirements or availability change
FlexibilityBe ready to adjust for unexpected challenges or opportunities
Stakeholder involvementInvolve key stakeholders in resource planning to gain insight and buy-in
Documentation and reportingKeep records of the plan and report utilisation and progress regularly
People are still the most important agile resource. Agile emphasises collaboration, adaptability and shared ownership; cross-functional teams reduce hand-off delays and make it easier to respond to change. Resource management therefore includes not just staffing but building a team environment that supports communication, flexibility and sustainable delivery.

Case study: the Cloud Banking Application

Case Week 6

A 12-week Cloud Banking Application project with a budget of $100,000. The team is three backend developers, two frontend developers, two QA testers and one DevOps engineer. By week 6:

  • Backend Developer 1 is on leave, reducing availability and causing task delays
  • The DevOps engineer is struggling with complex deployments, lowering productivity
  • The QA testers are multitasking across modules
  • Cloud server usage has exceeded the planned budget

Worked answer — the four case questions, answered with Week 6 vocabulary.

1

Identify the resource challenges. A sudden availability loss (a backend developer on leave) that the plan had already spent; a productivity shortfall in a scarce specialist role (DevOps and complex deployments); over-allocation of the QA testers, who are multitasking across modules instead of finishing one; and a cost variance on a physical resource (cloud usage above the planned budget). Three of the four are resource-management problems wearing a schedule costume.

2

Improve the absent developer's productivity while on leave. You cannot motivate someone who is not there, so the levers are handover and load: pair the remaining backend developers with the tasks that only Developer 1 can do; capture the knowledge in the open (design notes, code review, runbooks) so the absence stops being a single point of failure; reassign the tasks that a different role can absorb; and, if the schedule cannot tolerate it, secure short-term contractor capacity as an external acquisition. Cross-training is the structural fix: it converts a person-shaped dependency into a team capability.

3

Prioritise within a fixed budget. Protect the critical path first, then the resources that only one person provides. Concretely: level the QA workload instead of allowing multitasking; move work with float later rather than paying overtime; apply a schedule-based assignment only where the critical path is at risk and a cost-based assignment everywhere else; and treat the cloud spend as a controlled resource — right-size environments, shut down idle capacity, and re-plan the remaining contingency. Delivery scope is the variable that has to be renegotiated, not the budget.

4

Monitor DevOps productivity earlier. Control resources means record, compare with plan, analyse variance before correcting. The measurable version of “struggling with complex deployments” is deployment lead time, change failure rate and the time a specialist spends unblocking others: track them weekly against the plan, and the variance appears in week 2 rather than week 6. Then act on the cause — training, pairing, better automation, or a narrower deployment scope — instead of on the symptom.

5

Answer the deck's closing questions too: resource levelling improves availability and cost efficiency because it removes the peak that forces overtime, emergency hiring and expedited cloud spend; and automation, cross-training and flexible scheduling attack all three failure modes at once — they raise productivity, widen availability and avoid the premium cost of a bottleneck.
