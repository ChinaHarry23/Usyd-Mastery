Foundations: definitions, the constraint system, and the numbers behind it

Week 1 is where the vocabulary is fixed and the constraints are named. This page does three things: it writes the definitions in the form that earns marks, it turns the triple constraint into relationships you can reason with, and it builds the quantitative machinery the unit will use from Week 3 onwards — a WBS effort roll-up, a critical-path forward and backward pass, and a complete earned-value calculation with PV, EV and AC.

Definitions that must be exact

Marks in this unit are lost on definitions more often than on ideas. Write each of these as one sentence, with the consequence included.

Project (PMBOK® Guide, 6th ed., 2017, p. 4)

A temporary effort undertaken to create a unique product, service or result. Four characteristics follow from that sentence: temporary (a clear start and end, not an ongoing operation), unique outcome, specific objectives, and constraints of scope, time, cost and resources.

Project management (PMBOK® Guide, 6th ed., 2017, p. 10)

The application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities to meet project requirements. The lecture's seven key elements are scope, time, cost, quality, risk, communication and resources.

Program and portfolio

A program is a collection of related projects managed in a coordinated way to achieve benefits not possible by managing them separately — the focus is interdependencies, risks and strategic alignment. A portfolio is a collection of programs and projects grouped to meet strategic business goals — the focus is investment decisions, resource allocation and prioritisation. Coordination distinguishes a program; investment distinguishes a portfolio.

Project management framework

A structured approach that defines how projects are planned, executed, monitored and completed, providing guidelines, processes, tools and best practices. Its four core components: the project lifecycle; processes and knowledge areas; tools and templates; and the methodologies within the framework.

1. The three rules of the constraint system

The lecture states them as one-way implications. Set \(S\), \(T\) and \(C\) as the planned scope, time and cost:

\[ \Delta S > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta T > 0 \;\;\text{and/or}\;\; \Delta C > 0 \] \[ \Delta T < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta C > 0 \;\;\text{or}\;\; \Delta S < 0 \;\;\text{or efficiency must rise} \] \[ \Delta C < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta S < 0 \;\;\text{or}\;\; \Delta T > 0 \;\;\text{or}\;\; \Delta Q < 0 \;\;\text{or}\;\; \Delta R > 0 \]

The third rule is the one candidates forget. Reducing the budget does not remove work — it moves the cost to quality or risk, where it is invisible on the triangle and expensive later.

2. A change request is a ledger, not a conversation

Any approved change must balance. With \(\Delta S\) priced as additional effort, the ledger reads:

\[ \underbrace{\Delta C_{\text{work}} + \Delta C_{\text{delay}}}_{\text{what the change costs}} \;=\; \underbrace{\Delta C_{\text{budget}} + \Delta C_{\text{scope removed}} + \Delta C_{\text{quality accepted}}}_{\text{how it is paid for}} \]

If nothing appears on the right-hand side, the change is being paid for with unpaid quality or unrecorded risk — which is exactly what happened to the Aurora Tech CRM.

3. Success is a vector, not a scalar

The lecture names eight or more dimensions of success. Treating them as a vector makes the trade-off visible: a project can be excellent on two components and negative on the one that mattered.

\[ \mathbf{Succ} \;=\; \bigl(\underbrace{\text{time},\ \text{cost},\ \text{scope},\ \text{quality}}_{\text{delivery}},\ \underbrace{\text{value},\ \text{adoption}}_{\text{outcome}},\ \underbrace{\text{team},\ \text{sustainability},\ \text{strategic fit}}_{\text{long horizon}}\bigr) \]

The Aurora Tech CRM scored acceptably on delivery and negatively on adoption and business value. That is why the lecture insists success is no longer just “on time, on budget”.

Why Week 1 shows you this: the triple constraint is not a poster. Index values below 1.00 are the mechanism by which scope pressure turns into time and cost pressure, and the next section works the numbers in full.

Worked example — the Aurora Tech CRM as constraint arithmetic. Baseline: \(T = 6\) months, \(C = \$500{,}000\). Outcome from the case study: extended by 4 months, exceeded the budget by 40%.

1

Cost overrun. \(\Delta C = 0.40 \times \$500{,}000 = \$200{,}000\). Actual cost \(= \$500{,}000 + \$200{,}000 = \$700{,}000\).

2

Cost growth factor. \(\$700{,}000 / \$500{,}000 = 1.40\) — the project spent 1.40 dollars for every planned dollar, the reciprocal of a CPI of \(1/1.40 \approx 0.71\).

3

Schedule overrun. \(\Delta T = 4\) months on a 6-month baseline, so the actual duration is \(10\) months and the schedule growth factor is \(10/6 \approx 1.67\) — a 67% slip, the reciprocal of an SPI of \(0.60\).

4

Who paid. The case study says scope grew without formal change management, so \(\Delta S > 0\). By the first rule, \(\Delta T\) and/or \(\Delta C\) had to rise — and both did. The ledger balanced on the delivery constraints.

5

What did not balance. Adoption was low and trust in IT declined. The extra \(\$200{,}000\) and four months bought a system the users did not want, so the outcome components of \(\mathbf{Succ}\) went negative even though the work was eventually delivered.

6

The one-sentence answer. “Uncontrolled scope growth forced time and cost up by 67% and 40% respectively, and because stakeholders were not engaged during delivery the added spend did not convert into adoption or business value.”

WBS, critical path and earned value

Week 1 shows you the shape of a status report. Weeks 3 and 4 supply the arithmetic that fills it in. These four worked calculations are the quantitative foundation the rest of the unit builds on; each is stated with its assumptions so you can reuse the method rather than the numbers.

1. The WBS roll-up: effort, cost and duration

A work breakdown structure decomposes scope into work packages. Its arithmetic is a sum, and the sum is what makes an estimate defensible. For a work package \(i\) with effort \(e_i\) hours at a blended rate \(r\) dollars per hour:

\[ E \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i, \qquad C_{\text{labour}} \;=\; r \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i \;=\; rE, \qquad D \;=\; \frac{E_{\text{assigned}}}{h_{\text{available per day}}} \]

The 100% rule: the sum of the children's scope must equal the parent's scope — no more (which would be gold-plating) and no less (which would be an omission). The 8–80 rule is the standard textbook guidance for work-package size: smaller than 8 hours and the tracking overhead exceeds the work; larger than 80 hours and progress becomes invisible between reporting periods.

2. The critical path: a forward pass and a backward pass

Activities with durations \(D\) form a network. The forward pass computes the earliest start and finish, the backward pass the latest start and finish, and the difference is the float:

\[ ES_j = \max_{i \prec j}\bigl(EF_i\bigr), \qquad EF_j = ES_j + D_j \] \[ LF_i = \min_{j \succ i}\bigl(LS_j\bigr), \qquad LS_i = LF_i - D_i, \qquad Float_i = LS_i - ES_i \]

The critical path is the chain of activities with zero float. It is the longest path through the network, and therefore the shortest possible project duration. Every activity on it is a schedule commitment.

ActivityDescriptionDuration \(D\)Predecessors

Forward pass from the start: A gives \(ES = 0, EF = 4\). B gives \(ES = 4, EF = 10\). C gives \(ES = 4, EF = 7\). D waits for both predecessors, so \(ES_D = \max(10, 7) = 10\) and \(EF_D = 17\). E gives \(ES = 17, EF = 19\). The project duration is therefore 19 days. The backward pass from \(LF_E = 19\): E gives \(LS = 17\); D gives \(LF = 17, LS = 10\); B gives \(LF = 10, LS = 4\); A gives \(LF = 4, LS = 0\); C gives \(LF = 10, LS = 7\), so \(Float_C = 7 - 4 = 3\) days. Float is 0 for A, B, D and E, so A → B → D → E is the critical path, and C can slip up to three days without moving the end date.

3. Earned value: PV, EV and AC

Earned value compares what was planned with what was earned and what was spent. With \(BAC\) the budget at completion:

\[ PV = \text{planned value}, \qquad EV = \text{earned value}, \qquad AC = \text{actual cost} \] \[ CV = EV - AC, \qquad SV = EV - PV, \qquad CPI = \frac{EV}{AC}, \qquad SPI = \frac{EV}{PV} \] \[ EAC = \frac{BAC}{CPI}, \qquad ETC = EAC - AC, \qquad VAC = BAC - EAC \]

Read the indices rather than the differences: \(CPI = 0.88\) means every dollar buys 88 cents of planned work, and \(EAC = BAC/CPI\) says that if nothing changes, the same inefficiency applies to the whole budget.

4. Cost of quality: prevention is cheaper than failure

The cost of quality splits into conformance cost, which is spent deliberately, and non-conformance cost, which is paid involuntarily:

\[ CoQ \;=\; \underbrace{C_{\text{prevention}} + C_{\text{appraisal}}}_{\text{conformance}} \;+\; \underbrace{C_{\text{internal failure}} + C_{\text{external failure}}}_{\text{non-conformance}} \]

External failure is the most expensive category, because it is discovered by the customer and includes reputational as well as repair cost. Mature organisations shift the mix towards prevention, and the shift is measurable.

The arithmetic of the shift: conformance costs \(24{,}000 + 30{,}000 = \$54{,}000\), non-conformance costs \(26{,}000 + 70{,}000 = \$96{,}000\), and \(CoQ = \$150{,}000\). Non-conformance is \(96{,}000/150{,}000 = 64\%\) of the total. An extra \(\$20{,}000\) in prevention that removes half of the external failures would save \(\$35{,}000\) and leave \(CoQ = \$135{,}000\) — a $15,000 net gain, before counting the reputation that was not damaged.

Worked example — rescheduling after a critical-path delay. The network above is 19 days. Management wants the project finished in 16 days. Work the answer as an examiner would mark it.

1

Identify where the reduction can come from. Only the critical path A → B → D → E controls the duration. Crashing C, which has 3 days of float, changes nothing at all.

2

Convert days to cost. Crashing costs money, and it costs more per day as the compression grows, because the cheapest options are taken first. Suppose the crash cost per day is \$1,500 for B, \$2,200 for D and \$3,000 for E, with a maximum crash of 2 days each.

3

Buy the cheapest days first, re-checking the path every time. Crash B by 2 days: \(B = 4\), duration 17. Crash D by 1 day: \(D = 6\), duration 16. Total crash cost \(= 2 \times 1{,}500 + 1 \times 2{,}200 = \$5{,}200\).

4

Re-run the float after compression. A = 4, B = 4, C = 3, D = 6, E = 2. The A→C→D→E path is now \(4 + 3 + 6 + 2 = 15\) days, so C keeps 1 day of float and does not become critical. No second critical path has appeared.

5

State the trade-off, not just the answer. Three days have been bought for \$5,200 of additional cost, at the price of a compressed schedule that leaves no float on B or D. The triple constraint says the third option is to reduce scope instead — and the answer should say which one was chosen and who approved it.
