What a Project Is

Every later week — scope, time, cost, quality, risk, resources, procurement — is a set of tools for the same object. Get the definition exact and the rest of the unit hangs off it.

Definition (PMBOK® Guide, 6th ed., 2017, p. 4)

A project is “a temporary effort/work undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result.”

The four key characteristics

Temporary

It has a clear start and end point. It is not an ongoing operation, and the end may arrive because the objective was met, because it was abandoned, or because the funding ran out.

Unique outcome

The goal is to produce something new — a product, a service, an event. Even a project that resembles an earlier one carries a different client, context, technology or team.

Specific objectives

Projects aim to meet particular goals and requirements, or to solve a stated problem. “Improve the sales process” is not an objective; a named deliverable with an acceptance test is.

Constraints

Projects work within limitations of scope, time, cost and resources. The constraints are what make the work a management problem rather than a purely technical one.

DimensionProjectOperations (business as usual)
DurationDefined start and end; temporary by designOngoing; continues until the service is retired
OutputA unique product, service or resultRepeated, standardised outputs at a steady rate
Success measureObjectives met, value realised, stakeholders satisfiedEfficiency, throughput, service levels, cost per unit
TeamAssembled for the work, then releasedStable, functional, permanently staffed
ChangeChange is expected and controlled through a change processChange is minimised; process stability is the goal

The six IT project shapes named in the lecture

Test it both ways: if the work has no end date, it is operations. If the output already exists and only has to be produced again, it is operations. Temporary and unique together are what make the management toolset of this unit applicable.
A bank runs a nightly batch reconciliation that must complete before 6 am every business day. Why is this not a project?
Because it costs too little to be managed as a project

Worked example — classify four pieces of IT work. The lecture's first end-of-lecture question is really this one in disguise. Run each item through temporary, unique, specific objectives and constraints.

1

“Migrate 400 mailboxes to the new tenant by 30 June.” Temporary (a stated end date), unique (this tenant, these mailboxes, this cutover), specific objective (400 migrated), constrained (the June date and the licence budget). Verdict: project.

2

“Run the nightly identity reconciliation.” No end date, same output every night, measured by service level rather than by a unique result. Verdict: operation. The project was the work that built it.

3

“Improve the student experience.” Neither temporary nor specific: there is no end point and no acceptance test, so it cannot be estimated, staffed or closed. Verdict: not yet a project. The task is to convert it into one — for example, “replace the enrolment flow so that a student can change units without contacting the faculty, live for the February intake”.

4

“Deliver a new student portal, a timetabling engine and an identity refresh under one manager who tracks their shared dependency on the identity service.” Each item is a project; the coordination of all of them for a shared benefit is a program. If the question had been which investments to fund across the whole IT estate, that would be the portfolio.

5

The sentence that earns the mark. “It is a project because it is temporary — it has a defined start and end — and it produces a unique result against specific objectives within scope, time, cost and resource constraints; ongoing work that repeats the same output is an operation, and needs a different management approach.”

Why IT Projects Are Inherently Difficult

IT projects are technology-focused initiatives aimed at delivering a digital solution, system or service. The lecture's claim is that they operate in environments of uncertainty and change — and that this is a property of the environment, not a failure of the team.

Four sources of uncertainty

  • Requirements evolve as the organisation learns what is possible
  • Outcomes are often intangible and hard to define upfront
  • Integration with existing systems introduces hidden complexity
  • Decisions must be made before all the information is available

What that changes about the job

Successful IT projects depend not only on technical skill, but on professional judgement and the ability to adapt. A manager who waits for certainty before deciding will deliver the decision after it was needed.

This is why the unit spends eight weeks on processes and artefacts rather than on tools: the tools change, the judgement does not.

Project, Program and Portfolio Management

The lecture places project management inside a three-tier progression: Project Management → Program Management → Portfolio Management, representing increasing levels of strategic oversight and coordination across an organisation.

TierUnit of workWho owns itKey objectiveExample from the lecture
Project — tactical executionOne projectProject managerDeliver a successful projectLaunching a new website or developing a mobile app
Program — strategic coordinationA collection of related projectsProgram managerDeliver business value across multiple projectsA digital transformation program or a HealthTech system rollout
Portfolio — enterprise alignmentPrograms and projects grouped strategicallyPortfolio manager or PMOMaximise value and alignment with organisational strategyAn IT portfolio of cybersecurity, cloud, AI and productivity tools; a product portfolio of all R&D and market launches

Why the tiers are not a hierarchy of importance

A program is not “a big project”. Its defining feature is coordination: related projects are managed together because the benefit comes from the relationship between them, not from any one deliverable.

Why a portfolio can contain unrelated work

A portfolio is grouped by strategy and investment, not by shared deliverables. Cybersecurity, cloud migration and a productivity-tool rollout may have nothing technically in common and still belong to one IT portfolio.

The Triple Constraint

The triple constraint names the three factors that define and shape any project: scope, time and cost. They are not independent, and every change request moves at least one of them.

If this happens……the slide's answer isWhat the manager actually has to do
Scope increasesTime and/or cost will usually increaseCost the change, re-baseline, or trade something out to hold the date
Time is reducedCost may increase, scope may decrease, or delivery must become more efficientCrash the schedule, cut scope, or find genuine efficiency — and say which one you chose
Budget is reducedScope may decrease, time may increase, or quality/risk may be affectedDecide explicitly which of the three absorbs the cut and record who accepted it
Why the interior matters: quality and risk usually do not appear on a drawn triangle, which is exactly why they absorb the damage. A budget cut that is “absorbed by the team” is a decision to accept more defects or more risk — and it should be written down as one.

Worked example — the Aurora Tech CRM project. The lecture case study: a custom CRM for the sales department, six-month timeline, $500,000 budget, involving software development, integration with legacy systems and user training. Trace what each failure pattern did to the constraint triangle.

1

Scope came first. Sales leadership requested multiple new features during execution without formal change management. Scope grew, and because no change process existed, nothing was traded out to pay for it.

2

Time absorbed part of it. The project was extended by four months — from six months to ten, a 67% schedule overrun on the original baseline.

3

Cost absorbed the rest. The budget was exceeded by 40%: an overrun of \(0.40 \times \$500{,}000 = \$200{,}000\), giving an actual cost of about $700,000. The Foundations page works this arithmetic through in full.

4

Integration complexity pressed on time, not scope. Repeated delays from legacy-system integration are the classic underestimated-dependency pattern: the work was always in scope, it simply was not in the estimate.

5

Quality and value paid the final bill. Adoption was low and trust in the IT department declined. Both extra months and extra money were spent, and the deliverable still did not deliver value — which is precisely why success is not the same as finishing on time and on budget.

6

The counterfactual in one sentence. A change control process would have forced each new feature to be priced in time or cost, engaged the sales team in testing, and surfaced the legacy-integration risk during planning — turning three of the four causes into recorded, sponsored decisions.

Project Success

Project success is how well a project meets or exceeds its defined objectives, expectations and stakeholder satisfaction across several key dimensions. The lecture's framing is blunt: success is no longer just about “on time, on budget” — it is also about delivering value.

DimensionWhat it asksWhere it is felt
TimeWas the schedule met, or was a slip justified by value added?Delivery commitments and dependent work
CostWas the budget respected, and was the spend efficient?Funding body and business case
ScopeWere the agreed deliverables produced to the agreed standard?Acceptance and sign-off
QualityDo the outcomes meet the required standards?Defects, rework, support load
Stakeholder satisfactionDo the people affected consider the outcome acceptable?Trust, sponsorship, future funding
Business valueDid the investment produce the benefit in the business case?Revenue, cost, risk, capability
Team performanceDid the team grow, or was it consumed?Retention, capability, morale
SustainabilityCan the result be operated and maintained economically?Total cost of ownership
User adoption and satisfactionDo people actually use it, willingly?The lecture case study's decisive failure
Alignment with strategic goalsDoes it move the organisation where it said it was going?Portfolio decisions
Long-term operational impactWhat does it change about how work is done, years later?Operating model
Risk management and flexibilityWere the risks handled, and is the result changeable?Future change requests

Three misconceptions the lecture names

“Finishing on time and budget = success”

Not if the product is not usable or valuable. The Aurora Tech CRM was late and over budget and unused; only the third fact explains why trust in IT declined.

“Meeting business goals is enough”

Ignoring team morale or stakeholder input may cause long-term damage. A project that burns its specialists to hit a date has spent an asset the next project needed.

“All success is immediate”

Some projects show impact only in the long term — especially in IT, R&D and transformation work. Judging a transformation at go-live measures the wrong interval.

A project finishes two weeks early and 5% under budget by dropping the reporting module that operations needed to reconcile month-end. Which statement is best supported by the lecture's view of success?

It is not a straightforward success: delivering less than the stakeholders needed trades a cost win for a value loss
It is a success because reducing scope is always the correct way to protect a schedule

Project Management and Its Knowledge Areas

Project management is “the application of knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to project activities to meet project requirements” (PMBOK® Guide, 6th ed., 2017, p. 10). Those requirements are met through a defined set of areas — one per week of this unit.

The seven key elements named in the lecture

  • Scope — what must the project achieve, and what are its deliverables?
  • Time — how long will it take, and what are the deadlines?
  • Cost — what budget is available, and what resources are needed?
  • Quality — are the outcomes meeting the required standards?
  • Risk — what could go wrong, and how do we mitigate it?
  • Communication — how will information be shared among stakeholders?
  • Resources — what people, tools and technologies are needed?

Why it is critical in IT specifically

IT projects are often complex, high-stakes and directly tied to an organisation's strategic goals. Without proper management they go over budget, miss deadlines, or fail entirely.

The lecture's seven reasons, in order: aligns technology with business goals; delivers value on time and within budget; manages complexity across teams; avoids scope creep and missed deadlines; reduces risks and improves accountability; improves communication; handles change effectively.

The Project Management Framework

A project management framework is a structured approach that defines how projects are planned, executed, monitored and completed. It provides guidelines, processes, tools and best practices to ensure successful delivery.

StageThe question it answersThe artefact that leaves it
InitiatingShould this work exist at all, and who authorises it?Charter and stakeholder register
PlanningWhat exactly will be done, by when, for how much, to what standard?Scope, schedule, cost and quality baselines; risk register
ExecutingIs the work being done, and are the people able to do it?Deliverables, work performance data
Monitoring & controllingAre we where we said we would be, and if not, why?Performance reports, change requests, approved changes
ClosingIs the result accepted, and what should the next project know?Final acceptance, transition, lessons learned

The four core components — and why an organisation bothers

Core components

  • The project lifecycle
  • Processes and knowledge areas
  • Tools and templates
  • Methodologies within the framework

Week 2 supplies the methodologies; every week after that supplies a knowledge area and its tools.

Why use a framework

  • Ensures consistency across projects
  • Reduces risk and increases predictability
  • Improves communication and accountability
  • Supports decision-making with structured processes
  • Aligns with organisational goals and standards

Read the list as benefits to the organisation, not to one project: consistency and alignment are portfolio-level returns.

Week 1 Recap

A project is temporary and unique

Temporary effort to create a unique product, service or result — with specific objectives and constraints of scope, time, cost and resources.

Core definition

IT failure is rarely technical

Volatile requirements, hidden integration complexity, optimism bias, misalignment, weak governance and absent stakeholders — six patterns, none of them a technology problem.

Diagnosis

Three tiers, three objectives

Project delivers the output; program delivers coordinated benefit; portfolio maximises strategic value. Coordination and investment, not size, distinguish them.

Structure

Scope, time, cost — pick two to move

Increase scope and time or cost rises; cut time and cost rises or scope falls; cut budget and scope, time, quality or risk absorbs it.

Constraint

Success includes value

Time, cost, scope and quality are the floor. Adoption, business value, team performance and long-term operational impact decide the verdict.

Judgement

Frameworks buy consistency

Lifecycle, processes and knowledge areas, tools and templates, methodologies — the point is predictability and alignment across projects, not paperwork.
