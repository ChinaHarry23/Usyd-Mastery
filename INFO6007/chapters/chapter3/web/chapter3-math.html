INFO6007 — Week 3 supplement

Foundations: the arithmetic behind a schedule

Week 3 is the first quantitative week of the unit. The examinable material is small and exact: the decomposition rules of the WBS, the forward and backward pass that produce early and late dates, the float formula, and the PERT weighted average with its standard deviation. This page states each one precisely and then works the numbers through, so you can reproduce them under exam conditions.

Definitions that must be exact

Marks in this unit are lost on definitions more often than on arithmetic. Write each of these in one sentence, with the consequence included.

Work breakdown structure

A deliverable-oriented hierarchical decomposition of the work involved in a project that defines the total scope of the project. It is produced by decomposition: high-level deliverables are broken into sub-deliverables, and those into work packages — the lowest level at which work is estimated, assigned and controlled. Every item is documented in the WBS dictionary.

Critical path

The longest sequence of dependent activities in the project — the path that determines the shortest possible project duration. Every activity on it has zero float, so any delay on the critical path delays the entire project.

Float (slack)

The amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the project finish date (or a constrained milestone). It is computed as \(\text{float} = LS - ES = LF - EF\). Zero float defines a critical activity; a small positive float defines a near-critical activity, which a status report must track just as closely.

Schedule baseline

The approved version of the schedule model, used as the reference for reporting progress and measuring variance. It is distinct from the current forecast: a forecast that has drifted is information, whereas a forecast quietly substituted for the baseline makes variance reporting impossible.

Three-point estimate (PERT)

An estimate built from three durations: optimistic \(O\) (shortest time if everything goes well), most likely \(M\) (best guess under normal conditions) and pessimistic \(P\) (longest time if things go wrong). They are combined as \(E = \dfrac{O + 4M + P}{6}\) with spread \(\sigma = \dfrac{P - O}{6}\).

Two rules that carry the whole method. On the forward pass an activity with several predecessors takes the maximum early finish as its early start: it cannot begin until the last prerequisite is done. On the backward pass an activity with several successors takes the minimum late start as its late finish: it must be done before the earliest of them needs to begin. Every CPM error traces back to taking the wrong one of those two.

The critical path, worked end to end

The lecture's Activity-on-Node example gives ten activities. Its real value is that it contains every complication the method has: multiple predecessors, a merge point, a branch that turns out not to be critical, and a near-critical activity that a careless answer would miss.

1

Forward pass. Set \(ES_i = 0\) for every activity with no predecessor, then sweep the network from start to finish:

\[ ES_i = \max_{j \in P(i)} EF_j, \qquad EF_i = ES_i + d_i \]

Merges are where the maximum rule earns its keep. F has predecessors C (\(EF = 14\)) and D (\(EF = 15\)), so \(ES_F = 15\) even though one predecessor finished a day earlier. H has F (\(EF = 18\)) and G (\(EF = 21\)), so \(ES_H = 21\).

2

Project duration. The network's finish date is the largest early finish present:

\[ T = \max_i EF_i = EF_J = 33\ \text{days} \]
3

Backward pass. Set \(LF_i = T\) for every activity with no successor, then sweep from finish to start:

\[ LF_i = \min_{k \,\in\, S(i)} LS_k, \qquad LS_i = LF_i - d_i \]

where \(S(i)\) is the set of successors of \(i\). The minimum rule is what protects the critical chain. G has two successors, H (\(LS = 23\)) and I (\(LS = 22\)), so \(LF_G = \min(23, 22) = 22\) and \(LS_G = 16\): it is I, not H, that constrains G. H has only one successor, J, so \(LF_H = LS_J = 30\) and \(LS_H = 23\). Merges on the backward pass take the minimum for exactly the reason merges on the forward pass take the maximum.

4

Float and the critical path. For every activity, \(F_i = LS_i - ES_i = LF_i - EF_i\). The critical path is the set of activities with \(F_i = 0\), which here is A, B, D, E, I and J:

\[ \text{path} = A \to B \to D \to E \to I \to J, \qquad \sum d = 5 + 4 + 6 + 7 + 8 + 3 = 33 \]

The float column is where the marks are. G has 1 day, H has 2, F has 5 and C has 6 — so G and H are near-critical and belong in the same sentence as the critical path, while C and F are the resources you can borrow from. Note also what the method did not do: it did not declare G critical. G starts on the same day as E and feeds the same successor, but G takes 6 days where E takes 7, so the longer branch wins and G keeps its float.

The exam-safe way to present a CPM answer. State the rule you applied at each merge (“ES is the maximum early finish of the predecessors”), give the table, name the critical path with its summed durations, and finish with the two consequences: which activities have zero float, and which have small float and therefore need watching. An answer that only names a path has answered half the question.
Activity F has predecessors C (EF = 14) and D (EF = 15). What is F's early start on the forward pass?

The lesson is the one people get wrong under exam pressure: the critical path is not the chain with the largest durations, it is the chain with zero float, and a start-to-start lead can move the zero-float point downstream. Seven days of float on the front end is real, but it is not free — it is the only room the plan has, and the vendor's access date, the uncertain legacy data and the two shared specialists are all competing for it.

Three-point estimating: weighted average and spread

PERT replaces a single duration with a distribution. Two numbers come out of it: the expected duration, which goes into the network, and the standard deviation, which tells you how much the answer can move.

The PERT weighted average

With \(O\) the optimistic, \(M\) the most likely and \(P\) the pessimistic duration:

\[ E = \frac{O + 4M + P}{6}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{P - O}{6} \]

The most likely value carries four of the six weights, so the estimate stays anchored to normal conditions; the optimistic and pessimistic values share the remaining two. Because the pessimistic tail is usually further from \(M\) than the optimistic one is, \(E\) normally sits above \(M\) — and that asymmetry is the point of the technique rather than an accident of the arithmetic.

Reading the two numbers

\(E\) is what you put in the network; \(\sigma\) is what you tell the sponsor. Testing has the widest spread in the table (\(2\sigma = 4.7\) weeks) even though its expected duration is shorter than the build's. A schedule that carries point estimates hides exactly this difference.

Where PERT is the wrong tool

For a four-week pilot with strong precedent, three estimates are overhead: analogous estimating with expert judgement gives the same answer faster. PERT earns its cost on uncertain technical work — interfaces, integrations and anything with an unproven supplier.

Summing variances, not deviations. When activities are independent, the variance of a path is the sum of the individual variances, \(\sigma_{\text{path}}^2 = \sum \sigma_i^2\), and the path deviation is its square root. Standard deviations are never added directly. If the two-week uncertainty of testing and the two-week uncertainty of the build share a cause — the same unfamiliar interface — they are not independent, and the sum overstates the confidence. State that caveat; it is usually worth a mark.
A build is estimated at O = 10, M = 12, P = 20 weeks. What are E and \(\sigma\)?
E = 14 weeks, σ = 1.67
E = 13 weeks, σ = 1.67
E = 12 weeks, σ = 0.33

Worked example — from three estimates to a defensible schedule range. A vendor integration is estimated at \(O = 3\), \(M = 6\), \(P = 15\) weeks. The project manager is asked: “can you commit to nine weeks?”

1

Expected duration. \(E = \dfrac{3 + 4(6) + 15}{6} = \dfrac{42}{6} = 7.0\) weeks. Compare that with the plain arithmetic mean, \(\dfrac{3 + 6 + 15}{3} = 8.0\) weeks: PERT's four-fold weight on \(M\) pulls the estimate down here, because the pessimistic value is not far enough from the most likely one to dominate. The direction of the pull depends entirely on the shape of the three numbers, which is why the formula must be applied rather than eyeballed.

2

Spread. \(\sigma = \dfrac{15 - 3}{6} = 2.0\) weeks, so the one-sigma band is 5–9 weeks and the two-sigma band is 3–11 weeks.

3

Interpret the commitment. Nine weeks is exactly \(E + 1\sigma\). Under a normal approximation that is roughly an 84% confidence level — defensible, but it leaves a one-in-six chance of overrun. A nine-week commitment is therefore reasonable if the plan carries contingency and the variance threshold for escalation is set below one week.

4

State the assumptions. The normal approximation is an assumption, not a result; \(O\), \(M\) and \(P\) came from expert judgement, so they carry the estimator's optimism; and the three points are treated as independent draws from one distribution rather than as a mixture of scenarios. Naming these is what separates a defensible answer from a confident one.

Dependency and constraint taxonomy

Most Week 3 exam questions reduce to classification: which dependency type is this, which estimating technique applies, and what does the constraint do to the network? These are the axes the deck gives you.

Axis 1 — the four dependency relationships

TypeReads asEffect on the networkExample
Finish-to-Start (FS)B starts after A finishesThe default; \(ES_B \ge EF_A\)Build the interface, then integration-test it
Start-to-Start (SS)B starts when A startsOverlaps the two; with a lead, \(ES_B = ES_A - \ell\)Write checker guidance as support preparation begins
Finish-to-Finish (FF)B finishes when A finishesCouples the end dates; \(EF_B \ge EF_A\)Testing cannot finish until the build finishes
Start-to-Finish (SF)B finishes when A startsRare; usually a handover constraintLegacy support ends when the new service starts

Axis 2 — mandatory or discretionary, internal or external

Mandatory (hard logic)

  • Inherent in the nature of the work
  • Cannot be removed by decision
  • Example: integration before end-to-end testing
  • Schedule consequence: never the place to look for compression

Discretionary (soft logic)

  • Chosen from best practice, preference or convenience
  • Can be re-decided when the schedule demands it
  • Example: usability testing before rather than alongside the security review
  • Schedule consequence: the first place fast tracking looks

External dependencies rely on work outside the project team's control — police and licence-checker readiness before a live pilot, or a vendor granting access to a payment system. Internal dependencies sit between activities the team controls. The classification matters because it decides what the project manager can actually change: soft logic is negotiable, hard logic is not, and an external date has to be managed by contract and fallback rather than by better planning.

Choosing and defending an estimate

The tutorial work for this week is requirement gathering and stakeholder analysis, and the schedule is where those outputs become commitments. The skill being tested is not arithmetic — it is being able to say what an estimate is based on, and what would invalidate it.

An estimate is four things

The number, its basis, its range, and the assumption that would invalidate it. An answer that gives only the number has given a quarter of an estimate.

Requirements become activities

Every requirement you trace in the RTM has to be satisfied by some activity in the schedule. If it maps to no activity, either the requirement is out of scope or the WBS is incomplete.

Constraints decide feasibility

Fixed dates, limited specialists and external approvals are constraints, not estimates. Negative float is the network reporting that a constraint cannot be met as written.

Say what you would do next

Name the activity you would crash, the dependency you would fast track, the estimate you would re-baseline, and the milestone you would escalate — in that order of preference.

Worked answer — defending the schedule for the HR management system case study. Twelve months, a fixed December deployment, two senior developers, a design team free only after four weeks, and payroll vendor approval that may take up to three weeks.

1

Separate estimates from constraints. Requirements gathering, design, development, testing and training are estimates — they have ranges. The December date, the two developers and the four-week design wait are constraints — they have no range and cannot be estimated away.

2

Model the resource constraint. Two senior developers available at a time means development activities that would otherwise be parallel are serialised. In the network that is a resource-driven dependency: it looks discretionary, but with only two people it behaves like hard logic.

3

Model the external dependency. Payroll vendor approval is external, so it is scheduled with a committed date and a fallback: begin the interface specification in parallel so that only the final integration waits. Then compute the sensitivity rather than assuming the worst — if approval takes five weeks instead of three, integration starts two weeks later, and whether the end date moves depends entirely on how much float integration has.

4

Give the answer in the right shape. “Deployment stays on 1 December provided payroll integration retains at least two weeks of float. It currently has n weeks. If approval slips beyond three weeks, integration becomes critical and I will either fast-track training against testing — a discretionary dependency I can re-decide — or request approval to crash the two critical development activities. If neither recovers the time, the December date requires an approved baseline change, and I will bring that to the steering committee with the forecast attached.”

5

Close the loop with scope. Requirements gathered from HR stakeholders become activities; the requirements traceability matrix is the check that nothing was gathered and then dropped. That is why scope validation gates are also schedule milestones: an unaccepted deliverable cannot be depended on by the activity that follows it.
