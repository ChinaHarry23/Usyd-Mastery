Two Plans Before Any Dates

Week 3 covers the back half of the scope knowledge area and all six processes of project schedule management. Nothing on the schedule can be built until the scope has been decomposed, so the two plans are taught together.

The scope management plan defines how project scope will be defined, validated and controlled. It is what guarantees that all the work — and only that work — is included and completed successfully. The schedule management plan then defines how the schedule will be developed, monitored and controlled: the method, the units, the estimating technique, the baseline approval path and the variance thresholds.

Scope management plan componentWhat it settlesSchedule consequence
Scope statementWhat will be delivered — and explicitly what will notBounds the activities that may ever appear in the schedule
Scope management approachPredictive, agile or hybridPredictive schedules a full baseline; agile schedules a release and sprint horizon
Requirement collectionHow stakeholder needs are gathered and documentedFeeds the activity list; the RTM traces a requirement to the activity that satisfies it
Work breakdown structureHierarchical decomposition of the deliverablesDirect input: work packages become the activities that get sequenced and estimated
Scope validation processHow deliverables are reviewed and approvedAcceptance gates become milestones on the schedule
Scope control processHow change is identified, assessed and managedAn approved scope change re-baselines the schedule, not just the document
Roles and responsibilitiesWho defines, validates and controls scopeDetermines who owns each work package and who approves baseline changes
Assumptions and constraintsConditions taken as given or fixedConstraints (fixed dates, limited specialists) drive the critical path

The schedule management plan answers six questions

ComponentDecision it records
Schedule development approachMethod (Critical Path Method, agile, rolling-wave planning) and tooling (MS Project, Primavera, Jira)
Activity definition and sequencingHow activities are identified from the WBS, and the rules for defining dependencies and constraints
Estimating durationsThe technique (analogous, parametric, three-point/PERT), estimation assumptions and the accuracy range
Schedule baselineHow and when the baseline is set, and the process for approving it
Schedule control and monitoringPerformance metrics (SPI, SV, milestone tracking), update frequency, and the variance thresholds that trigger corrective action
Reporting formatGantt charts, milestone charts or dashboards, and the update frequency for stakeholders
The two baselines are different artefacts. The scope baseline is the approved scope statement plus WBS plus WBS dictionary. The schedule baseline is the approved version of the schedule model — the dates you are measured against. Approving one does not approve the other, which is why a scope change can be signed off while its schedule impact is still being assessed.
A sponsor approves an extra deliverable but says the schedule impact can be worked out later. Which statement is correct?
Both baselines changed, because scope and schedule are the same baseline
The scope baseline changed; the schedule baseline still needs impact analysis and approval
Nothing changed, because an approved change is no longer a change

The Work Breakdown Structure

The WBS is the backbone of project planning: it is the deliverable-oriented grouping of all the work in the project, and it defines the total scope. Everything the schedule later does — sequencing, estimating, resourcing, tracking — hangs off it.

Definition

A work breakdown structure (WBS) is a deliverable-oriented hierarchical decomposition of the work involved in a project that defines the total scope of the project. The technique used to create it is decomposition: breaking high-level deliverables into sub-deliverables, and then into work packages. A work package is the lowest level you plan, estimate, assign and control at.

WBS tree diagram

The visual hierarchy: project at the root, deliverables at level one, sub-deliverables below that, work packages at the leaves. It is a deliverable view — nouns, not verbs.

WBS dictionary

The companion document. For every element it records the identifier, the description of work, the owner, the estimate, the acceptance criteria and the testable output. Each WBS item must appear in it.

Approach to developing the WBSHow it worksUse it when
Using guidelinesThe organisation supplies a standard structure — for example the US Department of Defense publishes WBS guidelinesCompliance or comparability across projects is required
Analogy approachReview the WBS of a similar project and tailor itYou have a close precedent and a short planning window
Top-down approachStart with the largest items of the project and break them downYou understand the whole deliverable but not yet the detail
Bottom-up approachStart with the specific tasks and roll them upDetailed task knowledge exists in the team; the big picture is unclear
Mind-mappingBranches radiate out from a core idea to structure thoughts and ideasYou are exploring scope with people who do not read Gantt charts

Six rules that decide whether a WBS is usable

Structure

  • A unit of work appears in only one place in the WBS.
  • The work content of a WBS item is the sum of the items below it.
  • A WBS item is the responsibility of only one individual, even though many people may work on it.

Practice

  • The WBS must be consistent with the way work will actually be performed.
  • Team members should help build it, for consistency and buy-in.
  • Every item must be documented in the WBS dictionary.
Exam framing: the WBS organises work by deliverable, not by the agencies, teams or departments involved. A tree drawn by org chart is a responsibility structure, not a WBS — and it breaks the “one unit of work in one place” rule as soon as two teams contribute to one product.

Worked example — decomposing one deliverable into schedulable work packages. Deliverable 3 of the case-study project is Licence-data integration. Decompose it, then hand it to the schedule.

1

State the deliverable and its acceptance test. “Licence data held by Transport for NSW is visible in the app with correct status.” The test is a traceable requirement, not a feeling: R-04 to R-06.

2

Decompose into sub-deliverables. 3.1 TfNSW interface · 3.2 Status updates · 3.3 Data validation. Each is a product, not an action — note that they are nouns.

3

Decompose until the leaf is controllable. 3.1 splits into interface specification agreed, interface implemented and interface tested against the TfNSW sandbox. Only now does each leaf have one owner, a duration that can be estimated and a testable output.

4

Check the sum rule. If 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3 are fully described, deliverable 3 is fully described. If anything is missing from them, it is missing from the project — that is the whole point of “the work content of an item is the sum of the items below it”.

5

Hand over to the schedule. Each work package becomes one or more activities with an action verb, an owner, a duration and a predecessor. “Interface implemented” becomes “build the TfNSW interface”, preceded by “agree the interface specification”, and it feeds “integration and security testing”.

Control test: could two different people, given this WBS, estimate and schedule it and agree? If not, the decomposition is not finished — or the dictionary is missing a field.

Sequencing Activities and Drawing the Network

Sequencing determines the logical order of tasks and defines dependencies. Get the dependency types right and the network diagram writes itself; get them wrong and every later calculation is confidently wrong.

Two ways to classify a dependencyTypeMeaningCase-study example
By how binding it isMandatory (hard logic)Inherent in the work and cannot be changedThe app must be built before it can be integrated; integration before end-to-end testing
Discretionary (soft logic)Set by best practice, preference or convenienceChoosing to run usability testing before, rather than alongside, the security review
By where control sitsExternalDepends on work outside the project team's controlPolice and licence-checker readiness before a live pilot; vendor access to the payment system
InternalBetween activities inside the team's controlInterface build before integration testing

Three ways to draw the same network

MethodHow it is drawnTrade-off
Activity on Node (AON)Activities are boxes (nodes); arrows show dependenciesMost widely used today: easier to understand and draw, supported by MS Project and Primavera
Activity on Arrow (AOA)Activities are arrows; nodes are events or milestonesLess common now because of project complexity; the only method that normally needs dummy activities (zero-duration arrows) to express dependencies correctly
Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM)AON that names the dependency type on each link and allows lead and lagRicher and more flexible modelling; supported by modern PM tools

Lead time

The amount of time by which a successor can start before its predecessor finishes. Its purpose is to accelerate the schedule — technically it speeds the schedule up.

Lag time

The amount of time that must pass after a predecessor finishes before the successor can start. Its purpose is to account for delays or waiting periods — technically it slows the schedule down.

The design question the deck poses: a university must replace its online enrolment system before enrolment opens in 14 weeks. Configuring, migrating records, integrating with the payment system, testing and training all have to fit. The payment integration cannot begin until an external vendor provides access in four weeks. The condition of the existing student data is uncertain, and the same two technical specialists are needed for both data migration and integration. The go-live date cannot be postponed. Every one of those sentences is a scheduling constraint: one external dependency, one estimate with a wide range, one resource conflict, and one fixed milestone.
The two-specialist constraint in the enrolment scenario is best handled in which process, and how?
Sequence activities, by converting the dependency into a mandatory one
Develop schedule, by allocating resources and resolving the conflict before the baseline is set
Control schedule, by logging the conflict as a variance and escalating it at the first status meeting

Estimating Activity Durations

Estimating predicts how long each activity will take, considering resource availability, risk and uncertainty. The choice of technique is a trade: speed and cost against accuracy, and every estimate needs a stated basis.

TechniqueHow it worksStrengthsLimits
AnalogousTop-down: predict duration or cost from historical data on similar projects or activitiesFaster and less costly than detailed estimating; works early when information is limited; applies to time and costRequires expert judgement to confirm the similarity is valid
ParametricQuantitative: use a measurable relationship between variables — determine a unit rate and multiply by the number of units requiredUses historical data plus a mathematical model; works well when the relationship is consistent and scalable; applies to time, cost or resourcesFails when the relationship is not actually linear or the unit rate is stale
Three-point / PERTEstimate optimistic (O), most likely (M) and pessimistic (P); combine themRepresents uncertainty and variability instead of a single value; exposes the range to the sponsorThree estimates cost more to produce and can be gamed by widening P
Expert judgementDraw on the experience of people who have done the workFast; captures context a model missesBiased by optimism or by whoever speaks loudest
Three-point estimate (PERT)

Three durations are estimated for each activity: Optimistic (O) — the shortest time if everything goes well; Most Likely (M) — the best guess under normal conditions; Pessimistic (P) — the longest time if things go wrong. The PERT weighted average is \( E = \dfrac{O + 4M + P}{6} \) and the standard deviation is \( \sigma = \dfrac{P - O}{6} \). The full derivation and worked arithmetic are on the Foundations page.

Activity (case study)Illustrative methodDuration rangeBasis
App and API buildBottom-up10–14 weeksWork-package estimates
Integration and security testingThree-point6–10 weeksInterface and defect uncertainty
Dubbo pilotAnalogous + expert4 weeksComparable rollout work
Expanded trials and release readinessRolling-wave8–12 weeksRefined using pilot learning
Why the range matters more than the number. The lecture's schedule management plan sets units (working days and weeks), calls for ranges or three-point estimates for uncertain technical work, and sets variance thresholds for control. A single figure hides all three. An estimate of “6–10 weeks” tells the sponsor what to plan for and tells the control process when to escalate.

Developing the Schedule: The Critical Path

Developing the schedule integrates activity durations, sequences and resources into one complete timeline. The Critical Path Method is the technique that tells you which sequence of activities actually determines the end date.

Critical path

The critical path is the longest sequence of dependent activities in the project — the path that determines the shortest possible project duration. Activities on it have zero float, so any delay on the critical path delays the entire project.

What the method buys you

  • Prioritisation — you know where a day of slippage actually hurts.
  • Visibility of flexibility — the float on every other path.
  • A basis for compression — fast tracking and crashing can only shorten the critical path.

Terms you need exactly

  • Early start / finish (ES, EF) — earliest an activity can begin and end.
  • Late start / finish (LS, LF) — latest without moving the project end date.
  • Float (slack) = LS − ES = LF − EF.

The lecture's Activity-on-Node example has ten activities. Click each box to see how its dates are derived; the arithmetic itself is worked through on the Foundations page.

Worked example — the enrolment system with a lead time (the lecture's discussion scenario, quantified). Five activities, one external dependency and one start-to-start lead.

1

Lay out the network. A Configure the new system (5d) · B Migrate student records (7d, after A) · C Integrate with the payment system (6d, after A, but a vendor provides access only in week 4 — a lag) · D Test end to end (4d, after B and C) · E Train staff (8d, may start 2 days before D finishes — an SS lead, so E starts at D start − 2).

2

Respect the external dependency. C cannot start before the vendor grants access at day 4, so C's ES = max(EF of A = 5, 4) = 5, EF = 11. If the vendor slips by a week the whole downstream chain slips with it — that is what makes it external rather than merely late.

3

Forward pass. A: 0–5. B: 5–12. C: 5–11. D: max(12, 11) = 12 to 16. E: D starts at day 12 and E starts two days before that, so E: 10–18.

4

Identify the critical path and the float. The project duration is 18 days, set by A → C / B → D → E. The critical chain is A → B → D → E, with C carrying 1 day of float (LF 13 against EF 11) — unless the vendor slips past day 5, in which case C becomes critical and the float disappears.

5

State the schedule conclusion. The fixed go-live date is 14 weeks (70 working days at five days a week). At 18 working days the plan has ample room, but the two constraints that can consume it are the vendor's external dependency and the two shared specialists. The defensible answer to the discussion question is therefore: schedule migration and integration sequentially for the same two people, protect the vendor date with a written commitment and a fallback, put a range on the data migration estimate because the condition of the legacy data is uncertain, and hold the go-live milestone as the one date that cannot move.

In the example above, activity C has 1 day of float. What does that mean?
C can be delayed by a day with no consequence at all
C can slip by one day before it starts delaying its successors; beyond that it becomes critical
C is on the critical path because it is the shortest activity

Gantt Charts and the Schedule Baseline

A Gantt chart is the schedule made visible. It is the artefact most stakeholders actually read, which is why the schedule management plan names the reporting format rather than leaving it to taste.

Gantt chart

A bar chart that displays project activities or tasks against time. Each task is a horizontal bar whose length represents its duration. It is used to visualise the project timeline, track progress against the baseline schedule, identify overlaps, leads and lags, and communicate the schedule to stakeholders clearly.

What the baseline is

The approved version of the schedule model. It is the reference against which progress is reported, the thing variance is measured against, and the artefact that must be formally re-approved when the plan genuinely changes.

What it is not

It is not the current forecast. A forecast that has drifted from the baseline is information; a forecast quietly substituted for the baseline destroys the ability to report variance at all.

Reading a Gantt chart critically: the bars show duration and overlap, but they do not show why a bar cannot move. Only the network does that. Two bars that look parallel may be linked by a finish-to-start dependency, and a bar with visible slack may be the one everybody is about to need. Use the Gantt chart to communicate; use the network to decide.

Controlling the Schedule

Controlling the schedule means monitoring progress, identifying variance and taking corrective action. The two compression techniques that appear in every exam answer are fast tracking and crashing — and they are not interchangeable.

TechniqueWhat you doEffect on scheduleCost and risk
Fast trackingPerform activities in parallel that were originally sequentialShortens the scheduleIncreases risk — rework, interface defects and coordination overhead all rise
CrashingAdd more resources to critical-path activities to finish soonerShortens the schedule, but only as far as the critical path allowsIncreases cost directly, and may increase risk; there is a point beyond which more people add nothing
Both techniques have the same blind spot: compressing anything that is not on the critical path shortens nothing. If an activity has float, finishing it earlier just increases its float. This is the single most common error in exam answers on schedule compression.
Control activityWhat it producesCase-study illustration
Update actual progressReliable status on each activityWeekly status from workstream leads
Compare with the baselineSchedule variance and milestones planned versus actualExpanded-trial testing takes longer than planned
Analyse variance and forecastEffect on release readiness, critical-path variance and float, expected finish dateModel the effect on the launch date before promising anything
Approve corrective actionRe-sequenced non-critical work, compression, or an approved baseline changeRe-sequence non-critical onboarding work; escalate any baseline change
Update and communicateRevised schedule and the approved change recordControl the schedule without hiding approved change
Earned value is the quantitative arm of this process. Schedule performance is measured with SPI and SV, which the Week 4 deck develops fully: SPI compares earned value with planned value, and SV is their difference. For now, note where the metric lives — in control schedule, not in the baseline.
The launch date is at risk. Activity H has 9 days of float; activity I has none and is eight days long. Where does crashing help?
Crash H, because it is the longest activity with the most room to absorb extra people
Crash I, because only critical-path activities can move the project end date
Compress both equally, since any reduction in work shortens the schedule

Tutorial-style exercise — the 12-month HR management system. The lecture's case study asks you to reason about dependencies and a slipping vendor. Worked answer.

1

Name the three scope risks. (i) Requirements creep from HR stakeholders during design, addressed by a signed scope statement with explicit exclusions and a formal change control path. (ii) Payroll integration behaviour being discovered late because vendor approval is external, addressed by an early interface specification and a contractual date. (iii) Training and deployment colliding with the December fiscal deadline, addressed by scheduling training as an SS lead against testing rather than after it.

2

Explain how the WBS controls creep. Every proposed addition must land inside an existing work package, or the WBS and its dictionary must be formally changed. Because each package has one owner, an out-of-scope request cannot be absorbed silently — it becomes a visible change request with a schedule impact attached.

3

Design the validation gates. Validate at the end of each phase: requirements signed off before design, design approved before build, tested increments accepted before training, and deployment accepted against agreed criteria. Each gate is a milestone on the schedule, and each produces accepted deliverables or a documented defect or change request.

4

Classify the dependencies. Mandatory: requirements before design, design before development, development before testing, training before deployment. External: vendor approval for payroll integration; availability of the design team, who are free only after four weeks. The four-week design wait is external and fixed, so it must be reflected as a constraint, not absorbed into an estimate.

5

Answer the critical-path question. If vendor approval takes five weeks instead of three, integration starts two weeks later. Whether the project end date moves depends entirely on float: if integration has two or more weeks of float, the critical path is unchanged and only the float is consumed; if it has less, integration becomes critical and the December date is at risk. The correct answer always states the condition rather than asserting a date.
