From Castles to Firewalls

Week 1 opens with an analogy the rest of the unit keeps returning to: the castle. It explains both why security feels obvious and why it keeps failing.

The old security idea, in the deck's words: something valuable is inside — money, data, secrets, reputation. So keep trusted people in and keep attackers out. Build higher walls and make access harder.

Why the castle model works

It gives you a boundary to defend, a list of things worth defending, and a simple success condition: nobody unauthorised gets in. Perimeter firewalls, network segmentation and physical access control are all direct descendants of the wall.

Why it breaks immediately

A perfectly closed castle is secure — and useless. People need to enter, information needs to leave, business needs to happen. The moment we add access, we also create risk. Every door is a control point and an attack surface at the same time.

Castle ideaModern equivalentWhat it buysWhat it misses
High wallsPerimeter firewall, network segmentationKeeps unsolicited traffic away from internal servicesAnything already inside, and any allowed path out
Gate and guardAuthentication and access controlOnly identified, authorised users get inStolen or guessed credentials arrive as a legitimate user
The door you had to addAn exposed service, API or admin consoleMakes the system usefulIt is now reachable by people you have never met
Guards patrolling insideLogging, monitoring, intrusion detectionNotices behaviour that credentials alone cannot justifyOnly useful if somebody reads and acts on the alerts
“Trusted people”Least privilege, separation of duties, insider-threat programmesLimits what any single person can do aloneTrust is an assumption; the deck asks what happens if it is wrong
Two questions the walls cannot answer, straight from the deck: What about someone already inside? and What if we trust the wrong person? Both are failures of authorisation rather than of the perimeter — and both are exactly what the Bybit case later in this page demonstrates.
Today there is no single castle. Data moves through many connected systems, and each connection is simultaneously a capability and a risk. The same request crosses two trust boundaries before it reaches the data store, and the backup path is another way in — or the way back after an incident.
The deck says a perfectly closed castle is secure but useless. What is the security consequence it draws from that?
Security means removing all access until nothing can get in
Adding access creates risk, so risk has to be managed rather than eliminated
Risk only exists once an attacker is already inside the perimeter

Defence in Depth and the Weakest Link

If one control cannot protect the whole system, you add more. Week 1 gives two mental models for that: layers, and a barrel made of uneven boards.

Protect who gets in

  • Identity, authentication, least privilege
  • Segmentation of networks and accounts

Protect what matters

  • Encryption of data in transit and at rest
  • Integrity checks and controlled change

Watch what happens

  • Logging and monitoring
  • Intrusion detection and response

Those five staves are the deck's chain: People → Devices → Networks → Software → Data. Security is end to end, and an attacker only needs one stave to be shorter than the rest. The animated barrel below is that argument, made visible.

Two rules to memorise. No single control protects the whole system — so use layers. A system is only as secure as its weakest point — so spend effort on the shortest board, not on the boards that already look tall. And the deck's fourth take-away: security involves trade-offs, because more security can affect usability and convenience.
A bank has excellent network monitoring, hardened servers and full-disk encryption, but help-desk staff reset passwords after a phone call with no identity verification. Which statement matches the Week 1 argument?
The weakest link sets the practical security level, so the reset process is the priority
Technical controls outweigh process controls, so the reset process does not matter
Defence in depth means every layer is equally likely to be attacked

The CIA Triad: What Are We Protecting?

Week 1 reduces “what are we trying to protect?” to three properties and one question each. Every control you meet for the rest of the unit serves at least one of them.

Confidentiality

Keep secrets secret. Only an authorised user can see a particular piece of data or use a particular service.

Asks: “Who is allowed to see this?”

Integrity

Keep information correct and trustworthy. No one can change it without permission.

Asks: “Has the information been changed?”

Availability

Keep systems working. The service is there when you need it.

Asks: “Can I access it when I need it?”

Definition

The CIA triad is the standard vocabulary for security objectives: confidentiality (only authorised parties can read or use something), integrity (information stays correct and trustworthy, and changes are authorised and detectable) and availability (systems and information are accessible when needed). The mirror image used for attacks is the DAD triad: disclosure, alteration, destruction.

PropertyThe question it asksAttacked by (DAD)Representative controlsWeek 1 example
ConfidentialityWho is allowed to see this?DisclosureAccess control, encryption in transit and at rest, need-to-know, key managementCard details read by a skimmer; an unencrypted database dump
IntegrityHas the information been changed?AlterationDigital signatures and MACs, checksums, append-only logs, dual control, independent verificationA withdrawal amount altered in flight; audit records edited afterwards
AvailabilityCan I access it when I need it?Destruction / denialRedundancy, tested backups, rate limiting, denial-of-service protection, capacity planningRansomware encrypting a server; flooding the public site
Extensions worth naming in an exam answer: confidentiality, integrity and availability are necessary but not sufficient. Security also cares about authenticity (is this really from who it claims?), non-repudiation (can the action be denied later?) and, increasingly, privacy and safety. Also note the trade-off: the three properties pull against each other — aggressive availability (open, replicated access) usually costs confidentiality.
In the Bybit case, the attackers never needed to read the cold wallet's secrets. They got staff to approve a transaction that was not what it appeared to be. Which property was primarily attacked?
Confidentiality, because the cold wallet held valuable secrets
Integrity: the approval and the transaction details were falsified
Availability, because the funds became unavailable to Bybit

Risk: Threat, Vulnerability, Impact

Security is not a binary. Week 1 gives you the vocabulary to say how much risk a system carries, and then to argue about which risk to treat first.

The words that must be exact

Asset — something worth protecting (data, money, reputation, a service people depend on). Threat — a circumstance or actor with the potential to cause harm. Threat actor — the person or group behind it. Vulnerability — a weakness that a threat can exploit. Impact — how much harm results if it succeeds. Risk — the combination of how likely that is and how bad it would be.

The working formula

\[ \text{Risk} \;=\; \text{Threat} \times \text{Vulnerability} \times \text{Impact} \]

Read it as a product, not a sum: if any factor is zero the risk collapses. No threat means nothing to fear; no vulnerability means nothing to exploit; no impact means nothing at stake. Standards such as NIST SP 800-30 express the same idea as a function of likelihood and impact; the Foundations page works the arithmetic, including annualised loss and the cost of a control.

Threat actorWhat they wantWhat they can doWhere Week 1 meets them
SkimmerMoney and card dataPhysical access to the ATM or its card readerATM modelling, Step 5: know your attacker
Shoulder surferA PIN or a passwordObserve a user at the machine or on cameraATM modelling; the case for privacy shields and PIN hygiene

Four ways to treat a risk (standard risk-management vocabulary, and visible in the deck's own incident list): mitigate it with a control, transfer it (insurance), avoid it by not doing the activity, or accept it knowingly. Choosing between them is a business decision informed by the numbers on the Foundations page — not a purely technical one.
A clinic stores patient records on a server that is patched promptly (low vulnerability) and holds data that would be extremely damaging to leak (high impact). A ransomware crew is actively targeting clinics in the same city (high threat). Using the Week 1 formula, what should the risk assessment conclude?
Risk is low, because vulnerability is low and patching is current
Risk is material, so prevention must be paired with detection, tested backups and recovery
Risk is negligible because all three factors must be high at the same time

Modelling the System: Think Like Both Sides

Before you can defend anything you have to say what it is. The deck's five-step ATM exercise is the template for the whole unit's threat modelling.

Think like an attacker

  • What do they want? Money, data, disruption
  • What can they do? Steal a password, trick a user, exploit software, act as an insider
  • Which assumption breaks? Attackers look for assumptions they can break

Think like a defender

  • What are we protecting? People, systems, data
  • What can we do? Prevent → Detect → Recover
  • What is assumed? Every security model starts with assumptions — what if one is wrong?
StepWhat the ATM exercise producesThe question that earns marks
1. Model the systemComponents (ATM, card reader, keypad, cash dispenser, network, bank server, account database); actors (customer, bank, maintenance and cash-replenishment staff); environment (public location, bank network); trust assumptions (server secure, network protected, software correct)What have we assumed, and what changes if the assumption is false?
2. Model intended behaviourBefore: the card is checked and the PIN verified securely. During: the bank approves the withdrawal and the correct amount is released. After: the transaction is recorded and the session ends safelyWhich of these are assumptions rather than guarantees?
3. Think like an attackerBefore: a fake or stolen card, a stolen PIN. During: the withdrawal request is changed, or too much cash is released. After: logs are changed or deleted, or the previous session stays openWhere does the system trust an input it cannot verify?
4. Define what must be protectedConfidentiality: PIN and card details. Integrity: transaction amounts and account balances. Availability: the ATM is there when customers need itWhich property does each control actually serve?
5. Know your attackerSkimmer, shoulder surfer, insider, remote hacker — with different capabilities (physical access, observation, trusted access, network interception) and different goals (money, card data, account access, disruption)Which attacker does this control actually stop, and which does it leave untouched?

Worked example — running the five steps on a different system. System: a university results portal with single sign-on and a staff grade-entry console.

1

Model the system. Components: student browser, identity provider, portal application, grade database, staff console, nightly backup. Actors: students, teaching staff, professional staff, the identity team, an outsourced hosting provider. Environment: public internet for students, campus network for staff. Trust assumptions: the identity provider authenticates correctly; only the unit coordinator can enter grades for that unit; the hosting provider's administrators cannot read the database.

2

Model intended behaviour. Before: the student authenticates and is authorised for their own record only; the coordinator is authorised for their own unit only. During: a grade is submitted, validated against the assessment scheme, and recorded with who changed it and when. After: the student can read the result, the change is auditable, and the session ends.

3

Think like an attacker. A student guesses or phishes a coordinator's credentials; an insecure direct object reference lets a student request somebody else's record; a grade is changed and the audit trail is edited; the console session stays open on a shared machine; the backup is read by someone who should not have it.

4

Define what must be protected. Confidentiality: results are personal information and must not be disclosed. Integrity: a grade must not be changeable without authorisation, and every change must be attributable. Availability: the portal must be usable at results release, when load peaks.

5

Know your attacker. A curious student (low capability, high motivation, inside the trust boundary as a legitimate user); a credential thief (medium capability, targets staff); an insider with console access (high capability, low detectability); an opportunist scanning for exposed services (low effort, broad reach). Different controls address each: object-level authorisation for the first, phishing-resistant MFA for the second, separation of duties and append-only audit logs for the third, patching and monitoring for the fourth.

In the ATM exercise, why does the deck model the system before thinking about attacks?
Because attacks are only meaningful against a stated set of components, actors and trust assumptions
Because auditors require a system diagram before a risk assessment
Because the attacker list cannot be written until the goals are known

Case Study: The $1.4 Billion Mistake

In 2025, thieves took roughly US$1.4 billion from Bybit in a matter of minutes. No cryptography was broken. A screen was falsified and a human approved it.

System model (the deck's Case Study 2)

  • Assets: cold-wallet funds, worth billions of dollars
  • Components: System 1, the UI for transaction approval; System 2, the cold wallet with an aggregate-signature process
  • Actors: Bybit staff approving requests; the cold-wallet system processing signatures
  • Assumptions: the UI shows accurate, verified details; signatures are only given for valid requests; staff follow verification procedures

Threat model

  • Adversary type: a social engineer who knows Bybit's processes
  • Capabilities: create fake approval screens; convince staff to approve malicious transactions
  • Goals: obtain cold-wallet access and transfer the funds
  • Attack vector: fake UI request → staff click approve → the cold wallet signs without independent verification
  • What it exploits: human trust, plus the missing second-layer validation
The three assumptions that failed. The UI was assumed to show the truth; the signer was assumed to verify what it signed; the staff were assumed to follow a procedure that included an independent check. Every one of those is a trust assumption in the Step 1 sense — and the attack did not break any cryptography at all. The technical countermeasure has a name you will meet again in Weeks 5 and 6: what you see is what you sign, with the signed payload displayed by the component that actually signs, and a second, independent approver for high-value actions.

Worked answer — the deck's take-home exercise: state the Bybit system and threat models, then break the chain.

1

System model. Assets: cold-wallet funds (multi-billion USD) and the reputation and solvency of the exchange. Components: an approval UI (System 1) and a cold wallet that aggregates signatures (System 2). Actors: staff who approve requests, and the cold-wallet system that signs. Environment: a high-value transaction workflow operated by humans under time pressure. Assumptions: the UI is accurate and verified; signatures are only produced for valid requests; staff follow the verification procedure.

2

Threat model. Adversary: a social engineer with knowledge of Bybit's internal processes. Capabilities: produce convincing fake approval screens and persuade staff to authorise them. Goal: cold-wallet access, then transfer of funds. Vector: falsified request → human approval → signature without independent verification → transfer. Exploited weaknesses: human trust and the absence of a second validation layer.

3

Locate the failure in CIA terms. Not confidentiality and not availability: the property that failed is integrity — specifically the authenticity of the approval. The system authorised an action the humans did not intend, because the authorisation data was altered before it was trusted.

4

Break the chain with defence in depth. (i) The signing component displays the decoded payload it is about to sign, so a falsified UI cannot hide the real transaction — what you see is what you sign. (ii) High-value transfers require two independent approvers who see the same decoded payload through separate channels. (iii) A destination allow-list and velocity limits bound the loss even when an approval is wrong. (iv) Immutable, out-of-band alerting turns a silent approval into a visible event within seconds, which is what makes recovery possible.

5

Say what it teaches. Every one of the four layers above is ordinary defensive engineering, and each one alone would have changed the outcome. That is the Week 1 argument in one sentence: no single control protects the whole system, and the layer that was missing was a human-protecting control, not a cryptographic one.

Which control would most directly have defeated the Bybit attack as the deck describes it?
Stronger encryption of the transaction traffic
Displaying and independently validating the decoded payload at the signing step
A longer and more complex administrator password

Security Is a Lifecycle, Not a Launch

The deck's second big idea: security does not end when a system is launched. It runs before deployment, after deployment, and after the incident — and then it starts again.

PhaseActivities in the deckTake away 2
Before code deploymentDefine the goals and security properties; follow best coding and implementation practice; run a manual audit that covers human factors, not only the code; search for vulnerabilities by pattern matching (an oracle), with static and dynamic analysisBuild securely: understand what matters → identify threats → test for weaknesses
After code deploymentMonitoring with intrusion detection and prevention; vulnerability search by pattern matching against the live system, mostly dynamic analysis; white-hat hacking that includes decompilation, fuzzing and AI-assisted searchWatch and protect: monitor the system → detect suspicious activity → patch and improve
After an incidentPost-mortem: reflect on what happened and how to prevent it next time. Disaster recovery: freezing money, tracing, insurance, legal stepsRespond, then learn: contain the damage → recover services → investigate → fix and feed the lesson back
The deck's closing line for this idea: “Security is never finished. We build, watch, respond, learn and improve.” In a written answer, name the phase you are talking about — static analysis belongs before deployment, intrusion detection after it, and a post-mortem after the incident. Marks are lost by listing every technique in one undifferentiated paragraph.
A team runs fuzzing and decompilation against its own production service while it is live, and also reviews the source with a static analyser before each release. How does the Week 1 lifecycle classify these?
Static analysis belongs to the pre-deployment phase; the live testing is the post-deployment phase
Both belong to the after-incident phase, because they find real vulnerabilities
Both belong to the after-incident phase, because white-hat hacking is an incident response

Security Controls: Prevent, Detect, Recover

The defender's answer to “what can we do?” is a control. Controls are classified twice: by what they do, and by what kind of thing they are.

Definition

A security control (or countermeasure) is anything that reduces risk by preventing, detecting, correcting or recovering from an unwanted event. The deck's defender question is the shorthand: Prevent → Detect → Recover. Standards add two more functions — deterrent controls that discourage an attempt, and compensating controls that substitute for a control you cannot apply.

FunctionWhat it doesExampleFailure mode if it is the only control
PreventiveStops the event happeningFirewall rule, MFA, access control, input validation, disk encryptionOne bypass and there is nothing left to notice it
DetectiveNotices that it happened, or is happeningIDS/IPS alerts, audit logs, integrity monitoring, alert triageYou learn about the incident from a third party
CorrectiveLimits or reverses the damageAccount lockout, session revocation, patch deployment, quarantineContainment becomes a manual scramble
RecoveryRestores service and dataTested backups, failover, disaster-recovery plan, tracing and fund freezingDowntime and loss become permanent
DeterrentDiscourages an attemptVisible cameras, warning banners, legal agreements, prosecutionDeterrence is only as good as its credibility
CompensatingSubstitutes for a control you cannot applyExtra monitoring where a legacy system cannot be patchedRisk is accepted with eyes open, not removed

By category

  • Technical (logical) — implemented in hardware or software: firewalls, encryption, authentication, logging
  • Administrative (operational) — policy and people: training, procedures, separation of duties, risk assessment, incident plans
  • Physical — the built environment: locks, guards, privacy shields, cable protection, visitor escorts

Choosing a control

A control is only a control if it is attached to a risk. Name the asset, the threat, the vulnerability it addresses and the property (C, I or A) it serves — then say what it costs in usability, money and attention. The Bybit counterfactual in the previous section is a control-selection argument in exactly that form.

Worked example — selecting controls for a small clinic's patient-records system. The clinic runs one server, a booking web app and a shared front-desk account. Run the Week 1 machinery over it.

1

Assets and properties first. Patient records (confidentiality, high), appointment and prescription data (integrity, high), and the booking service itself (availability, high — a clinic that cannot see its schedule still has to treat people). Also: reputation and legal obligations under privacy law.

2

Threats and vulnerabilities. Ransomware crews targeting health providers (high threat, and a sector that cannot tolerate downtime); a shared front-desk account that destroys accountability (vulnerability); an unpatched booking app exposed to the internet (vulnerability); staff who can be phished (vulnerability, human).

3

Preventive layer. Per-user accounts with MFA instead of the shared login; patching and a firewall limited to the ports the booking app actually needs; disk encryption on the server and on any laptop holding records; input validation and parameterised queries in the booking app.

4

Detective and corrective layer. Central logging with an alert on bulk record reads and after-hours logins; integrity monitoring on the record store; account lockout and session revocation as corrective controls; tested offline backups, with one copy that ransomware cannot reach from the network, plus a written one-page recovery procedure.

5

Say what you deliberately did not do, and why. A full security-operations centre is not proportionate for one clinic: it costs more in staff than the loss it prevents, and the alerts would go unread. This is the risk-treatment decision — and being able to state the trade-off is what separates a security argument from a shopping list of products.

A hospital cannot patch a legacy imaging server, so it places the server on an isolated network segment and adds extra monitoring around it. Which classification is most precise?
A purely preventive technical control that removes the vulnerability
A compensating control, supported by detective monitoring, with residual risk accepted
A recovery control, because the hospital can restore the server afterwards

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework

Week 1's lifecycle says what happens when. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework says what an organisation should be able to do at each point — and gives you a vocabulary for arguing about it. Click each marker to see what the function covers.

FunctionThe question it answersExample outcomesWeek 1 link
GovernHow is risk decided, owned and overseen?Risk strategy, roles, policy, supply-chain risk managementRisk treatment decisions; the human and regulatory layer
IdentifyWhat do we have, and what matters?Asset inventory, business context, risk assessmentStep 1: model the system, its actors and its assumptions
ProtectHow do we make success less likely?Access control, training, data security, hardeningDefence in depth; the CIA properties
DetectHow quickly would we know?Continuous monitoring, detection processes, event analysis“Watch what happens”; intrusion detection
RespondWhat do we do when it happens?Response plan, analysis, mitigation, communicationTake away 2: contain, recover, investigate
RecoverHow do we get back, and get better?Recovery planning, improvements, restoration testingPost-mortem and disaster recovery
How to use the framework without over-claiming it. The CSF is voluntary and outcome-based: it describes what an organisation should be able to do, not which product to buy, and it maps onto control catalogues such as NIST SP 800-53. It also defines profiles (a current profile and a target profile — useful for gap analysis) and tiers (1 Partial, 2 Risk Informed, 3 Repeatable, 4 Adaptive) that describe how rigorous and repeatable the risk practice is. Say that, rather than pretending it is a certification.

Week 1 Recap

The castle became a network

Walls and gates assumed one boundary. Modern systems have many connections, and every connection is both a capability and a risk.

Framing

Layers, and the shortest board

No single control protects the whole system, and a system is only as secure as its weakest point: People, Devices, Networks, Software, Data.

Defence in depth

CIA, and risk as a product

Confidentiality, integrity, availability; risk = threat × vulnerability × impact. Threat actors differ in goal and capability.

Vocabulary

Model, then cycle

Model the system before the attack; model intended behaviour before the failure. Then build, watch, respond, learn — and map it to the NIST CSF functions.

Method

Check yourself

Can you state the CIA questions, write the risk formula, run the five modelling steps on a system of your own, place six controls against the lifecycle, and name the six CSF functions?
