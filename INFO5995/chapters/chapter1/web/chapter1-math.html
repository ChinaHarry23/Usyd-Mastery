Foundations: the arithmetic behind security decisions

Week 1 is mostly vocabulary, but every claim in it can be made quantitative: risk as a product, loss as an annualised expectation, password strength as entropy, detection as conditional probability, and defence in depth as a product over layers. This page pins down the definitions that must be exact, then works the numbers you can be asked to reproduce. The formulas are standard security and NIST material, not lecture notation — the deck argues in prose, and these equations are the revision aid that makes the argument checkable.

Definitions and notation

Marks are lost on definitions more often than on ideas. Each of these should be writable in one sentence with the consequence included.

Asset, threat, vulnerability, impact, risk

An asset is something with value that needs protection. A threat is a circumstance or actor with the potential to cause harm; a threat actor is the agent behind it. A vulnerability is a weakness a threat can exploit. Impact is how much harm results if it does. Risk is the combination of how likely that is with how bad it would be.

Exposure, attack surface, control

Exposure is the state of being open to a threat (an internet-facing service is exposed; a patched, segmented one is less so). The attack surface is the sum of the ways an attacker can interact with the system — every port, account, input field, API, human process and supply chain. A control (countermeasure) is anything that reduces risk: preventive, detective, corrective, recovery, deterrent or compensating. The deck's shorthand is Prevent → Detect → Recover.

Why “risk = threat × vulnerability × impact” is a product

Each factor is a gate. No threat means nothing to fear; no vulnerability means nothing to exploit; no impact means nothing at stake. That is why multiplying is the right shape for the qualitative model, and why a single zero should stop you writing a risk report.

The CIA triad, stated precisely

The deck gives each property a question. For a written answer, add the condition that makes the property testable.

1. Confidentiality is an authorisation property of reads

A subject \(s\) may read an object \(o\) only if it is authorised for that object:

\[ \text{read}(s,o) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{auth}(s,o) \]

The deck's question — “who is allowed to see this?” — is the left-hand side of the implication. Encryption is how you keep the property while the data is in transit or at rest; access control is how you keep it while the system is running.

2. Integrity is a property of writes

A change taking object \(o\) to \(o'\) is permitted only if it is authorised and remains attributable:

\[ o \to o' \;\Rightarrow\; \text{authorised}(o \to o') \;\wedge\; \text{logged}(o \to o', \text{who}, \text{when}) \]

Two consequences follow, and both appear in the Bybit case. First, integrity is not only about preventing changes: it requires being able to detect them, which is what signatures, MACs and append-only logs provide (Weeks 4–5). Second, “authorised” has to mean the authoriser saw what they were authorising — the what-you-see-is-what-you-sign requirement.

3. Availability is a ratio of uptime to total time

With \(\mathrm{MTBF}\) the mean time between failures and \(\mathrm{MTTR}\) the mean time to repair:

\[ A \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{MTBF}}{\mathrm{MTBF} + \mathrm{MTTR}} \]

Worked example: \(\mathrm{MTBF} = 2000\) hours and \(\mathrm{MTTR} = 4\) hours give \(A = 2000/2004 = 0.9980\), that is \(99.80\%\) availability, which is about \(17.5\) hours of downtime a year (\(0.002 \times 8760\)). Halving the repair time to 2 hours raises availability to \(99.90\%\) and halves the downtime — which is why detection and response speed are availability controls, not just security theatre.

Do not add the three properties into a single score and call it security. A weighted sum \(S = w_C C + w_I I + w_A A\) is a decision aid for comparing options, and the weights have to come from a risk assessment for a named asset. Two systems with the same \(S\) can have completely different failure modes — one leaks, one silently corrupts — and averages hide exactly the property that matters.

Risk arithmetic

Two models, one idea: risk is a quantity you can compare, budget against and reduce. The qualitative model ranks; the quantitative model prices.

1. Qualitative: risk as a product of three factors

\[ R \;=\; T \times V \times I, \qquad T, V, I \in \{1, \dots, 5\} \]

Standards such as NIST SP 800-30 write risk as a function of the likelihood of the event and its impact, \(R = f(L, I)\), with \(L\) itself driven by threat and vulnerability. The three-factor form is the practical version used in workshops: score each factor, multiply, sort the list, argue about the top of the list.

2. Quantitative: single loss and annualised loss

Let \(\mathrm{AV}\) be asset value, \(\mathrm{EF}\) the fraction lost in one event, and \(\mathrm{ARO}\) the expected number of events per year:

\[ \mathrm{SLE} \;=\; \mathrm{AV} \times \mathrm{EF}, \qquad \mathrm{ALE} \;=\; \mathrm{ARO} \times \mathrm{SLE} \]

\(\mathrm{ALE}\) is the number a budget conversation needs. It is also a place to be honest about uncertainty: \(\mathrm{ARO}\) is an estimate, and events are rarely independent.

3. Worked example: is the control worth buying?

A clinic's records system has \(\mathrm{AV} = \$50{,}000\) (hardware, records reconstruction, notification cost and lost appointments). Ransomware would destroy or lock about \(40\%\) of that in one event, and the clinic estimates \(0.5\) events per year at current defences.

\[ \mathrm{SLE} = 50{,}000 \times 0.4 = \$20{,}000, \qquad \mathrm{ALE} = 0.5 \times 20{,}000 = \$10{,}000\ \text{per year} \]

A backup and recovery programme costing \(\$3{,}000\) per year reduces the exposure factor to \(0.1\):

\[ \mathrm{ALE}_{\text{after}} = 0.5 \times (50{,}000 \times 0.1) = \$2{,}500, \qquad \text{saving} = 10{,}000 - 2{,}500 = \$7{,}500 \gt 3{,}000 \]

The control is justified on the numbers alone, and the residual \(\mathrm{ALE}\) of \(\$2{,}500\) is the risk the clinic still carries. If the same control had saved only \(\$2{,}000\) a year, the arithmetic would say no — and a regulatory obligation or a reputational argument would then have to be made explicitly rather than smuggled in as “best practice”.

Name the asset and its value. Member contact data (personal information, privacy obligations, low direct cash value); payment integrity (the provider holds the cards, so the club's exposure is to fraud disputes and chargebacks, say \(\mathrm{AV} = \$10{,}000\)); the site itself (availability matters during the enrolment window).

2

Score the top three risks with \(T \times V \times I\). (a) Automated scanning and exploitation of an out-of-date plugin: \(T = 4\) (opportunistic, constant), \(V = 4\) (unpatched, internet-facing), \(I = 3\) (defacement, data exposure) \(\Rightarrow 48\). 3

Price the top two. For (a): if the site is defaced and must be rebuilt, \(\mathrm{EF} = 0.3\) of a \(\$10{,}000\) exposure gives \(\mathrm{SLE} = \$3{,}000\), and \(\mathrm{ARO} = 0.4\) gives \(\mathrm{ALE} = \$1{,}200\). Automatic plugin updates plus a staging check (about 4 hours of volunteer time, call it \(\$400\)): reduces both \(V\) and the \(\mathrm{ARO}\), plausibly cutting each \(\mathrm{ALE}\) by \(40\%\) — a saving near \(\$960\) for \(\$400\) spent. Two-factor authentication on the admin account (free, one hour to configure) attacks (b) directly and is the highest value per dollar on the list.

5

State the residual risk and the decision. After both controls, roughly \(\$1{,}000\) a year of expected loss remains. The club accepts it, records the number, and reviews it if the site starts holding payment data directly — at which point the impact changes and the whole calculation has to be redone.

Entropy, keyspace and guessing cost

Confidentiality eventually rests on a secret that cannot be guessed. This is the one piece of mathematics that underpins every credential, key and token in the unit, and it is also the origin of the “is this password strong enough?” argument.

1. Entropy measures how much an attacker does not know

For a random variable \(X\) taking values with probabilities \(p_i\), the Shannon entropy in bits is:

\[ H(X) \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log_2 p_i \]

If the value is uniform over \(N\) equally likely possibilities the sum collapses to \(H = \log_2 N\): one bit per halving of the search space, so a 4-digit PIN carries \(\log_2 10^4 \approx 13.3\) bits.

2. Keyspace for a password of length \(L\) over an alphabet of size \(N\)

\[ H \;=\; L \log_2 N \quad \text{(only if every character is independent and uniform)} \]

The bracket is the whole difficulty of password strength. Human-chosen passwords are neither uniform nor independent, which is why breach lists, dictionary rules and pattern masks beat the \(L \log_2 N\) bound by orders of magnitude. A randomly generated password or passphrase does meet the assumption.

3. Guessing cost, and why length beats complexity

If an attacker can test \(r\) guesses per second, a full search of a space of \(2^{H}\) possibilities takes

\[ t_{\text{worst}} \;=\; \frac{2^{H}}{r}, \qquad t_{\text{expected}} \;\approx\; \frac{2^{H-1}}{r} \]

Doubling the length of a random password squares the keyspace and therefore doubles \(H\); adding one character class multiplies the alphabet by a much smaller factor. That is why a 12-character random password beats an 8-character one with symbols. Two defences change \(r\) rather than \(H\): rate limiting and account lockout reduce attempts per second, and a deliberately slow password hash (bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2) multiplies the real cost of each guess. The unit returns to hashes and key sizes in Weeks 4 and 5.

Reading the last table honestly. The RSA and AES rows are a forward reference, not Week 1 content: a symmetric key of \(n\) bits gives about \(n\) bits of security against exhaustive search, an RSA modulus of 2048 bits gives roughly 112 bits of security because the best known attack is factoring rather than enumeration, and a hash function with an \(n\)-bit output gives only about \(n/2\) bits against collisions by the birthday bound. Those equivalences are how NIST SP 800-57 compares algorithms, and they are why “longer key” and “stronger algorithm” are different claims.

Worked answer — is the help desk's reset policy strong enough? The help desk issues temporary passwords of 8 random lowercase letters, valid for 24 hours, with no rate limit on the login page. Assume an attacker can attempt \(10^{9}\) guesses per second against a stolen hash, and \(10\) guesses per second against the live login form.

1

Entropy of the temporary password. \(H = 8 \log_2 26 = 37.6\) bits, so the space is about \(2.1 \times 10^{11}\) candidates.

2

Against the live form. At \(r = 10\) guesses per second the expected search is \(2^{36.6}/10 \approx 1.0 \times 10^{10}\) seconds — about 320 years. The live login is not the weak point, and the 24-hour validity makes the attack hopeless.

3

Against a stolen hash. At \(r = 10^{9}\) guesses per second the same space falls in about \(2^{36.6}/10^{9} \approx 100\) seconds. The password policy is fine only while the hash stays secret — which is not an assumption a security argument may rest on.

4

The fix is two-sided. Raise \(H\) (12 characters, or a five-word passphrase at about 64.6 bits) and raise the per-guess cost with a slow hash and a rate limit, so that a leaked database no longer converts into a working credential within a coffee break. Then add the policy layers: one-time use, forced change on first login, and no reuse of the previous password.

5

Say which control addresses which risk. Entropy addresses offline cracking; rate limiting addresses online guessing; expiry limits the value of any single credential that leaks; MFA makes a guessed password insufficient on its own. Four controls, four different risks — which is exactly what defence in depth means in practice.

Detection arithmetic and layer probability

The deck puts monitoring and intrusion detection in the after-deployment phase. Two pieces of mathematics decide whether that monitoring is useful: the base rate on the day, and whether your layers fail independently.

1. A detector is described by two conditional probabilities

\[ \mathrm{Se} \;=\; \Pr(\text{alert} \mid \text{attack}), \qquad \mathrm{Sp} \;=\; \Pr(\text{no alert} \mid \text{no attack}) \]

with the false-positive rate \(\mathrm{FPR} = 1 - \mathrm{Sp}\). Vendors quote these. The number an analyst experiences is different: the probability that an alert is real,

\[ \mathrm{PPV} \;=\; \Pr(\text{attack} \mid \text{alert}) \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{Se}\,\pi}{\mathrm{Se}\,\pi \;+\; (1-\mathrm{Sp})(1-\pi)} \]

where \(\pi\) is the base rate: the prior probability that a given event is a genuine attack. This is Bayes' theorem, and it is the single most useful formula on this page.

2. Worked example: a good detector with a terrible queue

Take \(\mathrm{Se} = 0.90\) and \(\mathrm{Sp} = 0.99\) — respectable numbers — with a base rate of \(\pi = 10^{-4}\), meaning one in ten thousand events is a real attack.

\[ \mathrm{PPV} = \frac{0.90 \times 10^{-4}}{0.90 \times 10^{-4} + 0.01 \times 0.9999} = \frac{9.0 \times 10^{-5}}{1.009 \times 10^{-2}} \approx 0.0089 \]

Under \(1\%\) of alerts are real: about \(99.1\%\) are false positives. At a million events a day the queue is \(10^4\) false alerts plus roughly \(90\) true ones — about \(10{,}090\) alerts a day, or one every \(8.6\) seconds. This is why the deck treats monitoring as a process with people in it, and why Week 2 insists that every AI-assisted finding is verified. Alert fatigue is not a cultural problem; it is arithmetic.

3. Three levers that raise precision

(i) Raise the base rate by narrowing the scope — monitor authentication to internet-facing services rather than all traffic. (ii) Raise specificity — better rules and models, at the cost of sensitivity. (iii) Correlate independent signals, because the posterior of the first test becomes the prior of the second: \(\pi_2 = \mathrm{PPV}_1\), so two conditional checks can lift precision by orders of magnitude. That third lever is what a SIEM is actually for, and it is the same “layer” idea as defence in depth.

4. Defence in depth is a product — if the layers are independent

Let \(q_i\) be the probability that layer \(i\) stops a given attack, and \(p_i = 1 - q_i\) the probability it is bypassed. If the attacker must defeat every layer and the layers fail independently, the attack succeeds only when all of them fail:

\[ P(\text{success}) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} (1 - q_i) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i \]

Four layers each stopping \(70\%\) give \(0.3^4 = 0.0081\), under \(1\%\). But if the attacker only needs one way in — the wooden barrel in the Week 1 deck — the model becomes

\[ P(\text{success}) \;=\; \max_i p_i \]

and improving every layer except the weakest changes nothing at all. The two models are not in conflict: the product applies when all layers must be traversed, the maximum applies when the attacker can choose a path. Say which one you are using, because the difference is the whole argument.

The independence assumption is where defence-in-depth arguments go wrong. If one phishing email defeats both the awareness training and the mail filter, then \(P(\text{both fail}) \ne p_1 p_2\); the failures are correlated and the product is far too optimistic. Practical consequence: prefer layers that fail for different reasons — a technical control and a procedural control, or two controls operated by different teams — and say out loud which single event could defeat several of them at once.
A detection rule catches \(99\%\) of real attacks and has a \(2\%\) false-positive rate. In a system where 1 event in 1000 is a real attack, roughly what fraction of alerts are real?

Worked answer — should the security team buy the new detector, or hire a second analyst? Current state: \(10^6\) events a day, \(\pi = 10^{-4}\), existing detector \(\mathrm{Se} = 0.85\), \(\mathrm{Sp} = 0.995\). One analyst can triage about \(120\) alerts per shift.

Current alert load. False positives: \(10^6 \times 0.005 = 5{,}000\) per day. True positives: \(10^6 \times 10^{-4} \times 0.85 = 85\). Total \(5{,}085\) alerts a day against a capacity of \(120\) — the queue is failed by a factor of \(42\), and precision is \(85/5085 \approx 1.7\%\).

2

The proposed detector. \(\mathrm{Se} = 0.95\), \(\mathrm{Sp} = 0.9995\). False positives fall to \(10^6 \times 0.0005 = 500\) per day; true positives rise to \(95\). Load \(595\) alerts, precision \(16\%\). Better on every axis — and still five times the analyst's capacity.

3

Narrow the scope instead of buying sensitivity. Restrict monitoring to authentication events on internet-facing services: suppose that reduces the event population to \(2 \times 10^4\) a day while retaining \(80\) of the \(95\) true positives. The base rate rises to \(\pi = 80/20{,}000 = 4 \times 10^{-3}\), and the same detector now yields false positives \(2 \times 10^4 \times 0.0005 = 10\) and precision \(80/90 \approx 89\%\). Ninety alerts a day is a queue one analyst can actually work.

4

Decide with the numbers, then state the trade-off. Scoping beats sensitivity here, because precision is dominated by the base rate rather than by \(\mathrm{Se}\). The cost is coverage: events outside the scope are no longer watched, so the residual risk is the attack that never touches an authentication endpoint. That risk is accepted knowingly, mitigated by a lower-priority daily review of the remaining events.

5

Connect it back to defence in depth. The scoped detector is one layer with \(q \approx 0.9\) for the attacks it sees. Adding a second, differently-shaped layer — say integrity monitoring on the record store — multiplies the failure probability only if the two fail independently, and takes over exactly where the first layer is blind.

The modelling checklist

Most Week 1 exam questions reduce to “model this system, then say what you would do”. Run the steps in order and the justification writes itself.
