Foundations: the numbers behind applied cryptography

Week 6 is a design week, but every design decision in it rests on arithmetic you can be asked to reproduce: how much entropy a password actually has, what “112-bit security” means across symmetric and asymmetric primitives, what a password-hashing cost parameter buys you, how two authentication factors combine, and how many bits an HMAC tag really needs. This page collects those relationships in one place.

Entropy and guesswork

Everything in this chapter starts from one quantity: how many equally likely secrets an attacker would have to try. The deck's warning is that hashing raises the cost of guessing — so you need to be able to say how many guesses there are.

1. Keyspace and entropy

If a secret is chosen uniformly from a set of size \(N\), the number of candidates is \(N\) and the entropy is its base-2 logarithm:

\[ H = \log_2 N \quad\text{bits}, \qquad N = 2^{H} \]

For an \(L\)-character password drawn uniformly from an alphabet of \(R\) distinct characters:

\[ N = R^{L}, \qquad H = L\,\log_2 R \]

Entropy measures the choice, not the string. A 12-character password that a user built from their dog's name and a birth year is not a uniform draw from a \(62^{12}\) keyspace, and its effective entropy is far lower than the formula suggests.

2. Guesswork: average versus worst case

An attacker who works through a keyspace without repetition expects to find a uniformly random secret about halfway through:

\[ G_{\text{avg}} \approx \frac{N}{2} = 2^{\,H-1}, \qquad G_{\text{worst}} = N = 2^{H} \]

In bits this is why one bit of entropy is worth one bit of security: doubling the keyspace doubles the work, and the average case simply loses one bit to the factor of two.

Worked example — how long is a 40-bit secret worth opposing?

Harbour University configures Argon2id so that one guess costs \(W = 1\ \text{ms}\) on the attacker's hardware. A secret with \(H = 40\) bits has \(N = 2^{40} \approx 1.10\times10^{12}\) candidates.

\[ \text{expected guesses} = \frac{N}{2} \approx 5.50\times10^{11} \] \[ t = 5.50\times10^{11} \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s} \approx 5.50\times10^{8}\ \text{s} \approx 17.4\ \text{years} \]

Now raise the cost by a factor of ten — \(W = 10\ \text{ms}\), still entirely acceptable at login — and the same secret takes about 174 years. Or leave \(W\) alone and add 10 bits of entropy instead: \(N\) grows by \(2^{10} \approx 10^{3}\), so the time grows by the same factor. Two independent levers, one formula.

The two levers are not equivalent. Raising the cost factor multiplies every guess and is bounded by how much latency a login can tolerate. Raising entropy multiplies the number of guesses and is bounded only by whether humans will accept the result. The deck's insistence on password managers and passkeys is really an argument for the second lever: let a machine generate and store the entropy.

Security strength and equivalent key sizes

“128-bit encryption” and “128-bit security” are not the same claim. The brief's requirement is that you can move between security levels and key sizes across every primitive in the deck.

3. Security strength is the work factor of the best known attack

A primitive has security strength \(b\) bits when the best known attack needs on the order of \(2^{b}\) operations. Three consequences follow, and they are the ones exam questions turn on:

\[ b_{\text{exhaustive}}(k) = k \qquad b_{\text{collision}}(n) \approx \frac{n}{2} \qquad b_{\text{factor}}(k) \approx \left(\frac{64}{9}\right)^{1/3} k^{1/3}\,(\log k)^{2/3} \]

A \(k\)-bit symmetric key resists exhaustive search at \(2^{k}\). An \(n\)-bit hash resists collisions only at about \(2^{n/2}\), by the birthday bound. And an RSA modulus of \(k\) bits has no known attack near \(2^{k}\) — the number field sieve is far cheaper, which is why RSA keys must be enormous to reach the same strength as an AES key.

Birthday bound

In a space of \(n\)-bit outputs there are \(2^{n}\) values, so after roughly \(\sqrt{2^{n}} = 2^{n/2}\) hashes a collision is likely. This is why a hash used for collision resistance needs an output twice the target strength, and why SHA-256 is quoted as 128-bit collision resistance rather than 256-bit.

Password hashing cost and the verifier

The deck lists Argon2id, scrypt, bcrypt and PBKDF2 as the acceptable password-hashing functions, "configured to be costly". This section makes that phrase quantitative.

4. Work per guess, and what a cost parameter buys

A memory-hard KDF with \(m\) blocks of memory, \(t\) iterations and \(p\) parallel lanes does work proportional to its memory-time product:

\[ W \;\propto\; m \cdot t \cdot p \]

For PBKDF2 the analogous quantity is the iteration count alone, \(W \propto c \cdot H(\cdot)\). Time to exhaust a fraction \(f\) of a keyspace of size \(N\):

\[ t_{\text{attack}} \;=\; f \cdot N \cdot W, \qquad t_{\text{attack, avg}} \;=\; \frac{N \cdot W}{2} \]

Doubling \(m\) or \(t\) doubles the defender's login cost and the attacker's per-guess cost. That symmetry is the whole design: the defender pays once per login, the attacker pays once per guess.

What the server stores

The stored record is the derived verifier plus the salt plus the algorithm parameters. All three are needed at login, because the server must repeat exactly the same computation:

\[ v = \mathrm{KDF}(\text{password}, \text{salt}, m, t, p), \qquad \text{login: } \mathrm{KDF}(\text{candidate}, \text{salt}, m, t, p) \stackrel{?}{=} v \]

Missing parameters mean an unverifiable account after a migration. That is a real operational failure mode, not a theoretical one: rotate the parameters and you must re-derive on the next successful login, because the plaintext is not available to re-hash.

What the salt does and does not do

A unique per-account salt defeats precomputed tables and stops two users with the same password from sharing a verifier. It does not slow a single guess at all, and it does not help if the attacker is targeting one account rather than the whole database. The salt removes a shortcut; the cost parameter sets the price of a direct guess.

Factors, false accepts and lockouts

The deck's counting rule — factors count separately only when each is independently verified — is a statement about probability, and it is worth being able to write down.

5. Independent factors multiply; dependent factors do not

If factor 1 is defeated with probability \(p_1\) and factor 2 with probability \(p_2\), then for independent factors:

\[ p_{\text{bypass}} = p_1 \, p_2 \]

If the two are verified through the same channel — one phishing page that collects the password and relays the one-time code in real time — then a single compromise defeats both and the best you can claim is the weaker of the two:

\[ p_{\text{bypass}} \approx \max(p_1, p_2) \]

With \(p_1 = p_2 = 0.01\), independence gives \(10^{-4}\) and dependence gives \(10^{-2}\): a hundredfold difference produced purely by how the factors are verified, not by which factors they are.

6. False accepts, false rejects and the operating point

A biometric or behavioural matcher returns a similarity score, and a threshold turns it into a decision. Two error rates follow, and they move in opposite directions as the threshold changes:

\[ \mathrm{FAR}(\tau) = \Pr[\text{accept} \mid \text{impostor}], \qquad \mathrm{FRR}(\tau) = \Pr[\text{reject} \mid \text{genuine}] \]

Tighten the threshold and FAR falls while FRR rises; loosen it and the reverse happens. The two curves cross at the crossover error rate (CER), which is a convenient single-number summary of a matcher. Choosing where to sit on the curve is a policy decision about which error is worse for the use case — unlocking a phone is not the same risk as authorising a payroll change.

Worked example — lockout as a rate limit

An attacker is allowed \(A\) password attempts per account per day against a keyspace of \(N\) uniformly random candidates. Modelling attempts as independent draws without replacement, the probability that at least one attempt succeeds is:

\[ p_{\text{success}} = 1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\right)^{A} \approx \frac{A}{N} \quad \text{for } A \ll N \]

With \(N = 2^{40}\) and \(A = 10\), \(p_{\text{success}} \approx 10 / 1.10\times10^{12} \approx 9.1\times10^{-12}\) per account per day.

Now apply the same \(A = 10\) across \(U = 50{,}000\) accounts — the pattern the deck calls password spraying, chosen precisely because it stays under per-account lockout. If each account had an independent \(10^{-3}\) chance (a weak password), the expected number of compromised accounts is:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{compromised}] = U \cdot p_{\text{success}} = 50{,}000 \times 10^{-3} = 50 \ \text{accounts} \]

The lesson the arithmetic teaches: per-account lockout does not bound a horizontal attack. You need rate limiting and anomaly detection that measure attempts across the whole population, which is why the deck pairs lockouts with monitoring rather than treating either as sufficient.

Worked answer — rating a proposed login policy with the arithmetic. The proposal: minimum 8 characters, no MFA for students, lockout after 5 failed attempts per account, and a help desk that resets any account on name plus date of birth.

1

Bound the keyspace. An 8-character human-chosen password is not \(95^{8}\). If it is a dictionary word with substitutions, the effective keyspace is more like \(10^{4}\)–\(10^{6}\) candidates. Assume optimistically \(2^{30}\).

2

Price the offline attack. With \(W = 1\ \text{ms}\), the average attack is \(2^{29} \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s} \approx 6.2\ \text{days}\) per account on a single core, and that parallelises trivially. The hashing cost is doing real work, but it cannot rescue a 30-bit secret.

3

Price the online attack and its workaround. Five attempts per account gives \(p \approx 5/2^{30} \approx 4.7\times10^{-9}\) per account. Across 50,000 accounts that is \(2.3\times10^{-4}\) expected compromises per day from a single pass — small, but repeated daily and combined with a leaked verifier it is not the binding constraint.

4

Find the cheapest path, which is not cryptographic. Name plus date of birth is not a secret; both are widely knowable. The recovery path therefore has a bypass probability far higher than either the password or the lockout, and by the rule \(p_{\text{bypass}} \approx \max(p_i)\) it sets the security of the whole design.

5

State the fix order, justified by the numbers. (1) Close the recovery path, because it dominates. (2) Raise the entropy floor with a password manager or passphrase, because \(W\) alone cannot fix 30 bits. (3) Add MFA for students, which multiplies rather than adds when the channel is independent. (4) Move monitoring to the population level, since per-account lockout does not bound spraying.

HMAC and tag length

Week 6 assumes the Week 5 construction. Two properties are worth having exactly: how HMAC is built, and how many bits of its output you actually need.

7. The HMAC construction

With a hash \(H\), block size \(B\) bytes and a key \(K\) padded to \(B\) bytes:

\[ \mathrm{HMAC}(K, M) = H\bigl((K \oplus \mathrm{opad}) \,\|\, H((K \oplus \mathrm{ipad}) \,\|\, M)\bigr) \]

The nested form is what makes HMAC safe with a Merkle–Damgård hash: the outer hash binds the inner digest to the key again, so the length-extension property of \(H(K \| M)\) cannot be exploited to forge a tag.

8. How long should a tag be?

For a MAC with an \(n\)-bit output and an \(n\)-bit key, forgery resistance is about:

\[ b_{\text{mac}} \approx \min(n,\; n_{\text{key}}) \quad\text{against forgery, versus}\quad b_{\text{coll}} \approx \frac{n}{2} \quad\text{against collisions} \]

This is why a 128-bit truncated HMAC-SHA-256 tag is a sound choice: an attacker forging a tag must find a specific value, not any collision, so there is no birthday advantage and the full 128 bits of work are required. Truncating below about 96 bits is where online forgery attempts start to matter in practice.

HMAC does not give freshness. A recorded \((M, T)\) pair still verifies when replayed, because nothing about it is wrong. Replay protection needs a nonce, a timestamp or a sequence number — the same mechanism that TLS records use, and the reason a captured handshake message cannot simply be re-sent.

The TLS 1.3 key schedule

The study page walks the handshake message by message. This section states what the messages are for: turning one shared secret into several short-lived keys, each labelled for exactly one purpose.

9. One secret in, several labelled keys out

TLS 1.3 uses HKDF, a two-step extract-then-expand construction. The shared secret is extracted with a salt, then expanded with an info label into each key it needs:

\[ \mathrm{PRK} = \mathrm{HMAC}(\text{salt}, \text{IKM}), \qquad \mathrm{OKM} = \mathrm{HKDF\text{-}Expand}(\mathrm{PRK}, \text{info}, L) \]

The schedule produces a separate secret per epoch — early, handshake, application — and within each epoch a separate key per direction and per purpose. Two properties follow, and both are examinable:

  • Compartmentalisation. Compromising one traffic secret does not reveal another, because each is derived under a distinct label rather than sliced out of one long key.
  • Forward secrecy. Handshake keys are discarded once application keys exist; the ephemeral key exchange means a later compromise of the server's certificate key does not decrypt recorded sessions.

10. Record protection: AEAD, nonce and sequence number

Each record is sealed with authenticated encryption using a per-record nonce and an implicit sequence number:

\[ C = \mathrm{AEAD\text{-}Seal}\bigl(k,\; \mathrm{nonce} \,\|\, \mathrm{seq},\; \text{plaintext},\; \text{AAD}\bigr) \]

Because the sequence number is an input to the tag, a reordered, replayed or truncated record fails verification rather than being silently accepted. This is the practical answer to the replay gap that a bare HMAC leaves open.

Worked answer — sizing the crypto in one page for a design review. You are asked to justify every key length and cost parameter in a new student portal. Produce the numbers.

1

State the target strength. Student records with a multi-decade retention requirement point at 128-bit strength as the floor and 192–256 bits where a long confidentiality lifetime is claimed. Write the number down before choosing anything.

2

Translate into primitives. AES-128 or AES-256 for data at rest; SHA-256 or SHA-384 for integrity, remembering that collision resistance is half the output length; a 256-bit elliptic curve or a 3072-bit RSA modulus for signatures and key transport.

3

Set the KDF cost. Pick \(W\) so a legitimate login stays under a tolerable latency, then quote the offline attack time for a stated entropy assumption. At \(W = 1\ \text{ms}\), a 40-bit secret is about 17 years of average attack; state that number rather than the word “strong”.

4

Check the components that are usually missed. Certificate chain and trust store, backup encryption with an independent key, log integrity, the session token's signing key, and the recovery path's identity evidence. Each needs a length, an owner and a rotation date.

5

Write the weaknesses down. Any component below the target strength, every place a shared secret is stored, and the one path most likely to be attacked — usually recovery, because it is a human process. A design review that names its own weakest link is worth more than one that claims none exists.
