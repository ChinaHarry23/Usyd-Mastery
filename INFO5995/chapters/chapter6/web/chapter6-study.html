If No Encryption Was Broken, Where Did the Attack Happen?

Week 6 opens with a running case study. Harbour University runs one connected system — a web portal, single sign-on, a marks database and a cloud platform — serving roughly 50,000 students and staff. The week's incident is the question the rest of the deck answers.

The timeline in the deck. At 8:03 a.m. a staff login succeeds with a valid password. By 8:17 a.m. 8,000 student records — names, marks, student IDs and contact details — have been accessed. The deck's own verdict: the data was encrypted, but the attacker logged in as an authorised user.

What did not fail

Encryption in transit (HTTPS) and encryption at rest both worked as designed. No cipher was broken and no key was cracked. Every control the deck drew on the diagram — TLS, SSO, the encrypted database — was operating.

What did fail

The system could not tell which human was holding a valid credential. Once the attacker held a working password, the session was legitimate from the application's point of view, and the application served plaintext because that is what applications must do.

The pivot of the whole week. Weeks 4–5 taught you to authenticate a message or a peer: MAC/HMAC, digital signatures, certificates and TLS. Week 6 moves up a layer and asks the same question about a user and a session — password risk, MFA and passkeys, recovery and monitoring. The key sentence from the deck: encryption can protect the channel; it cannot prove the right human is using the account.

Exam framing: the deck's four pause-and-think questions are the skeleton of a good answer. Identity — who logged in? Access — what could they reach? Data — where did it travel? Cloud — who owned each control? Answer an incident question with those four, in that order.

System-Level Authentication

A password demonstrates knowledge of a secret. It does not prove the legitimate owner is present — and that gap is where the Harbour University incident lives.

Three questions, three decisions

Identification — who do you claim to be? Alice enters alice@harbour.edu.au; the portal now knows which account is being claimed. Authentication — can you prove the claim? Alice's passkey response is verified. Authorisation — what are you permitted to do? Policy allows Alice to view /marks/1042 and not /marks/1043. The order is always claim identity → verify evidence → evaluate permission.

Passwords are reusable, shareable and phishable. The deck's framing is therefore not “passwords are bad” but “one kind of evidence is a single point of failure”. Alice must supply different kinds of evidence, and those kinds are the classic factor categories.

FactorEvidenceDeck exampleCharacteristic weakness
Something she knowsA secret held in memoryPasswordReusable, shareable, phishable
Something she hasA device or key she controlsRegistered phoneCan be lost, stolen or exhausted by push fatigue
Something she isA biometric traitFingerprintProbabilistic match; hard to replace after compromise
The counting rule the deck stresses: factors count separately only when each is independently verified. Password + PIN is two steps but one factor category. Two passwords, two TOTP apps on the same phone, or a password plus a push to the same device are all one category wearing two hats.
The five principles of strong authentication — (1) identity is never absolute, systems build confidence from evidence not certainty; (2) passwords alone are not enough; (3) independent factors are stronger; (4) authentication is continuous, because device, location and behaviour change risk after login; (5) recovery must be secure, because a weak fallback bypasses the strongest primary method. Authentication is about building confidence that the right person is accessing the right system at the right time.
A portal requires a password followed by a PIN that the user also memorised. How should this be described?
Two-factor authentication, because two separate secrets are required
Two authentication steps in one factor category, so the independent-evidence property is not met
Three-factor authentication, because the PIN is verified by a different server component

Worked example — explaining the breach to the Harbour University council. The council asks: “we encrypted everything, so how did this happen?” Build the answer in four moves.

1

Separate the layers. Name what each control does. TLS protects data in transit between student device and portal. Database encryption protects data at rest. Neither control is asked to answer “is this the legitimate Alice?”

2

Name the failed decision. Authentication is the decision that failed. The credential was genuine, so the authentication step succeeded at the only question it was asked: is this a valid secret for this account? The right person was never established.

3

Explain why the data was readable. A valid session receives plaintext because the application has to use the data. Encryption at rest is unwrapped by the application before it builds the response page. That is not a bug; it is how the stack works.

4

Locate the real control gap. Either authorisation was too broad (the session could reach 8,000 records it did not need) or detection was too slow (fourteen minutes of bulk access with no alert). Both are Week 6 answers; neither is a cryptography answer.

Choosing Authentication Methods by Threat

The deck's rule is to choose by threat, not fashion. Each method proves something different and fails in a different way.

MethodWhat it provesMain limitationBest use
PasswordSomething you knowCan be stolen, reused or phishedGeneral compatibility; baseline authentication
Authenticator app (TOTP)Something you haveCodes can still be phished in real timeStronger than passwords; widely supported
Push approvalSomething you haveVulnerable to push fatigue if users approve unexpected requestsGood usability; enable number matching where available
Security key / passkeyPublic-key credential stored on the user's deviceRequires compatible devices and servicesPreferred for phishing-resistant and high-risk authentication

Push fatigue

Repeated prompts pressure a user into approving an attacker's session. The attack does not defeat MFA; it recruits the user. Countermeasures: number matching, rate limits on prompts, and treating an unexpected prompt as a reportable event.

Biometrics are identifiers, not secrets

A sensor captures a sample and compares it against a protected template, so the result is probabilistic rather than an exact string comparison. You cannot reissue a fingerprint. Design consequences: liveness checks, secure hardware, retry limits, and a carefully protected fallback.

Passkeys change what the server stores

The device creates and protects a private key; the portal stores only the matching public key — never a reusable password. The private key is not sent to the university. The result is origin-bound authentication that resists credential stuffing and many phishing attacks, because the signature is bound to the site's origin and cannot be replayed to a look-alike domain.

Adaptive authentication: the same login carries different risk. 9:00 a.m. on a known laptop on the Sydney campus is lower risk; 3:14 a.m. from a new browser in an unusual country is higher risk. Adaptive authentication can require stronger evidence or restrict sensitive actions when the context changes — and it needs monitoring to know that the context changed.
Recovery is an authentication path. The deck's 2:13 a.m. scenario, and the help-desk path behind it: “I lost my phone” → weak identity check (name plus date of birth) → account reset → attacker takes control. Recovery is often the weakest authentication path in the system, which is exactly why principle 5 exists.
Why does the lecture insist that a password should be hashed rather than encrypted before storage?
Hashing is faster, so login stays responsive under load
Because encryption is reversible, so the decryption key becomes a single point of failure for every stored password
Because a hash makes even a weak password computationally impossible to guess

Authorisation: What May You Do?

Everyone logged in successfully. Now the second question: should a tutor access marks for students outside their classes? Should every administrator be able to view student grades?

Authentication answered “who are you?”. Authorisation asks “what may you do?” The deck's five-role ladder for one system — student, tutor, lecturer, unit coordinator, administrator — is a ladder of scope, not of status. The rule attached to it: scope should match responsibility, not status, and higher roles do not automatically inherit permission to read every record.

Least privilege limits the blast radius

Compare a tutor role that can view all students, export all marks, change enrolments and manage users with one that can only view the assigned class and enter assigned marks, with no bulk export. Grant the minimum scope, actions and duration required for the task. The reason is stated in the deck: if the tutor account is compromised, the attacker reaches fewer records and fewer actions.

Separation of duties

No single person should control the entire process. The lecturer proposes marks, the coordinator reviews and approves, the system publishes results. Separating critical actions reduces the risk of fraud, mistakes and unilateral misuse.

Broken object-level authorisation

A logged-in student with ID 1042 requests GET /marks/1043. The server asks whether student 1042 may view record 1043 and denies. Changing an ID in a URL is a broken authorisation control, not a failed authentication.

Access follows the person

Join: assign the role and minimum permissions. Move: remove old permissions, grant only the new role's access. Review: verify current permissions are still required. Leave: disable the account, revoke sessions, remove access.

Say the right layer out loud. In incident reports and exams alike, “the IDOR let them read another student's marks” is an authorisation finding. “They logged in with a stolen password” is an authentication finding. Scoring the wrong layer loses the marks even when the observation is correct.
An attacker authenticates with a valid staff credential, then requests other students' mark records by editing the record ID in the URL. What has failed?
Authentication, because the attacker should not have been able to log in at all
Authorisation, specifically object-level authorisation — the server never checked whether this subject may access that object
Encryption, because record IDs should have been encrypted in the URLs

Data Security Through the Lifecycle

Harbour University holds marks, student IDs, addresses, medical adjustments, research data, payroll and financial records. Not all of it has the same value, sensitivity or impact if compromised.

ClassificationDeck exampleWho may access it
PublicCourse handbookApproved for anyone
InternalStaff meeting notesUniversity workforce
ConfidentialStudent marksAuthorised academic roles
RestrictedPayroll • medical recordsTightly limited, high-impact access

Classify data before deciding who may access it, how it is protected and how long it is retained. The three states then follow from the classification, and each state has its own controls.

1

In transit

TLS, authenticated channels, VPN where appropriate — student device to cloud portal over HTTPS.

2

At rest

Database, disk and object-storage encryption — the encrypted database behind the portal.

3

In use

Application permissions, isolation, minimisation and monitoring — the state where the data is plaintext.

The sentence the case study is built to teach: the database was encrypted, the records were still exposed. Protection at rest worked; the failure was a valid login plus wrong permissions, and the application returned readable records. Encryption protects particular data states. It does not override application authorisation.

Minimisation

The deck's admission form: name, student ID and email are required; passport copy, driver licence and extra personal details are questioned. Ask before collecting — is it required for the stated purpose, is there a less sensitive alternative, how long must it be retained, and who will be able to access it? Less stored data means less data to expose, misuse, retain and delete.

Accountability

Every important action should leave evidence in five parts: time (09:02:14), actor (Alice, 1042), action (viewed marks), resource (student 1042) and context (Sydney, 203.x.x.x). Logs create accountability only when they are protected, monitored and reviewed — an unread log is a cost, not a control.

An encrypted database is breached and records are read through a valid application session. Which statement is correct?
Encryption at rest failed, because a well-configured store would never return plaintext
Protection at rest worked as designed; the exposure came from the permissions attached to a legitimate session
Minimisation failed, because the records should never have been stored in the first place

The Secure Channel: TLS, Certificates and Message Authentication

Everything the case study describes travels over HTTPS. TLS is the applied end of Weeks 4–5: it takes key exchange, certificates, AEAD and message authentication and makes them work on every page load. This section is the bridge from the deck's diagram to the protocol behind it.

TLS 1.3 messageCarried in clear or encrypted?What it establishes
ClientHello (+ key_share, ALPN, SNI)ClearClient random, supported versions, cipher suites, a guessed key share, the server name
ServerHello (+ key_share)ClearChosen version and cipher suite, the server's key share — both sides can now derive handshake keys
EncryptedExtensions, Certificate, CertificateVerify, FinishedEncryptedServer parameters, the certificate chain, a signature over the transcript proving private-key possession
Client FinishedEncryptedClient's transcript check; handshake keys are then discarded
Application dataEncryptedPayload under AEAD with per-record nonces and sequence numbers

What the certificate chain proves

A leaf certificate binds a public key to a name. It is valid only if an intermediate CA signed it and a root CA that the client already trusts signed the intermediate. Verification checks the signature chain, the validity window, the name, and revocation status. Remove any link and the chain proves nothing.

What the handshake deliberately hides

Because almost everything after ServerHello is encrypted, a network observer learns the server name (SNI) and the packet sizes and timings, and little else. That is a feature: the certificate, the negotiated extensions and the application traffic are all inside the encrypted envelope.

Where this fits Week 6: the handshake authenticates the server to the client, and optionally the client to the server by certificate. It does not authenticate Alice the human. A stolen password travels happily inside a perfectly valid TLS 1.3 tunnel — which is exactly why the case study's incident cannot be fixed with more encryption.
In a TLS 1.3 handshake, an observer on the network can read which of the following directly from the wire?
The server's certificate and public key, because certificates must be publicly verifiable
The server name in the ClientHello and the cipher suites the client offers
The plaintext of the first HTTP request, because application keys are derived later

Availability: An Attacked Pillar

Strong encryption and correct permissions still fail if students and staff cannot reach the service. Availability is the pillar that can be attacked without anyone reading or changing a single record.

What breaks availabilityMechanismPrimary control
DoS / DDoSOne source, or many distributed devices, overwhelm the service togetherRate limiting, scrubbing and elastic capacity
RansomwareProduction files are encrypted or systems disruptedIsolated, immutable backups and tested restores
Resource exhaustionCPU, memory, APIs or storage consumedQuotas, autoscaling and load balancing
FailureHardware or software stops workingRedundancy; no single point of failure
Human errorAccidental deletion or a bad changeChange control, versioning, protected copies
Dependency outageA cloud or third-party service failsDependency mapping and graceful degradation

The distinction the deck draws: DoS and DDoS have the same goal — make the service unavailable — and differ only in scale. Did the attacker need to read or change data? No. Availability alone can be attacked, and availability failures are not always data breaches — but they are still security failures.

Availability = prevent disruption + recover quickly. Four resilience controls (redundancy, load balancing, rate limiting, monitoring) sit in front of the four recovery steps (ransomware, isolated backup, restore and verify, continue operations). Preparation is for hardware failure, ransomware, human error, and regional or provider outage — and backups are useful only when restores are tested and recovery objectives are understood.
A university takes nightly backups to the same cloud account that hosts production, and has never test-restored them. What is the most accurate assessment?
Availability is covered, because backups exist and run on a schedule
The backup is not yet a recovery control: it is not isolated from the production account and its restore path has never been verified
Confidentiality is covered, because the backup copy is encrypted

Cloud Security and Key Management

Harbour University runs its portal, records, payroll and research services in the cloud. That changes both the opportunities and the risks, and it moves identity to the centre of the control plane.

Opportunities

  • Remote access from anywhere
  • Elastic resources instead of fixed capacity
  • Resilient architectures across zones
  • Faster deployment, shorter procurement

New risks

  • Internet exposure of services that were internal
  • Shared responsibility across a moving boundary
  • Configuration mistakes with enormous blast radius
  • Identity attacks replacing network-perimeter attacks
ResponsibilityCloud provider — security of the cloudHarbour University — security in the cloud
Physical and platformBuildings and physical security, hardware and storage infrastructure, core networking and hypervisor, provider-managed service components, physical hardware failures—
Identity and access—Student and staff identities, authentication and authorisation, customer-controlled configuration
Applications and data—Applications and data, backups, retention and recovery
The question to ask after an incident: if payroll leaks, ask which control failed — not merely “provider or customer?”. The exact boundary changes across IaaS, PaaS, SaaS and the service contract, so the useful answer names a control and an owner.
Key lifecycle in a cloud deployment

Encrypting every copy is necessary but not sufficient. Each key has a lifecycle: generate in a managed service or hardware module, distribute only to the components that need it, use under an access policy, rotate on a schedule or after an incident, revoke so that compromised material becomes useless, and destroy under dual control. Rotation is what makes the difference between one exposed key and one exposed dataset.

An administrator changes an object-storage setting and a dataset becomes readable by anyone on the internet. Who is responsible?
The cloud provider, because the storage service is theirs to secure
The university, because customer-controlled configuration — including storage policy — is security in the cloud
Both parties equally, because the boundary is always shared 50/50

Week 6 Recap

Secure systems are built in layers: identity → permissions → data protection → secure cloud operations. The incident is only mysterious if you expect one layer to do another layer's job.

Encryption is not authentication

TLS protects the channel; encryption at rest protects stored data. Neither proves that the legitimate owner is present. That is the whole incident in one sentence.

Core claim

Identify → authenticate → authorise

Three questions, three decisions: who do you claim to be, can you prove it, and what may you do? Naming the wrong layer loses the marks.

Model

Factors are counted by category

Know, have, are — and each category must be independently verified. Password + PIN is two steps and one factor.

MFA

Least privilege and separation of duties

Minimum scope, actions and duration; no single person controls the whole process. Scope should match responsibility, not status.

Authorisation

Encrypt every state and every copy

In transit, at rest, in use — and the recovery copy too. Then manage the keys, the access policy and the restore procedure.

Data

Identity is the modern control plane

In the cloud, requests are evaluated by identity and context rather than by network location. Configuration and monitoring decide how much damage one account can do.

Cloud

Worked example — auditing a cloud migration against the deck's activity. Harbour University is migrating to the cloud. Work through the four headings the deck's activity uses and name what is missing, who owns it, and what evidence would prove it works.

1

Authentication. Password only, or password plus MFA, or passkeys? The decision that matters is whether the strongest available method is required for privileged roles. Owner: identity team. Evidence: enrolment rate for phishing-resistant factors, and the proportion of privileged accounts covered.

2

Authorisation. Are roles defined, is least privilege applied, and is privileged access time-boxed? The deck's question is whether a compromised privileged account can alter infrastructure affecting thousands of users. Owner: application and platform owners. Evidence: an access review record showing each role's permissions still match its responsibilities.

3

Data. Is every dataset classified, is every copy encrypted, and is retention justified? Owner: data owners per classification. Evidence: a register that maps each dataset to a class, an encryption state and a retention period — including the backup copy.

4

Operations. Is monitoring alerting on impossible travel, bulk download, repeated failures and privilege changes? Are backups isolated and tested? Owner: cloud operations. Evidence: an alert that fired in a game-day exercise, plus a dated restore test with a measured recovery time.

5

The answer that earns the marks. “Cloud security is continuous, not a one-time configuration: identity first, secure configuration with drift detection, least privilege, continuous monitoring, and recovery and resilience.” Name the five principles, then attach an owner and a piece of evidence to each.

Worked example — rating an authentication design for a student portal. The proposal: passwords for everyone, TOTP for staff, push approval for administrators, and a help desk that resets any account after confirming the caller's name and date of birth.

1

Match method to threat, not to fashion. Passwords for all 50,000 users buys compatibility and nothing else. TOTP for staff is a genuine second category and a real improvement, but codes can be phished in real time.

2

Look for the category error. Push approval for administrators is the highest-privilege role on the weakest second factor: push fatigue targets exactly the population with the most to lose. The deck's answer is a security key or passkey for high-risk authentication, with number matching at minimum.

3

Attack the recovery path. Name plus date of birth is weak identity evidence — both are widely known. As written, this help-desk path bypasses every stronger method above it, which is principle 5 in the negative. Replace it with out-of-band verification against enrolment records, and log every reset as a security event.

4

Add continuity. Authentication is continuous: a new browser at 3:14 a.m. from an unusual country should require stronger evidence or restrict sensitive actions. That requires monitoring, which the proposal does not mention.

5

Verdict and fix order. The design is a reasonable first step and a poor finished state. Fix order: (1) close the recovery hole, (2) move administrators to phishing-resistant credentials, (3) add adaptive risk checks and monitoring, (4) reduce reliance on passwords for everyone else.

Final framing: identity → permissions → data protection → secure cloud operations. Week 6 is the week where cryptography stops being the answer and becomes one control among several.
