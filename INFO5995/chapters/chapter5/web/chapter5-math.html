Foundations: the arithmetic behind public-key cryptography

Week 4 ended with confidentiality; Week 5 adds integrity, authenticity and public verification, and the primitives that provide them are built on two hard problems. This page pins down the modular arithmetic behind RSA and Diffie-Hellman, the equations you should be able to write from memory, the real sizes and bounds from the reference standards, and the notation you need in order to read a certificate.

Modular arithmetic and the one-way gap

Every public-key primitive in this unit is a computation that is cheap in one direction and believed to be infeasible in the other. That asymmetry is the whole idea, and it lives in arithmetic modulo a large number.

Notation

\(a \bmod n\) is the remainder of \(a\) on division by \(n\). Two integers are congruent modulo \(n\), written \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\), when \(n\) divides their difference. The integers modulo \(n\) form a set written \(\mathbb{Z}_n\); the values coprime to \(n\) form a group of size \(\varphi(n)\), Euler's totient.

1. Modular inverse and the totient

A value \(a\) has a multiplicative inverse modulo \(n\) exactly when it is coprime to \(n\), and that inverse is what the extended Euclidean algorithm computes:

\[ a\,a^{-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{n} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \gcd(a,n) = 1 \]

For an RSA modulus \(n = pq\) with distinct primes, counting the values that are not coprime to \(n\) gives

\[ \varphi(n) \;=\; (p-1)(q-1) \]

Practical consequence: anyone who learns \(p\) and \(q\) can compute \(\varphi(n)\), then \(d\), and then read every message. The security of RSA is the secrecy of the factorisation, not the secrecy of the algorithm.

2. Fermat and Euler

For a prime \(p\) and any \(a\) not divisible by \(p\), and more generally for any \(a\) coprime to \(n\):

\[ a^{\,p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}, \qquad a^{\,\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n} \]

This is what makes exponentiation a permutation of \(\mathbb{Z}_n^{*}\): the exponents wrap around with period \(\varphi(n)\), which is exactly why \(e d \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}\) lets decryption undo encryption.

3. Easy forward, hard backward

Raising to a power modulo \(n\) is efficient by repeated squaring, at a cost of about \(O(\log x)\) modular multiplications:

\[ y \;=\; g^{\,x} \bmod p \qquad \text{(easy)} \qquad\qquad x \;=\; \log_g y \bmod p \qquad \text{(hard)} \]

The backward problem is the discrete logarithm problem. Its sibling is integer factorisation: computing \(n = p q\) is easy, and recovering \(p\) and \(q\) from \(n\) is not. Diffie-Hellman rests on the first, RSA on the second.

Why does the modular exponentiation \(y = g^{x} \bmod p\) work as a one-way function?
Because the values g and p must be kept secret for it to be hard
Because computing y is cheap with repeated squaring, while recovering x is the discrete logarithm problem
Because the operation is impossible to invert even for a tiny modulus like p = 23

RSA: key generation, encryption and signatures

RSA is one equation pair with a key-generation recipe attached. Learn the recipe, the two equations, and the reason the arithmetic is correct — then the sizes and the padding rules.

Key generation

\[ n = p\,q, \qquad \varphi(n) = (p-1)(q-1), \qquad e\,d \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)} \]

Public key \((n, e)\); private key \((n, d)\); \(p\), \(q\) and \(\varphi(n)\) destroyed or stored as secret material. NIST FIPS 186-5 requires a modulus of at least 2048 bits, primes \(p\) and \(q\) of equal bit length, and a public exponent in the range \(2^{16} < e < 2^{256}\) (typically \(e = 65537\)).

Encryption and decryption

\[ c \;=\; m^{\,e} \bmod n, \qquad\qquad m \;=\; c^{\,d} \bmod n \]

Correctness follows from Euler's theorem. Since \(ed = 1 + k\varphi(n)\) for some integer \(k\),

\[ m^{\,ed} \;=\; m^{\,1 + k\varphi(n)} \;=\; m \cdot \left(m^{\varphi(n)}\right)^{k} \;\equiv\; m \pmod{n} \]

This holds for messages coprime to \(n\); the Chinese Remainder Theorem covers the remaining cases. The practical lesson is the same either way: the exponent pair is what ties the two keys together.

Signatures reverse the roles of the keys

\[ s \;=\; H(m)^{\,d} \bmod n, \qquad\qquad s^{\,e} \bmod n \;\overset{?}{=}\; H(m) \]

Anyone can verify with \((n,e)\); only the holder of \(d\) can produce \(s\). Two warnings that the standards and the lecture both make: a signature key pair shall not be reused for encryption or key establishment, and raw textbook RSA must never be deployed — without an encoding such as PSS, the multiplicative structure of RSA makes signatures forgeable.

Worked example — build the key pair, then use it. Parameters: \(p = 61\), \(q = 53\), \(e = 17\), message \(m = 65\).

1

Modulus. \(n = 61 \times 53 = 3233\), so \(\mathrm{len}(n) = 12\) bits. Real deployments require \(\mathrm{len}(n) \ge 2048\).

2

Totient. \(\varphi(n) = 60 \times 52 = 3120\).

3

Validity of \(e\). Euclid: \(3120 = 17 \times 183 + 9\), \(17 = 9 + 8\), \(9 = 8 + 1\), so \(\gcd(17, 3120) = 1\) and the inverse exists.

4

Private exponent. Back-substituting gives \(1 = 2 \times 3120 - 367 \times 17\), so \(d \equiv -367 \equiv 2753 \pmod{3120}\). Check: \(17 \times 2753 \bmod 3120 = 1\). ✓

5

Encrypt with repeated squaring. \(65^{2} \equiv 992\), \(65^{4} \equiv 1232\), \(65^{8} \equiv 1547\), \(65^{16} \equiv 789 \pmod{3233}\), so \(c = 789 \times 65 = 51\,285 \equiv 2790 \pmod{3233}\). Only five multiplications were needed rather than sixteen.

6

Decrypt. \(2790^{2753} \bmod 3233 = 65\) by the same route. Anyone can reproduce step 5 from the public key; only the holder of \(d\) can run this step.

Diffie-Hellman: the shared secret equation

Two parties publish values derived from their own secrets. The algebra guarantees that both arrive at the same number, and the discrete logarithm problem is what stops everyone else.

The exchange

Public parameters: a large prime \(p\) and a generator \(g\). Alice chooses \(a\), Bob chooses \(b\), both uniformly at random from \(\{1, \dots, p-2\}\):

\[ A = g^{\,a} \bmod p, \qquad B = g^{\,b} \bmod p, \qquad K = B^{\,a} \bmod p = A^{\,b} \bmod p \]

Both sides agree because exponentiation composes:

\[ B^{\,a} = \left(g^{\,b}\right)^{a} = g^{\,ab} = \left(g^{\,a}\right)^{b} = A^{\,b} \pmod{p} \]

Anyone who can solve the discrete logarithm recovers \(a\) from \(A\) or \(b\) from \(B\), and therefore \(K\). That is the computational Diffie-Hellman assumption: \(g^{ab}\) is hard to compute from \(g^{a}\) and \(g^{b}\) alone.

Why the parameters matter

The hardness assumption only holds for good groups: a large prime-order subgroup, safe primes or approved elliptic curves, and validation of every received public value.

\[ \text{secure: } \mathrm{ord}(g) \text{ is a large prime} \qquad\qquad \text{insecure: } \mathrm{ord}(g) \text{ has small factors} \]

If the order has a small factor, an attacker can test candidate values of \(a\) in that small subgroup and learn \(a\) modulo that factor. Send an invalid public value, repeat, and the private exponent leaks. This is why the standards fix the groups (NIST SP 800-186 for curves) and require public-value validation.

Classical Diffie-Hellman

  • Modular arithmetic in \(\mathbb{Z}_p^{*}\)
  • Needs a large prime \(p\) and a generator of large prime order
  • Bigger values on the wire for the same security level
  • Vulnerable to small-subgroup attacks if parameters are weak

Elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman (ECDH)

  • Same structure with points on a curve instead of integers
  • A 256-bit curve gives roughly the security of a 3072-bit RSA modulus
  • Smaller keys and faster operations; this is what TLS deploys
  • Ephemeral use (ECDHE) gives forward secrecy for past sessions
Which property does the Diffie-Hellman exchange not provide by itself?
Assurance about who is on the other end of the exchange
Agreement between two honest parties on the same secret value
Secrecy of the derived key against a passive eavesdropper

Worked example — verify both sides reach the same secret. Parameters \(p = 23\), \(g = 5\), \(a = 6\), \(b = 15\).

1

Alice's public value. \(5^{2} = 25 \equiv 2\), \(5^{3} \equiv 2 \times 5 = 10\), so \(A = 5^{6} = (5^{3})^{2} \equiv 100 \equiv 100 - 4 \times 23 = 8 \pmod{23}\).

2

Bob's public value. \(5^{12} = (5^{6})^{2} \equiv 64 \equiv 18\), so \(B = 5^{15} = 5^{12} \cdot 5^{3} \equiv 18 \times 10 = 180 \equiv 180 - 7 \times 23 = 19 \pmod{23}\).

3

Alice computes. \(K = B^{a} = 19^{6} \pmod{23}\). Since \(19 \equiv 5^{15}\), this is \(5^{90}\). The order of 5 modulo 23 is 22, and \(90 = 4 \times 22 + 2\), so \(K \equiv 5^{2} = 25 \equiv 2 \pmod{23}\).

4

Bob computes. \(K = A^{b} = 8^{15} \pmod{23}\). Since \(8 \equiv 5^{6}\), this is \(5^{90}\) again, so \(K \equiv 2 \pmod{23}\).

5

Conclusion, and the caveat. Both sides hold \(K = 2\), which was never transmitted. Eve would have to solve \(5^{a} \equiv 8 \pmod{23}\) — 22 candidates here, infeasible for an approved group — and even then she would only have a shared secret with someone she has not authenticated.

The birthday bound

A collision attack does not need to hit a fixed digest; it needs any two inputs that agree. Comparing all pairs makes the expected work

\[ q \;\approx\; 1.177\,\sqrt{2^{n}} \;\approx\; 2^{\,n/2} \]

So an \(n\)-bit digest gives about \(n\) bits of pre-image resistance but only about \(n/2\) bits of collision resistance. That is why MD5 and SHA-1 fell first: their collision margins were the smallest, and collision resistance is the property signatures and certificates depend on.

HMAC is a nested keyed hash

From NIST FIPS 198-1, with \(K_0\) the key padded (or hashed then padded) to the hash block size \(B\), and ipad/opad the bytes 0x36 and 0x5c repeated \(B\) times:

\[ \mathrm{HMAC}(K, m) \;=\; H\!\left( (K_0 \oplus \mathrm{opad}) \,\|\, H\!\left( (K_0 \oplus \mathrm{ipad}) \,\|\, m \right) \right) \]

Two properties follow from the structure. The tag length equals the digest length (\(L\) bits), and the key is never fed to the hash directly, which is what a naive construction such as \(H(K \| m)\) gets wrong. Because the same construction works with any approved hash, HMAC-SHA-256 is the common modern default.

Entropy, and why a hashed password is not a key

For a key drawn uniformly from \(N\) possibilities, the entropy is

\[ H_{\text{key}} \;=\; \log_2 N \ \text{bits} \]

A 128-bit key drawn from a CSPRNG has 128 bits of entropy. A human-chosen password has far less, and hashing it does not add any: SHA-256 of a password with 30 bits of entropy still has 30 bits of guessability, even though the digest is 256 bits long. Password storage therefore uses a deliberately slow KDF with a salt (Argon2id, scrypt, bcrypt, PBKDF2), and keys come from a CSPRNG.

A system needs 128-bit security and uses SHA-256 in a digital signature. Is that consistent?
Yes: SHA-256 supplies about 128 bits of collision resistance, which matches a 128-bit target
No: a 256-bit digest only provides 256-bit security, so the target must be raised to 256 bits
No: SHA-256 cannot be used in signatures because it provides no confidentiality

Worked example — compute HMAC-SHA-256 by hand, at the level of the algorithm. Key \(K\) (say 20 bytes), message \(m\), hash \(H\) = SHA-256 with \(B = 64\) bytes and \(L = 32\) bytes.

1

Normalise the key. \(|K| = 20 < B = 64\), so \(K_0 = K \,\|\, 0^{44}\): append 44 zero bytes. If the key had been longer than 64 bytes, the rule would be \(K_0 = H(K) \,\|\, 0^{32}\) instead — hash first, then pad.

2

Inner block. \(K_0 \oplus \mathrm{ipad}\) where ipad is 0x36 repeated 64 times. XOR is byte-wise and self-inverse, so the verifier derives the identical block from the same key.

3

Inner hash. Append the message and hash: \(h_{\text{in}} = H\!\left((K_0 \oplus \mathrm{ipad}) \,\|\, m\right)\), a 32-byte value. Every byte of both the key and the message now influences it.

4

Outer block. \(K_0 \oplus \mathrm{opad}\) where opad is 0x5c repeated 64 times. Note that the message is not concatenated here — only the inner digest is.

5

Tag. \(T = H\!\left((K_0 \oplus \mathrm{opad}) \,\|\, h_{\text{in}}\right)\), 32 bytes for HMAC-SHA-256. Two hash computations over at least two blocks each, no matter how short the message.

6

Verify and bound the guarantee. The verifier recomputes \(T' = \mathrm{HMAC}(K, m')\) and compares. A modified \(m'\) yields a different \(T'\); a forged tag needs \(K\). But a valid pair recorded yesterday still verifies today, so freshness requires a nonce, timestamp or sequence number on top.

Signatures and trust binding, in notation

The signature equation tells you what a signature proves. The certificate structure tells you what it cannot prove on its own.

Hash-then-sign

A signature is computed over a digest, never over an arbitrarily long message directly:

\[ s = \mathrm{Sign}_{SK}(H(m)), \qquad \mathrm{Verify}_{PK}(m, s) = 1 \iff \mathrm{check}\!\left(PK,\, H(m),\, s\right) = 1 \]

Three reasons for hashing first: the signed object has a fixed size whatever the message length; the asymmetric operation is slow and is applied once to a short value; and the digest binds the signature to the exact bytes. Verification recomputes \(H(m)\), so changing a single byte of either \(m\) or \(s\) makes the check fail.

Why raw textbook RSA signatures are unsafe

Without an encoding, RSA signatures are multiplicatively malleable: if \(s_1\) verifies \(m_1\) and \(s_2\) verifies \(m_2\), then

\[ \left(s_1 s_2\right)^{e} \equiv m_1 m_2 \pmod{n} \]

so \(s_1 s_2 \bmod n\) is a valid signature on the product \(m_1 m_2\), which the attacker never had signed. Approved schemes prevent this by encoding the digest with a randomised, structured padding (RSA-PSS) or by using a different construction entirely. FIPS 186-5 approves RSA, ECDSA and EdDSA, and requires the hash strength to be at least that of the key.

The shape of ECDSA

With domain parameters \((q, G, n)\), private key \(d\), per-message secret \(k\), and \(e\) the leftmost bits of \(H(m)\) interpreted as an integer:

\[ R = [k]G, \qquad r = x_R \bmod n, \qquad s = k^{-1}\left(e + r\,d\right) \bmod n \]

The signature is the pair \((r, s)\). \(k\) must be unique per signature and secret: reuse it and the private key falls out of two signatures by simple algebra, which is exactly why the standard requires a fresh \(k\) at every invocation and allows deterministic generation to remove the temptation.

What a certificate adds

A certificate is a signed statement binding a public key to a subject, issued by a CA whose own key is trusted:

\[ \mathrm{Cert} = \left\langle\, \text{subject},\ PK,\ \text{issuer},\ [\text{notBefore}, \text{notAfter}],\ \text{extensions},\ \sigma_{CA} \,\right\rangle \] \[ \sigma_{CA} = \mathrm{Sign}_{SK_{CA}}\!\left(H(\text{certificate fields})\right) \]

Validation therefore checks more than one equation: the CA signature over the fields, a chain from the subject through any intermediates to a locally trusted root, the validity window, the name you asked for, key usage, and revocation status. A valid signature proves control of a private key; the chain is what turns that key into an identity.

Why does hash-then-sign not lose security compared with signing the whole message?
Because a hash hides the message, so the signer cannot be identified from it
Because a collision-resistant digest commits to the whole message in a fixed length, so one signature covers all of it
Because hashing makes the signature shorter than the message so it fits in one packet
