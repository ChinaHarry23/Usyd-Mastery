The Security Mindset

Week 1 opens with the unit's own framing: “With great power comes great responsibility.” Knowledge of how attackers operate is power, and the course treats the responsibility that comes with it as course content rather than as a disclaimer.

Why the course teaches the tools at all: the decks state the argument directly. We learn attacker tooling because we need to test the integrity of our own networks and applications, so that we can be assured we have made it as difficult as possible for others to do us harm. You cannot defend against a technique you refuse to understand.
Script kiddie (skid)

Someone who uses existing tools and scripts to penetrate a system, often without understanding how they work. The unit names this explicitly as the thing to move beyond: the marks in this course come from explaining what a tool did and why it worked, not from running it.

The CIA triad: the three properties you are protecting

Everything else in the unit is a means to one of three ends. When you write up a finding, name the property it breaks — that single sentence is usually the difference between a description and an impact statement.

Confidentiality

Information is readable only by those authorised to read it. Broken by disclosure: reading private data, exfiltrating a database, sniffing credentials off an unencrypted link.

Integrity

Information and systems are changed only in authorised ways. Broken by modification: altering a record, tampering with a log, substituting a file in transit.

Availability

Systems and data are usable when authorised users need them. Broken by denial: flooding a service, exhausting resources, encrypting files and withholding the key.

The triad is a question generator, not a definition to recite. Faced with an unfamiliar system, ask in order: who can read this, who can change it, and who can stop it working? Those three answers are a threat model in outline.
During an authorised test you find that a single request lets you read another customer's invoice. Which leg of the CIA triad have you demonstrated, and what is the correct next step?
Integrity — revert the change and document it
Confidentiality — stop, capture the minimum evidence, and report it
Availability — check whether the service is still up

Adversaries and Threat Models

The deck's instruction is precise: avoid generalising large groups of people and focus on particular threats. An adversary is not a personality type — it is anyone who uses network technologies to gain unauthorised access to a system.

Definition

Our adversaries are those who use network technologies to gain unauthorised access to a system. The word doing the work is unauthorised: the same action, performed inside a signed scope, is professional testing; performed without permission, it is an offence.

State-sponsored attackersIntelligence, persistence, strategic accessResourced, patient, and willing to develop their own toolingDetection and attribution over prevention alone
Hackers / hacktivistsPublicity, disruption, ideological outcomesMotivation is visible, but targeting is opportunisticAvailability, and the reputational cost of an outage

Assets and properties

Name the thing being protected and which leg of the triad it belongs to. “The exam results database” is an asset; “its integrity” is the property; “students changing their own marks” is the threat.

Boundaries and trust

Draw the line between what you control and what you do not. Every place data crosses that line — a login form, a file upload, an API call — is a place where input you did not write can arrive.

Exam framing: never answer “the threat is hackers”. The markable version names a specific adversary, the asset they want, the property they would break, and the control that stands in the way — and then says what evidence would tell you the control failed.
A hospital's patient-record system is reviewed after a clerk accidentally e-mails a spreadsheet to the wrong distribution list. Which row of the adversary table does this belong to, and what follows for the control you would recommend?
An inadvertent insider — so the control must limit what legitimate access can reach, not deny access
A state-sponsored attacker — so the control is network monitoring
A malicious insider — so the control is audit trails and dismissal

Consent, Transparency and Informing Impact

The Ethics, Rudeness, and Prison deck reduces the whole legal question to one line: the difference between legal and illegal hacking is permission. But permission alone is not enough — the deck pairs it with two further obligations.

The three obligations

Consent — written authorisation (a signed scope or rules of engagement) before any testing begins. Transparency — be upfront about the tools, techniques and methodology you plan to use, and disclose findings honestly. Informing impact — explain likely side effects before testing and report real-world consequences afterwards.

ObligationWhat it requiresWhat it rules out
ConsentA signed scope covering exactly which systems, IP addresses and time windows are in scopeVerbal permission; testing a host that merely resembles one on the list
TransparencyTelling the client which tools and methods you will use, and disclosing every findingHidden backdoors; undisclosed data collection; quietly dropping an embarrassing result
Informing impactWarning about service disruption and account lockouts beforehand; agreeing an emergency contact and a stop conditionDiscovering the outage together with the client's customers
The sentence worth memorising: permission without transparency isn't ethical testing — the client must understand what you are doing and why, before and after.

How to be polite once you are inside

The deck's “Rudeness” section is practical: most organisations watch server load and the volume and type of requests, and noisy tools are noticed. If response times start to climb, you are slamming their servers — and the client's likely responses are to shut you down, revoke the permissions they granted, decide you are in breach of their policy, or bring legal action.

Do

  • Ask permission — and keep the written record
  • Be respectful of the people who run the systems you are testing
  • Watch the signals — latency, error rates, lockouts
  • Report promptly if something you did had a real effect

Don't

  • Flood them with requests — the noise is what gets you caught and shut down
  • Read or copy private data — e-mail addresses, internal e-mail, credit cards
  • Modify or delete data — a proof of write access is already a finding
  • Keep going past the agreed stop condition
A client signs a scope and tells you verbally to “have a go at anything else you can reach while you are in there”. What is the correct action?
Test the extra systems — you have the client's verbal permission
Decline until the extra systems are added to the written scope, and record the exchange
Test them, but disclose everything you touched in the final report

Rudeness, Lawsuits and Prison

The third Week 1 deck is deliberately blunt about where this ends. The material is not there to frighten you out of the field — it is there because the legal system's reaction to hacking is a risk you have to model like any other.

Using penetration tools against any system on a public network, without prior permission, is unlawful. The deck extends this beyond attempts to penetrate a system: in the eyes of law enforcement it can reach reconnaissance activity as well. Looking is not automatically safe.

“It was easy” is not a defence

The deck is explicit that some methods of hacking seem innocuous — perhaps you were only looking at something you should not have, perhaps it was trivially easy to get in. Once it reaches court, none of that matters. The legal system does not understand the methods or the associated risks, but it does understand the hype.

Why sentences are harsh

High-profile cases have scared decision makers, and the gap in understanding produces disproportionate outcomes. Because the field is new and the tools are easy to use, less responsible individuals can cause considerable impact without understanding the consequences — so courts have made examples of defendants to deter others.

Consequences that outlast the sentence

Confiscated computer

Those arrested for computer crime usually have their computers taken away and inspected in great detail. Everything on them is in scope, not just the one system you touched.

Excluded from employment

Once convicted of computer crime — especially if it is classified as fraud — it becomes very difficult to get a job. That closes the career the skills were supposed to open.

Constantly watched

After authorities identify you as a cyber criminal they watch you closely for a long time. You remain under scrutiny long after the case is over.

Public and permanent

Penalties are frequently public, which compounds them: the reputational damage is often more lasting than the sentence itself.

The deck's own counter-argument, in its words: if you possess great IT skills it is very easy to make a comfortable living. You do not need to resort to hacking to earn money, and the extra cash is not worth watching your back for the rest of your life until you get caught. The comparative benefit is not worth the increased risk.

Worked answer — the incident review, planned backwards from the report. The Week 6 incident review asks you to identify how a real incident happened, what the impact was, and how to defend against it. Plan the answer with the Week 1 material and the structure writes itself.

1

Claim the incident, then reduce it to one sentence. “An attacker reached this asset by this route and broke this leg of the triad.” If you cannot fill all three blanks, you do not yet understand the incident well enough to write about it.

2

Name the adversary and the assumption that failed. Use the four rows from the adversaries table. Most incidents are not defeated by a brilliant attack; they are enabled by a control that was never there, or an insider whose legitimate access was wider than it needed to be.

3

State the impact in the triad's terms, then in the organisation's terms. Confidentiality, integrity or availability tells a security reader what happened; accounts, money or patients tells management why it mattered. Both sentences belong in the report.

4

Give the defence as a change, not a product. “Encrypt the link” and “reduce the export size a single account can perform” are changes. “Deploy a better firewall” is a purchase order, and it will not survive the question “how would that have stopped this?”

5

Add the disclosure sentence. Say what the organisation told the affected people and when. The Week 1 ethics material is a preview of the standard you are being held to as a professional: transparency, in both directions, even when the finding is embarrassing.

A student argues: “I only ran a port scan against the university's public web server from home. I did not exploit anything, so no offence occurred.” Which response matches the Week 1 material?
The scan is itself unauthorised activity — the deck says reconnaissance without permission can be treated as an offence
A scan is passive observation, so it falls outside the offence
It is lawful provided no damage was intended

Setting Up the Lab

Week 1's Basic Tools deck and Tutorial 1 build a small, deliberately isolated world. Everything you do in this unit happens between your own Kali and Ubuntu guests, on a network that has no route to anybody else's machine.

Read the layout as three decisions. sdb1 is optional unencrypted space, and it must be the first partition if Windows is ever to recognise the disk. sdb2 is the boot partition that allows the encrypted volume to be decrypted. sdb4 is the LUKS container, which LVM then divides into root, swap and home — and the deck is explicit that swap belongs inside it, not beside it.
You have installed Kali and Ubuntu as VirtualBox guests with the default NAT networking. Both can reach the internet, but neither can ping the other. What is the correct fix, and why is it the right one?
Switch both guests to bridged networking so they share your local network
Add a second NIC to each guest set to host-only adapter, and verify the addresses with ifconfig
Configure port forwarding rules on the NAT adapters

Choosing Your Tools: Linux, Kali, VirtualBox

The Basic Tools deck answers three questions in order: why Linux at all, why Kali specifically, and what Arch is for. The answers are about transparency and control rather than about which distribution is “best”.

DistributionWhat the deck saysChoose it when
Kali LinuxPreinstalled with many useful security utilities; based on Debian; the successor to Backtrack LinuxAlways, for the attacker workstation in this unit
UbuntuNot discussed in the deck beyond being the second guest the tutorial buildsFor the target machine, which should be ordinary rather than exotic

The line the deck quotes for Kali

“The quieter you become, the more able you are to hear.” It is worth reading as an instruction about method rather than as decoration: the point of the toolkit is to look and listen carefully, not to make the most noise.

The starting toolkit

Four tools carry the first tutorial. Read them as four different questions asked of the same machine, not as four programs to memorise.

nmap

A port-scanning tool. Basic usage is nmap <IP address>; the tutorial also has you try different scanning techniques such as nmap -sS and compare what each one puts on the wire.

Reachability and services

wireshark / tshark

A tool to capture network traffic. Capture while a ping or a scan runs in a parallel terminal, so that the packets you are explaining are the packets your own action produced.

Evidence, not guesswork

ssh

Remote login, enabled on the Ubuntu guest in Tutorial 1. Booting a VM gives you a console connection; SSH gives you a network session, which is what you actually need to test from Kali.

Remote access

scp

Copies files between servers securely. The tutorial has you move a file from Kali to the Ubuntu guest and verify it arrived — the simplest possible proof that the link works end to end.

Transfer
Tutorial 1's own deliverable is a short screencast showing the network setup and both addresses, a ping in each direction, an SSH login from Kali into Ubuntu, a file copied with scp, and an explanation of the difference between a TCP ACK scan and an Xmas scan with the capture on screen. The explanation is the marked part — the tool output is just the evidence.
An nmap run against the Ubuntu guest reports port 22 open. What is the most useful next step in the Week 1 lab?
Log in over SSH from Kali, because port 22 is the service the tutorial enabled for exactly that purpose
Immediately attempt to escalate privileges on the Ubuntu guest
Re-run the same scan with a different technique to confirm the result

The Testing Workflow

Week 1 gives you the shape of a penetration test before it gives you any of the techniques. The order matters more than the individual steps: every frame is defensible only because the frame before it happened.

What each frame leaves behind

  • Scope → a document you can point at later
  • Recon and scan → a list of targets and services
  • Enumeration → a shortlist of weaknesses worth proving
  • Access and escalation → captured evidence, and an impact
  • Report → the only artefact the client keeps

What a skipped frame costs you

  • Skip scope → the work is unlawful, however careful it was
  • Skip enumeration → you run tools without knowing what they proved
  • Skip evidence → nobody can reproduce or fix the finding
  • Skip the report → the client is no safer than before you started
Where this unit goes next: Weeks 2 and 3 move down the stack to give the scan frames their underlying mechanics — Ethernet, ARP and switching, then IP, TCP, NAT and firewalls. Weeks 4 and 5 do the same for web applications, and Weeks 6 and 8 for Linux privilege and the Android security model.
You have written permission for a /24 subnet and a four-hour window. At hour three you notice the scan results include a host whose address belongs to the client's payment provider. What do you do?
Continue — the address fell inside the range you were given
Exclude the host immediately, record what you saw, and raise it with the client before continuing
Finish the scan quietly and mention it in the report afterwards

Week 1 Recap

Four claims carry the whole week. If you can state each one in a sentence and give one example, you are ready for the tutorial and for the assessment that follows it.

Security is three properties

Confidentiality, integrity and availability. Every finding you write up names the one it breaks, and every control you recommend names the one it protects.

Core model

Permission is the whole legal question

The difference between legal and illegal hacking is permission — in writing, covering named systems, IPs and time windows. Verbal permission is not enough, and reconnaissance without it can be an offence on its own.

Ethics and law

Consent, transparency, impact

Permission without transparency is not ethical testing. Say what you will do, disclose what you found — even when it embarrasses the client — and warn about side effects before they happen.

Professional standard

The lab is deliberately small

Kali and Ubuntu on a host-only network, disk and swap encrypted. Two guests that can reach each other and nothing else — the safety of the whole semester depends on that isolation.
