Foundations: the vocabulary and the tests behind Week 1

Week 1 is conceptual rather than mathematical, so this page does the equivalent job. It pins down the definitions the three decks rely on, builds a taxonomy of adversaries, assets and controls, states the tests that decide whether an engagement is authorised, and finishes with the checklist that proves your lab is actually isolated before you point a tool at it.

Definitions that must be exact

Marks in this unit are lost on definitions more often than on ideas. Write each of these in one sentence, with the consequence included.

Adversary

Anyone who uses network technologies to gain unauthorised access to a system. The definition is deliberately not a list of people: it is defined by the absence of authorisation, which is why the same action can be testing or an offence depending only on the paperwork behind it.

CIA triad

Confidentiality — information is readable only by those authorised to read it. Integrity — information and systems are changed only in authorised ways. Availability — systems and data are usable by authorised users when they need them.

Consent

Written authorisation — a signed scope or rules of engagement — obtained before any testing begins, covering exactly which systems, IP addresses and time windows are in scope. Verbal permission is not enough.

Transparency

Being upfront about the tools, techniques and methodology you intend to use, and disclosing findings honestly even when they are embarrassing for the client. No hidden backdoors and no undisclosed data collection.

Informing impact

Explaining the likely side effects before testing — service disruption, account lockouts — agreeing an emergency contact and a stop condition, and reporting real-world consequences afterwards.

Script kiddie (skid)

Someone who uses existing tools and scripts to penetrate a system, often without understanding how they work. It is the state the unit is designed to move you out of: in this course you are marked on explaining what a tool did, not on running it.

Axis three — the control

Controls that act before

  • Authentication and least privilege
  • Network segmentation, such as the host-only adapter in your lab
  • Encryption at rest — including swap
  • Secure defaults, so the safe path is the easy path

Controls that act after

  • Audit trails, and knowing where they should be implemented
  • Monitoring of load and request volume
  • An emergency contact and an agreed stop condition
  • Disclosure: telling affected people what happened

Authorisation and consequence

Three relationships carry the legal half of Week 1. Learn them as tests you can apply to a scenario, not as statements to recite.

1. Authorisation is a conjunction, not a checkbox

An engagement is authorised when all of the following hold. Any one of them failing makes the activity unauthorised:

\[ P \;=\; \text{written} \;\wedge\; \text{prior} \;\wedge\; \text{specific} \;\wedge\; \text{current} \]

Written rules out the verbal go-ahead. Prior rules out permission granted after the fact. Specific rules out “anything you can reach”: the scope names systems, IP addresses and time windows. Current rules out a scope that expired, or a target that changed owner since it was signed.

2. An action is testing or an offence; the action does not decide which

Let \(x\) be the system acted on and \(S\) the signed scope. The same technique changes category depending only on membership:

\[ x \in S \;\Rightarrow\; \text{authorised testing} \qquad\qquad x \notin S \;\Rightarrow\; U \]

This is why the definition of an adversary mentions authorisation rather than intent, and why the deck says the difference between legal and illegal hacking is permission. It also explains why a host that merely appeared in your scan output is not a target: it was never in \(S\).

3. Legality and ethics are two different gates

Permission is necessary but not sufficient. The deck's standard is:

\[ \text{ethical test} \;=\; P \;\wedge\; T \;\wedge\; M \]

where \(T\) is transparency — disclosing method and findings, including the embarrassing ones — and \(M\) is informing impact — side effects explained beforehand, an emergency contact and a stop condition agreed. Permission without transparency is not ethical testing, even when it is perfectly legal.

4. Noise is what ends the engagement early

The client observes request volume and load. Once response times climb, the tool is no longer testing the target, it is degrading it — which turns a confidentiality or integrity exercise into an availability incident you caused:

\[ N \uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; A \downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{permissions revoked, legal action possible} \]

The mitigations are timing options that keep the request rate low, a scope that permits the technique, and a stop condition you act on the moment the agreed signal appears.

5. Consequences scale with publicity, not with cleverness

The deck's list of consequences — confiscated computer, exclusion from employment, ongoing scrutiny, harsh and public penalties — is not proportional to how difficult the attack was. It is proportional to how much of an example the court wishes to make. That is why “it was easy” and “I only looked” are not defences.

The professional reading: the same knowledge that makes an offence possible makes a comfortable living possible. The deck's own conclusion is that the comparative benefit of cyber-crime is not worth the increased risk — so the interesting question is never whether you can get in, but whether you were authorised to, and whether you can explain what you found.
A scope is signed on Monday for a two-week window covering a named subnet. On day nine the client's employee verbally asks you to also check the new staging server, which has a different address. Which of the four conditions of authorisation has failed?

Lab verification checklist

Isolation is the property that makes the whole unit lawful. Verify it in this order, and do not point a tool at anything until every step passes.

Reading the output

Three things are being checked here. The address 192.168.56.10 is inside the host-only range, so the guest is on the isolated segment. The ping replies with ttl=64 and sub-millisecond latency, so the peer is one hop away rather than somewhere across a network. The routing table shows a route for the host-only subnet and nothing that would carry that traffic off the segment.

Why this checklist is a legal control, not just an IT step: an isolated segment is what makes every later technique in the unit permissible. A guest that can reach the internet is a guest from which a mis-typed address reaches a third party, and the deck's position is unambiguous — using these tools against a system you do not have permission to test is unlawful, and reconnaissance counts.

Tutorial drill review

Tutorial 1 is submitted as a short screencast and is marked on your explanation. Use this list to check the recording before you upload it.

Show the addresses

Both guests, on camera, with ifconfig output visible and both addresses inside the host-only range. Narrate which interface is the host-only one.

Show reachability

A ping in each direction, and a capture running while it happens. Point at the request and the reply in the capture rather than at the terminal.

Show remote access

An SSH login from Kali into the Ubuntu guest, and one file moved with scp and verified on arrival. Say why SSH is a network session while the VM console is not.

Explain the scans

The assigned comparison is a TCP ACK scan against an Xmas scan, with packets captured on Kali. Explain what each probe sets, what a firewall sees, and why the two produce different answers from the same host.

What the tutorial says to install and enable

  • VirtualBox on your own device, or a tested alternative on Apple silicon
  • The Kali 64-bit Live Boot image — not the prebuilt VirtualBox image
  • Ubuntu Desktop, or Ubuntu Server if host disk space is tight
  • An SSH server on the Ubuntu guest, verified as running
  • Password login enabled for SSH, and the Ubuntu firewall adjusted to allow it

What the tutorial says to demonstrate

  • The network setup, with both guests' addresses on screen
  • That the two guests can ping each other
  • That Kali can log in to Ubuntu over SSH
  • That a file copies from Kali to Ubuntu with scp
  • The ACK versus Xmas difference, evidenced by a capture

Model answer — classify a scenario against the Week 1 tests. Scenario: a contractor runs a port scan against a client's web server from their own laptop, at 2 a.m., because the client's IT manager said on a phone call that “it's fine, go for it”. No written scope exists.

1

Apply the authorisation conjunction. Written fails — the permission was verbal, and the deck is explicit that verbal permission is not enough. Prior holds, because the call happened before the scan. Specific fails — there is no scope naming systems, addresses or a time window. Current cannot be assessed, because there was never a document to expire.

2

Classify the action. With \(x \notin S\) and no valid \(P\), the scan falls on the offence side of the membership test. It does not matter that scanning is trivial, that nothing was exploited, or that the request came from inside the client. The deck extends this to reconnaissance activity specifically.

3

Name the property at risk. None has yet been broken, and that is the point worth making in an exam: an offence does not require a broken property. Availability becomes the live risk the moment the scan is aggressive enough to slow the service, which is precisely the “don't be rude” failure the deck describes.

4

State the fix, as a change. Replace the phone call with a signed scope naming the hosts, the addresses, the permitted techniques and the window; add an emergency contact and a stop condition; and confirm the client still owns the target. Then the identical scan becomes authorised testing — the technique never changed, only the paperwork did.

Model answer — the ethics question, in the three obligations. Scenario: during an authorised test you find that a single unauthenticated request returns a colleague's full personnel record. You can get more records; the client asked you to be thorough.

1

Consent is satisfied but bounded. You are inside a signed scope, so the testing itself is authorised. Consent does not extend to reading data you do not need in order to prove the finding: the deck's rule is do not read or copy private data.

2

Take the minimum evidence. One request, one response, and a screenshot that establishes the class of data without reproducing an identifiable record. That is enough to prove \(E\) for the finding while keeping the confidentiality breach as small as the proof allows. Then stop.

3

Inform impact, then report. Tell the client immediately rather than at the end of the engagement: this is live exposure of personnel data, so the side effect is not hypothetical and the stop condition may need to be invoked. Reporting early is a transparency obligation, not an admission of failure.

4

“Be thorough” is not an instruction to keep going. Thoroughness in this unit means explaining the finding precisely — the request, the authorisation check that is missing, the affected records, and the fix — not demonstrating how many records you could have read. The markable report names the property, the exposure and the remediation; it does not quote the data.
