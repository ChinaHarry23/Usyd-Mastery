Foundations: the arithmetic behind Layers 1 and 2

Week 2 is mostly vocabulary and structure, and the numbers that go with it are small — which is exactly why they are worth being exact about. This page collects the frame-size rules, the Layer 1 capacity and delay formulas, the address arithmetic, and the checklist for reading a capture without guessing.

Frame arithmetic: sizes, padding and overhead

One frame, three constants and one rule. Get these right and every capture question becomes arithmetic.

Units used on this page

A bit is the Layer 1 unit and a byte is eight of them. Layer 2 moves frames, Layer 3 moves packets, Layer 4 moves segments (TCP) or datagrams (UDP), and everything above is simply data. The deck's own split is: Layer 1 deals with bits, Layer 2 with frames, Layer 3 with packets, Layer 4 with how packets are sent between endpoints.

1. Frame size is a sum

With \(H = 14\) bytes of Ethernet header (6 + 6 + 2), \(P\) bytes of payload and \(T = 4\) bytes of frame check sequence:

\[ F \;=\; H + P + T \;=\; 18 + P \ \text{bytes} \]

The payload is bounded on both sides:

\[ 46 \;\le\; P \;\le\; 1500 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 64 \;\le\; F \;\le\; 1518 \ \text{bytes} \]

An 802.1Q tag inserts a further 4 bytes, so a tagged frame is \(F' = 22 + P\) and reaches 1522 bytes at the maximum payload.

2. Padding is the floor, not an error

If the payload is shorter than the minimum, it is padded with zeros:

\[ P_{\text{on wire}} \;=\; \max(P,\ 46) \]

The rule exists because collision detection needs the frame to still be transmitting while the signal reaches the far end of the segment and returns. A frame that ended too early could not be distinguished from a collision.

3. Efficiency is payload over frame

Payload efficiency, and the goodput that follows from it at line rate \(R\):

\[ \eta \;=\; \frac{P}{F} \qquad\qquad G \;=\; \eta \cdot R \]

A full 1500-byte payload gives \(\eta = 1500/1518 \approx 98.8\%\). A 28-byte ARP message in a padded 64-byte frame gives \(\eta = 28/64 \approx 43.8\%\). On the wire you must add the 8-byte preamble and the 12-byte inter-frame gap, so the ARP frame really occupies 84 byte-times and \(\eta_{\text{wire}} \approx 33\%\).

A frame carries a 1200-byte payload. What is its total size on the wire, excluding the preamble?
1200 bytes, because the payload is below the MTU

Layer 1: capacity, delay and the limits of a medium

Layer 1 encodes and modulates bits onto a medium. How many bits per second fit, and how long they take to arrive, are the two questions worth being able to answer numerically.

1. Nyquist — the limit with no noise

For a channel of bandwidth \(B\) hertz using \(M\) distinguishable signal levels per symbol:

\[ C \;=\; 2B \log_2 M \ \text{bits per second} \]

The \(2B\) is the sampling bound: a signal band-limited to \(B\) can be reconstructed from \(2B\) samples per second. Each symbol then carries \(\log_2 M\) bits.

2. Shannon — the limit with noise

Real media have noise, so the achievable rate is capped by the signal-to-noise ratio \(S/N\):

\[ C \;=\; B \log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathrm{SNR_{dB}} \;=\; 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{S}{N}\right) \]

No modulation scheme escapes Shannon: past this rate, error-free delivery is impossible regardless of how clever the encoding is. It is the reason more bandwidth and better SNR, not more levels, are what raise a wireless link's ceiling.

3. Delay is distance over speed

A signal travels at a fraction of the speed of light in vacuum, \(c \approx 3 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}\):

\[ t_p \;=\; \frac{d}{v} \;=\; \frac{d}{\kappa\,c} \qquad (0.6 \lesssim \kappa \lesssim 0.8 \ \text{for copper and fibre}) \]

Two consequences worth carrying into the exam: latency on a long link is dominated by distance and is not fixed by buying a faster interface, and bandwidth is a rate while latency is a time — they are not the same resource.

Worked example — the ceiling on a wireless link. A 20 MHz Wi-Fi channel is measured at an SNR of 30 dB. What is the Shannon limit, and how does it compare with a copper link?

1

Convert the ratio. \(\mathrm{SNR_{dB}} = 30\) means \(10\log_{10}(S/N) = 30\), so \(S/N = 10^{3} = 1000\).

2

Apply Shannon. \(C = 20\times10^{6} \times \log_2(1 + 1000) = 20\times10^{6} \times 9.97 \approx 1.99\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{bit/s}\).

3

State the answer with units. Roughly 199 Mbit/s — the best any modulation scheme could achieve on that channel at that SNR, before protocol overhead.

4

Compare with a clean cable. On Cat6 the SNR is thousands rather than a thousand, and interference is far lower, which is why \(\log_2(1 + S/N)\) is not the binding constraint indoors. Distance and the 100 m channel limit are.

5

Sanity-check against Nyquist. If the same 20 MHz were noiseless with 16-level symbols: \(C = 2 \times 20\times10^{6} \times \log_2 16 = 160\ \mathrm{Mbit/s}\). Shannon's noisy bound is the realistic one; Nyquist's is the idealised floor case, not a target.

A link's SNR falls from 30 dB to 20 dB. What happens to the Shannon capacity, all else equal?
It falls, because log2(1 + S/N) shrinks as the ratio drops — from about 9.97 to about 6.66 bits per symbol
It falls by exactly 10 Mbit/s, because 10 dB of SNR is lost
It is unchanged, because the interface still negotiates the same link speed

Address arithmetic

Two addresses matter this week: the 48-bit hardware address a switch uses inside the LAN, and the 32-bit address ARP is asked to resolve into it.

1. A MAC address is two halves of 24 bits

\[ \underbrace{b_{47}\ldots b_{24}}_{\text{OUI: 24 bits, the vendor}}\;\underbrace{b_{23}\ldots b_{0}}_{\text{NIC-specific: 24 bits}} \]

The space is \(2^{48} \approx 2.81\times10^{14}\) addresses, and the vendor space is \(2^{24} = 16{,}777{,}216\) blocks. Two bits of the first octet carry meaning rather than identity: the I/G bit (0 unicast, 1 multicast or broadcast) and the U/L bit (0 factory, 1 locally administered). That second bit is what makes a randomised or spoofed address syntactically valid.

2. Layer 2 has no hierarchy, so it has no route

A MAC address encodes no network, which is why it can only be used inside one broadcast domain. The broadcast domain is the unit that matters for scaling and for exposure:

\[ \#\text{stations reachable by one broadcast frame} \;=\; \#\text{ports in the same VLAN} \]

Splitting a domain costs a Layer 3 hop; the return is a smaller blast radius. That trade is the whole argument for VLANs and subnets.

3. The addresses above, for context

IPv4 is a 32-bit address and IPv6 is 128 bits — the deck's two types. For an IPv4 subnet with prefix length \(p\), the usable host addresses are:

\[ N_{\text{hosts}} \;=\; 2^{\,32-p} - 2 \]

The subtraction removes the network address (the first address, which names the subnet) and the broadcast address (the last, which means every host on it). A /24 therefore holds 256 addresses and 254 usable hosts. Ports are 16 bits: values 1–65535, with the destination port normally a low well-known number and the source port a high ephemeral one.

Worked example — reading addresses out of a capture. A frame shows source MAC 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E and, inside it, an IPv4 packet from 192.168.10.105 to 8.8.8.8. What can and cannot be concluded?

1

Split the MAC. 00:1A:2B is the OUI — a vendor block. 3C:4D:5E is the interface's own serial within that block. First octet 0x00 = binary 00000000, so both the I/G bit and the U/L bit are 0: a unicast, factory-assigned address.

2

Locate the source. 192.168.10.105 falls inside 192.168.0.0/16, a private range, so it is not routable on the public Internet. The host is behind a translating device.

3

Recognise the destination. 8.8.8.8 is public. Inside the LAN the frame's destination MAC will be the gateway's, not the server's: a host sends off-net traffic to its router at Layer 2 and lets Layer 3 carry it the rest of the way.

4

Say what is not proven. Nothing in the frame authenticates the source MAC, and nothing proves the IP address belongs to that interface. Both are claims; only a control such as port security or 802.1X gives them any weight.

5

State the exam sentence. “The OUI identifies a vendor block, so the hardware class can be guessed; the private source address shows the host is behind NAT; and the frame's Layer 2 destination is the default gateway because the Layer 3 destination is off-subnet.”

Why does 192.168.1.150/24 give 254 usable host addresses rather than 256?
Because the subnet mask itself consumes two addresses
The network address and the broadcast address are both reserved, leaving 2^8 − 2 = 254
Because one address is always reserved for the router's gateway interface

Checklist: reading a capture without guessing

A capture answers Layer 2 questions in a fixed order. Work down the stack and the diagnosis writes itself — bottom-up is faster than scrolling for keywords.

StepQuestionMarks are lost when…
1. Vantage pointWas this captured on a mirror port, a TAP, or the host itself?You claim to see the whole network from a single host's interface
2. FiltersCapture filter or display filter — and is anything missing as a result?You treat a filtered capture as a complete record
3. Layer 2 addressesWho sent it, who is it for, is either value surprising?You assume the source MAC is authentic
4. Broadcast or unicastShould this frame have reached everyone in the VLAN?You call normal ARP flooding an anomaly
5. EtherTypeDoes the declared type match the dissected contents?You skip the field that selects the dissector
6. Higher layersWhat do the IP addresses and ports add?You jump to payload before establishing who is talking

Worked example — a capture that looks wrong. In a 60-second capture on a SPAN port you see 4,812 ARP frames. The gateway address 10.0.0.1 appears in 3,206 of them, sourced from three different MAC addresses. Walk the checklist.

1

Vantage point. A SPAN port on the VLAN's switch sees every flooded frame, which is exactly why ARP volume here is expected rather than suspicious. Establish the denominator before calling anything anomalous.

2

Filter and count. Split the 4,812 frames by arp.opcode. Requests are broadcasts that every station sees; replies are unicast and should be roughly one per request. A flood of replies nobody asked for is the signal, not the volume of requests.

3

Layer 2 addresses. Three MAC addresses claiming 10.0.0.1 cannot all be the gateway. Check whether one of them also appears as the source of normal traffic — an attacker relaying traffic usually keeps the legitimate gateway's address while adding its own.

4

Timing against the switch. Correlate the first spoofed reply with the switch's MAC table. If one port suddenly sources an address it never sourced before, the port is the location and the timing is the evidence.

5

State the finding in one sentence. “Between 10:02:14 and 10:02:41 the gateway IP 10.0.0.1 was announced by three MAC addresses, none of which is the gateway's own, in a pattern of unsolicited replies consistent with ARP cache poisoning on VLAN 10. Impact: on-path visibility and modification for every host that accepted the mapping. Control: dynamic ARP inspection with DHCP snooping, and port security limiting learned addresses.”

6

Check the arithmetic before you publish. 3,206 frames in 27 seconds is about 119 per second — far above a normal re-ARP rate, which is what makes repetition the strongest single indicator in the whole capture.

A capture contains ARP requests but no replies at all. Which conclusion is best supported?
ARP replies must be blocked by dynamic ARP inspection
The capture point sees broadcasts but not the unicast replies, so the replies were probably never in view
The targets' ARP caches had expired, so they never replied

Tutorial arithmetic: what a tunnel can carry

Tutorial 2 turns the layer model into a decision. Before you configure anything, ask which layer the traffic you need actually lives at.

The rule behind the tutorial's warning

A forward or a proxy carries a transport conversation. An SSH local forward carries TCP, because SSH opens a TCP connection on the far side; nothing carries ICMP, because ICMP is neither TCP nor UDP and has no port to forward. A VPN instead puts the client into the remote network at Layer 3:

\[ \text{SSH } -L \;:\; \{\text{TCP}\} \qquad\qquad \text{VPN} \;:\; \{\text{IP}\} \supset \{\text{TCP, UDP, ICMP, }\ldots\} \]

Choose the narrow tool when you know the port (a single web service), and the broad one when you need the whole network — several ports, several hosts, or protocols that are not TCP at all, which is exactly why the tutorial moves from ssh -L to OpenVPN before running nmap.
